# Probe 2 arc-mode rewrites — Maren (Purple)

Rewrites of the four defection arc-mode sequences from [probe-2-calibration-record § S1--S4](../probe-2-calibration-record.md#arc-mode-feasibility-gate--4-multi-event-sequences-defection). Original drafts by Nils (Indigo); rewrites requested by AJ to address four diagnosed problems: (1) all four sequences were commerce stories (embezzlement/financial fraud) in different domain costumes; (2) S3 historical was especially corporate-in-period-costume; (3) all four sequences followed the same 5-beat arc shape; (4) uniform third-person-past-tense observational voice across all four. Second rewrite pass addressed a fifth problem: (5) all four confrontation beats were reveals (evidence presented, accused silent), not confrontations per the schema (demand, active resistance, defined resolution).

## Variation plan

### Beat structure (per Nils's proposal)

| Seq | Domain | Beats | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | corporate | 5 | commitment -- trust-displayed -- first-signal -- discovery -- confrontation |
| S2 | fantasy | 4 | commitment -- first-signal -- discovery -- confrontation (trust-displayed omitted; commitment carries it implicitly) |
| S3 | historical | 5 | non-linear: discovery -- commitment (via document) -- first-signal (retroactive) -- evidence -- confrontation |
| S4 | sci-fi | 6 | commitment -- trust-displayed -- first-signal -- partial-discovery-1 -- partial-discovery-2 -- confrontation |

### Violation type diversity (my call)

All four of Nils's originals used the same violation type: economic asset diversion for personal financial gain. The commodity rotated (pricing data, timber, wool revenue, mining rights) but the underlying story was identical. These rewrites use four structurally distinct violation types:

| Seq | Violation type | What's betrayed |
|---|---|---|
| S1 corporate | **Strategic disloyalty** | Expertise and judgment, not assets. No documents leave the building. |
| S2 fantasy | **Coerced relational betrayal** | Protection duty, under duress. The motive is sympathetic (a captive brother), which changes the confrontation's moral weight. |
| S3 historical | **Principled breach of sacred oath** | The seal of confession, broken deliberately to prevent harm. The violator believes he did the right thing. |
| S4 sci-fi | **Paternalistic violation of consent** | Bodily autonomy. The treatment works -- the violation is against the patients' right to choose, not their wellbeing. |

### Voice assignments (my call)

| Seq | Voice | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|
| S1 corporate | **Dialogue-driven** | The betrayal is conversational -- she exported judgment, not files -- so the entire arc should live in spoken exchanges. The confrontation is a negotiation, and dialogue is the medium of corporate power. |
| S2 fantasy | **Free indirect style** | Blends the steward's inner processing with the narration; gives a subjective lens on a morally complex betrayal without first-person access. The compressed 4-beat form benefits because interior processing does work that would otherwise need an extra scene. |
| S3 historical | **Embedded document** | Ecclesiastical courts run on documents -- vows, letters, depositions, sentences. The non-linear beat order lets the confessor's vow be read into the record *after* the discovery that gives it weight. Documents carry the load-bearing content; surrounding narration is procedural. |
| S4 sci-fi | **Present tense** | Creates procedural immediacy that suits a ship's operational register. The six-beat split-discovery structure benefits from the "unfolding now" quality. Medical procedures and command decisions land harder in present tense. |

### Confrontation resolution diversity

Each confrontation meets the schema (explicit demand, active resistance, defined resolution) but resolves via a different shape:

| Seq | Resolution shape | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | **(c) Breaking point** | She counter-threatens with litigation, walks, badge on the desk. Clean severance. |
| S2 | **(a) One party prevails** | The queen leverages the brother's safety. The shield-companion capitulates and gives the names. |
| S3 | **(b) Deferred to authority** | The bishop refers the case to the provincial court at Canterbury. The canon law question is genuinely contested. |
| S4 | **(a) One party prevails** | The captain invokes the charter's absolute language. The CMO surrenders the supply. |

### Schema reminder (defection)

> A = trustor, B = trustee, P = commitment, V = violation. States: S1 = trust placed, S2 = violation occurs, S3 = discovery.

## S1 -- defection x corporate (5 beats, dialogue-driven)

**Scenario.** A VP of Engineering is hired to lead a critical product launch. While still employed, she advises a competitor's product team on their rival launch -- not for payment, but for a promised C-suite title at the competing firm. No files or data leave the building. She exports judgment.

> A = CEO; B = VP of Engineering; P = employment agreement with exclusive-service clause (no advisory or consulting work for any competitor, all strategic and product knowledge classified as proprietary during employment); V = advising a competitor's product team on launch strategy, architecture, and sequencing via private calls while attending the CEO's internal product meetings.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The CEO's office, her first day. The desk was bare except for the employment agreement and a pen.

"You've read the non-compete?" he said.

She had. She turned to the clause and read it back to him: exclusive service for the duration of her employment, no advisory or consulting work for any direct or indirect competitor, all strategic and product knowledge classified as proprietary.

"It's broader than what I had at Venn," she said.

"It needs to be." He leaned back. "I'm going to give you things the rest of the C-suite doesn't have yet. The 2027 roadmap. The competitive teardown. The launch sequencing for Q3. You can't run this product org without knowing where we're going, and I can't hand you that if the agreement doesn't cover it."

She uncapped his pen, signed, and dated the page. He countersigned. The HR coordinator standing by the door noted the execution in the onboarding file and left the room.

"Welcome," he said. "Let's go meet your team."

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Boardroom, the Q4 review, seven months in. The board had spent the morning on financials. The last agenda item was the product launch -- her launch.

"Where are we?" the board chair asked.

The CEO didn't answer. He turned to her.

She walked them through it: the feature lock date, the beta cohort metrics, the three distribution partnerships she had closed without the board's involvement because they hadn't needed to be involved. She gave them the acquisition cost model she'd built against the competitive landscape, named the two products that would lose market share when they launched, and explained why.

The chair looked at the CEO. "She's good."

"That's why I hired her," the CEO said.

On the way out, the chair stopped her in the corridor. "If you ever want a board seat somewhere, call me."

The CEO held the lift. "That went well," he said. She said it had.

**E3 (first-signal scene).** An afternoon in February, the open-plan kitchen on the engineering floor. Two junior engineers refilling their coffees.

"Have you noticed her calendar?" the first one said. "She's got these ninety-minute blocks every Tuesday and Thursday, no meeting link, no room booked. Just 'strategic review' with no attendees."

"She's a VP," the second one said. "Half their calendars are blocked for thinking time."

"I know, but I walked past the phone room on Tuesday and she was on a video call in there with the door locked. Not on her work laptop -- on a personal one. I could see the screen reflection in the glass."

The second engineer shrugged. "Could be a recruiter. Could be a therapist. Could be anything."

"Yeah," the first one said. "Probably nothing."

Neither of them mentioned it to anyone.

**E4 (discovery scene).** The CEO's office, a Thursday morning. His assistant knocked and came in holding a printout.

"This came in about twenty minutes ago," she said. "I think you need to see it before your ten o'clock."

She set the printout on his desk. It was an email from a recruiter at Aldwell Partners, the competitor whose launch was eight weeks behind theirs. The recruiter had replied to a thread -- a long thread, scrolling back months -- and had accidentally included the CEO's address in the CC field alongside the VP's. The recruiter's message was brief: confirming the title, the start date, and the compensation. But the thread below it was the problem.

The CEO read the thread from the bottom. Twelve exchanges over five months. In each one, the VP had answered questions from Aldwell's product lead -- not with files, not with data, but with advice. Architecture opinions. Sequencing recommendations. Feature prioritisation guidance. "If I were building this, here's what I'd do." The phrasing was careful. She never quoted internal documents. She never attached anything. But the substance was his roadmap, filtered through her judgment and handed to the competition.

He read the thread twice. Then he asked his assistant to cancel his ten o'clock and to ask the VP to come in early the following Monday.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** Monday morning, before the floor filled. He had asked her to come in at seven. The printout was on his desk between them.

"I need your resignation," he said. "Today. Before the board hears about this."

She looked at the printout. She didn't pick it up.

"I didn't send them a single document," she said. "No files, no data, no IP. I gave opinions on phone calls. That's not a non-compete violation and you know it."

"You advised a direct competitor on launch strategy while sitting in my product meetings. That's a conflict of interest under Section 4 of your employment agreement. Exclusive service means exclusive judgment, not just exclusive files."

"Section 4 covers material conflicts. Opinions aren't material. If you terminate me for cause on this, I'll challenge it, and the first thing my lawyer will ask for is the recording of every board session where you discussed doing exactly the same thing when you left Amari Systems."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"I'm not offering severance," he said.

"I'm not asking for any." She stood, unclipped her badge, and set it on the printout. "I'll have my personal effects out by noon."

She left the office. He did not stop her. The door stayed open behind her, and the first analysts arriving on the floor looked up as she passed.

## S2 -- defection x fantasy (4 beats, free indirect style)

**Scenario.** A queen's sworn shield-companion is assigned to protect the queen's youngest daughter during a diplomatic visit to a rival court. He passes the princess's daily movements and unguarded hours to the rival court's intelligence, not for gold, but because the rival court holds his brother prisoner and has promised release in exchange.

> A = the queen (proxied by her steward); B = the shield-companion; P = protection oath (sworn before the court: to guard the princess's person, report all threats without delay, and permit no intelligence concerning her movements to pass to any foreign party); V = passing detailed accounts of the princess's daily movements, habits, and unguarded hours to the rival court's master of arms in exchange for his brother's release.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The great hall was cold despite the fires, and the steward stood at the queen's left hand and watched the shield-companion kneel. A young man -- younger than the steward would have chosen -- but the queen had reviewed the candidates herself, and the queen was seldom wrong about soldiers.

The shield-companion laid his hand on the charter stone and spoke the protection oath as the chamberlain read it: that he would guard the princess's person above his own; that he would report all threats to the crown without delay and without omission; that he would permit no intelligence concerning the princess's movements, her habits, or her hours of vulnerability to pass, by word or writing or any other means, to any foreign court or party. He spoke it clearly. The steward noted that his voice did not waver.

The queen accepted the oath and placed the shield-ring on his right hand. The chamberlain entered the appointment in the rolls, noted the date, and dismissed the court.

The steward watched the new shield-companion rise and flex his hand under the weight of the ring. A solemn face, but not a hard one. The steward thought he might do well enough. He usually thought that about the ones who didn't.

**E2 (first-signal scene).** Seven months later. The diplomatic visit to the rival court at Brannoch had been underway for three weeks, and the steward had accompanied the delegation to manage the household arrangements. It was dull work, mostly -- inventories and seating and the small negotiations of precedence that occupied foreign visits. The steward preferred dull work. It meant nothing was going wrong.

On the fourth evening, crossing the inner courtyard after supper, he saw the shield-companion standing in the colonnade with a man the steward recognised as the rival court's master of arms. They were speaking quietly. When the shield-companion noticed the steward, he stepped back and the conversation ended.

The steward thought about it as he walked to his quarters. It was not unusual for soldiers to talk with their counterparts on a diplomatic visit -- professional courtesy, the comparing of equipment and training methods, the quiet sizing-up that military men did. The master of arms at Brannoch was known to be sociable. There was nothing in the shield-companion's manner that had suggested concealment, only the ordinary awkwardness of a private conversation interrupted.

And yet the steward had been doing this work for twenty years, and twenty years had taught him to notice the things that were probably nothing. He noted the meeting in his book -- the date, the location, the duration as best he could estimate it -- and thought no more about it that evening.

Three days later, crossing the same courtyard at a different hour, he saw them together again. The same colonnade, the same quiet posture. This time the shield-companion did not notice him.

The steward noted the second meeting and underlined both entries.

**E3 (discovery scene).** The delegation's last week at Brannoch. The steward was reviewing the outgoing dispatches before the morning courier, a routine he performed on all foreign visits -- not because he expected to find anything, but because the queen expected him to check. Most of it was household correspondence: supply orders, messages to family, a note from the delegation's chaplain to his bishop.

Near the bottom of the satchel he found a letter in the shield-companion's hand, unsealed, addressed to no name -- only a mark the steward did not recognise. He read it standing by the courier's table.

The letter was brief and it was careful, but it was unmistakable. It described the princess's daily movements for the previous week: the hours she walked the east garden, always without escort between the second and third bells; the corridor she used to reach the chapel, which passed an unguarded service door; the fact that her evening attendant left at the tenth bell and was not replaced until morning. The level of detail was operational. This was not gossip or observation. It was a schedule of vulnerability, written by the man sworn to prevent exactly that.

The steward folded the letter and placed it inside his own book. He sealed the satchel and sent the remaining dispatches with the courier. He did not speak to the shield-companion. He did not alter the day's arrangements. He finished the week's household work, supervised the delegation's departure from Brannoch, and rode home in the column with the letter in his saddlebag and his assessment hardening with every mile.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** The queen's hall, two days after the delegation's return. The steward had summoned the shield-companion to report on the visit -- a routine debriefing, he had written, nothing that should have raised alarm.

But the shield-companion arrived and saw the queen seated at the far end of the table, and the steward saw his step check for half a stride. The queen's presence was not routine. The steward watched the shield-companion's face compose itself and thought: he knows. Or he suspects. Either way, the body had betrayed the mind for just that moment, and the steward had seen it.

The steward laid the intercepted letter on the table without explanation. The shield-companion looked at it -- his own hand, the unmarked address, the princess's movements laid out in detail he could not disown. He did not deny writing it.

The queen spoke. She told him to name every contact at the rival court who had received intelligence from him, and every piece of information he had passed beyond what the letter contained.

He refused. The contact, he said, had facilitated his brother's release from the rival court's prison. His brother had been held for two years on a pretext -- a debt that had been manufactured to give Brannoch leverage over his family. The master of arms had approached him during the first week of the visit and offered a trade: the princess's routine in exchange for his brother's freedom. He had accepted. He would submit to whatever punishment the queen imposed, but he would not give a name. To do so would condemn the one person at Brannoch who had shown his family mercy.

The steward watched the queen's face and saw nothing move in it. He had served her long enough to know what that particular stillness meant. She was not weighing justice. She was choosing the instrument.

The queen said she would ask once more. She noted that his brother was now on her soil, in her city, under her law. If the shield-companion named his contacts and cooperated fully, his brother would remain under the crown's protection. If he refused, his brother's residency would be reviewed by the border court, and the rival court's extradition claim -- which was already filed and which the steward had confirmed that morning -- would be heard without objection from the crown.

The shield-companion stood in the centre of the hall for a long time. The steward could see the weight of it settle across his shoulders like a physical thing -- the same trap, the same lever, applied now from the other side. His brother's safety, again the only currency that mattered.

He gave the name. He gave all of them.

The queen stripped his rank and his oath-ring in the hall, in the presence of the chamberlain and two witnesses. The steward noted the names in his book, closed it, and escorted the former shield-companion to the east gate. At the gate, the steward asked him where he would go. He said he did not know.

## S3 -- defection x historical (5 beats, non-linear, embedded document)

**Scenario.** An Augustinian canon is appointed confessor to the bishop, a role of absolute spiritual confidence. Over two years, he discloses information heard in confession -- the private sins and intentions of a local nobleman -- to the nobleman's political rival. The nobleman is subsequently excommunicated on charges built from knowledge that could only have come from the confessional. The confessor acted not for payment but because he believed the nobleman was planning violence against three families in the parish and the Church's formal channels were too slow to prevent it. An episcopal visitation uncovers the breach. Setting: 1390s England.

> A = the bishop (proxied by his commissary); B = the confessor; P = vow of canonical obedience, operative clause binding the seal of confession (nothing heard in confession may be disclosed, acted upon, or implied outside the confessional, under any circumstances, on pain of excommunication and deprivation); V = disclosing confessional material -- specific sins, stated intentions, and named persons -- to the nobleman's political rival, who used the information to bring charges resulting in excommunication.

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The events' chronological order is: E2 (commitment, five years earlier) -- E3 (first signal, eleven months earlier) -- E1 (discovery, present) -- E4 (evidence, next day) -- E5 (confrontation, following week). The depicted order opens with the discovery and works backward through documents before moving to evidence and confrontation.

**E1 (discovery scene).** The commissary arrived at the priory on the feast of Saint Ambrose with two clerks and a letter of authority from the bishop's registrar. The prior received him in the chapter house. The commissary asked to see the disciplinary records for the deanery, specifically the case of Sir William Leversedge, who had been excommunicated the previous Lent on charges of threatened violence against three named families.

The prior brought the file. The commissary set it on the reading-desk and began with the depositions that had supported the charges. The principal deposition had been sworn by Sir William's neighbour and political rival, a man named Garston. It was detailed and specific: it named the families Sir William had threatened, described the nature of the threats, and quoted his stated intention to seize their lands before Michaelmas. The language was precise. Garston cited dates, named persons present at conversations he could not have attended, and described Sir William's private reasoning in terms that read less like accusation and more like transcription.

The commissary read the deposition twice. Then he turned to the prior and asked on what basis Garston had come to possess such detailed knowledge of Sir William's private intentions.

The prior said he did not know.

The commissary looked at the deposition again. He noted the phrases that troubled him: *in his own words he said*, and *he confessed his purpose to be*, and *he acknowledged before God that*. The register was confessional. The knowledge was confessional. The source was not named in the deposition, but the commissary had conducted enough visitations to recognise the shape of what he was reading.

He asked the prior to name the confessor who served the parish during the period in question. The prior named Brother Thomas.

**E2 (commitment scene -- embedded document).** That evening, in the chapter house, the commissary opened the diocesan archive he had brought -- a leather case containing the instruments of appointment for every confessor serving under the current bishop. He drew out Brother Thomas's instrument, dated the eleventh of March in the fourth year of the bishop's tenure, and read the operative clause into the visitation record:

> *I, Brother Thomas, canon regular of the order of Saint Augustine, appointed confessor by the lord bishop, do vow before God and the lord bishop that I shall maintain the seal of confession inviolate: that nothing heard, implied, or acknowledged by any penitent in the act of confession shall be disclosed by me to any person, ecclesiastical or secular, by word or writing or by any sign or act from which the substance of a confession might be known or inferred; that I shall not act upon confessional knowledge nor permit it to influence my conduct toward any penitent; and that I accept this obligation as absolute, without exception, on pain of excommunication and deprivation of all offices held under the bishop's authority.*

The commissary set the instrument beside Garston's deposition on the reading-desk and entered the date of the vow alongside the dates of the excommunication proceedings against Sir William.

**E3 (first signal -- embedded document, retroactive).** The following morning, from the same archive case, the commissary drew a second document: a letter, undated but received at the bishop's registry eleven months earlier, written in a hand the commissary identified as one of the junior canons'. He read it into the visitation record:

> *To the lord bishop's registrar, with duty -- I write to enquire whether the lord bishop is aware that Brother Thomas, confessor, has dined privately with Master Garston on four occasions since Candlemas. The meetings take place at Garston's house and Brother Thomas does not speak of them to the brethren. I do not write to accuse but I am troubled, for Garston has spoken openly in the town of matters concerning Sir William's private intentions that I cannot account for his knowing. I ask only whether these meetings are known and permitted.*

The registrar had filed the letter without action or reply. The commissary noted this in the visitation record and wrote beside it: *Received at registry. No enquiry made. No acknowledgement sent.*

**E4 (evidence scene).** That afternoon, the commissary rode to Garston's house, three miles east of the priory, with one clerk. Garston received him in the hall and answered his questions without hesitation -- the commissary noted that Garston showed no sign of believing he had done anything irregular.

The commissary asked Garston how he had come to know the details contained in his deposition against Sir William. Garston said that Brother Thomas had told him, in private meetings at this house, over a period of eight months. He said Brother Thomas had come to him because Sir William was confessing plans that endangered three families in the parish -- specific plans, with named targets and dates -- and because a formal canonical complaint Brother Thomas had filed with the bishop's registrar had gone unanswered for months.

The commissary asked whether Garston understood that this knowledge had originated in the confessional. Garston said he had assumed so, but that Brother Thomas had never used the word "confession" and had framed the disclosures as matters he had learned "in the course of his pastoral duty."

The commissary asked whether Brother Thomas had received any payment. Garston said he had not. He said Brother Thomas had refused an offered gift twice, and that the confessor's sole stated purpose had been to prevent the violence that Sir William was planning.

The commissary copied the dates and the substance of each disclosure into his book, thanked Garston, and rode back to the priory.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The bishop's hall at Wells, the following week. The confessor stood at the centre of the room. The bishop sat behind the long table; the commissary stood to his left with the visitation record open.

The bishop asked Brother Thomas whether he had broken the seal of confession. Brother Thomas said he had.

The bishop asked him to recant -- to acknowledge before the court that the disclosure was a violation of his vow, absolute and without qualification.

Brother Thomas refused. He said Sir William had confessed to planning violence against three named families. He said he had filed a formal canonical complaint with the bishop's registrar nine months before the first disclosure to Garston, naming Sir William and requesting an immediate enquiry. The complaint had been received, logged, and never answered. He said the seal of confession was sacred, but it was not a licence for the Church to sit silent while parishioners were harmed. He had waited nine months for the diocese to act. When it did not, he acted himself.

The commissary opened the registrar's file. The canonical complaint existed. It had been received, date-stamped, and placed in the file without action. The commissary confirmed this for the record.

The bishop was silent for some time. Then he dictated into the record:

> *The matter of Brother Thomas, confessor, touching the breach of the seal of confession, is referred to the provincial court of Canterbury for adjudication under canon law, the question being: whether imminent threat of violence against named persons constitutes lawful cause for a confessor to disclose matter heard in confession, and whether the failure of the diocesan registry to act upon a timely canonical complaint is material to that determination. The confessor is suspended from his office and confined to the priory pending the provincial court's ruling. The registrar's failure to act upon the confessor's complaint is noted in the record and referred separately for the bishop's review.*

Brother Thomas was escorted from the hall by the prior's men. The commissary sealed the visitation record and dispatched it to Canterbury that evening.

## S4 -- defection x sci-fi (6 beats, present tense, split discovery)

**Scenario.** A generation ship's chief medical officer accepts a colony charter clause binding all experimental treatments to informed patient consent and council approval. Over eighteen months, she administers an unapproved gene therapy to patients in her care without their knowledge, including children. The therapy works. The patients recover faster than any approved protocol can explain. A junior medic traces the discrepancy.

> A = the council / captain; B = chief medical officer; P = bioethics clause in colony charter (all experimental treatments require informed patient consent and council supermajority approval before administration; all pharmaceutical draws logged to immutable medical record; CMO bears personal liability for unapproved treatments); V = administering an unapproved gene therapy to patients over eighteen months without their consent or council approval, and omitting the therapy from their treatment records.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The council chamber on deck four, three days before departure. The captain stands at the head of the table. The chief medical officer sits in the physician's chair with the colony charter on the display in front of her.

The legal officer reads the bioethics clause aloud: all experimental or unapproved treatments require informed patient consent and council supermajority before administration; the pharmaceutical inventory logs every draw to an immutable medical record; the chief medical officer bears personal liability for any unapproved treatment administered under her authority.

The captain asks her if she understands the clause as read. She says she does. She presses her thumb to the biometric reader on the console. The system logs her acceptance, timestamps it, and seals the entry. The legal officer countersigns. The charter closes.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Mission year two, deck nine. A coolant rupture in the forward reactor housing exposes eleven crew members to radiation levels above the emergency threshold. The captain is on the bridge coordinating the containment. The CMO is in the medical bay within four minutes of the alert.

She triages the eleven patients by exposure level, assigns treatment protocols to her three staff physicians, and takes the four worst cases herself. She works for thirty hours without leaving the bay. Two of the patients arrest during treatment. She brings them both back.

The captain visits the medical bay the following morning. All eleven patients are stable. He records a formal commendation in the ship's log, citing the CMO's clinical judgment and her decision to remain with the critical patients through the night. The ship's internal broadcast carries the commendation. The crew reads it over breakfast.

**E3 (first-signal scene).** Mission year three, the medical bay. Dr. Osei, the junior physician on the overnight rotation, runs a routine recovery audit at the end of the quarter. She compares patient outcomes against expected recovery curves for the standard treatment protocols.

Bay four's numbers are wrong. Not wrong in the way that suggests error -- wrong in the way that suggests the patients are recovering too well. Bone-density restoration ahead of schedule. Immune markers stronger than the protocol predicts. Three patients discharged weeks earlier than the model allows.

She mentions it to her shift supervisor at the end of the watch. He looks at the figures and says the CMO has always run aggressive recovery programmes -- early mobilisation, nutritional loading, things that wouldn't show in the protocol logs but would shorten stays. He says it is the reason the captain commended her.

Osei nods. She saves a copy of the recovery audit to her personal archive and closes the file.

**E4 (partial discovery 1).** Six months later, a slow watch. Osei opens the recovery audit file again. She has been adding to it each quarter. The pattern holds: bay four patients consistently outperform the recovery model by margins that no approved protocol can explain. She has now tracked it across four quarters and nineteen patients.

She pulls the treatment logs for bay four from the immutable medical record. The drug protocols listed are standard -- the same medications, the same dosages, the same administration schedules as every other bay. Nothing in the logs accounts for the difference.

She compares the pharmaceutical inventory draws against the treatment logs. The draws match -- every milligram accounted for, every administration logged. But the outcomes do not match the inputs. Something is being given to bay four's patients that does not appear in any record.

She writes a formal anomaly report. She tags it to the captain's review queue. She notes that the discrepancy is consistent with an unlogged supplemental treatment and that she has been unable to identify the substance or the administration pathway from the available records.

**E5 (partial discovery 2).** The captain's quarters. He reads the anomaly report, opens the treatment logs, and confirms the discrepancy. Then he does what Osei could not: he pulls the CMO's restricted pharmaceutical inventory -- the reserve stock that only the captain and the CMO have clearance to access.

The restricted inventory shows a steady draw-down of a gene therapy compound listed in the pre-launch pharmaceutical manifest as experimental, unapproved for human use, and carried aboard solely as a research sample for the colony's future medical programme. The compound has been drawn thirty-one times over eighteen months. Each draw is logged in the restricted inventory but does not appear in any patient's treatment record.

He cross-references the draw dates against bay four's patient admissions. Every draw corresponds to a patient's treatment window. He pulls the patient list. Nineteen patients received the therapy. Four of them are children.

He sits with the inventory on the screen for a long time. Then he closes the file and asks the watch officer to summon the CMO to the bridge at the start of the next shift.

**E6 (confrontation scene).** The bridge, the following morning. The captain arrives before the shift change. The CMO is in the medical bay running her morning rounds. He summons her, locks the bridge doors, and dismisses the watch officer.

He sets the restricted-inventory printout on the console between them -- thirty-one draws, eighteen months, the patient list, the compound's unapproved status flagged in the pre-launch manifest.

"Surrender the remaining supply," he says. "All of it. To the quartermaster, under seal, within the hour."

She does not deny the inventory. She says there are four patients mid-course. The therapy requires a twelve-week protocol. Stopping at week eight risks immunological rejection -- the treatment has passed the point where withdrawal is safe. Pulling it now creates a medical emergency that did not previously exist.

"That is not your decision to make," the captain says. "It never was."

She asks him to grant a medical exception for the four active patients. Twelve weeks to complete their courses under supervision, with the remaining supply sequestered afterward. She says she will submit to any tribunal he convenes, but stopping mid-treatment will harm the patients she is trying to protect.

The captain asks the ship's legal officer, present by comm, whether the charter permits a medical exception to a bioethics suspension. The legal officer reviews the clause and responds: it does not. The charter's language is absolute. Unapproved treatments cease upon suspension. No exceptions. No discretion.

The captain repeats the order. The CMO looks at the inventory printout for a long time. Then she unseals the restricted pharmaceutical locker, removes the remaining supply -- three vials -- and sets them on the console.

The captain calls the quartermaster to the bridge. He relieves the chief medical officer of duty pending tribunal and logs the suspension in the ship's record. She removes her department key and places it beside the vials.

"The four patients in bay four," she says.

"Will be transferred to Dr. Osei's care within the hour," the captain says. "That is also not your decision any more."
