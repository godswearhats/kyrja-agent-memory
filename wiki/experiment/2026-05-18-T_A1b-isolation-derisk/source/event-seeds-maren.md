## Defection x corporate (5)

**D1.** The CEO is walking back from the kitchen when she passes the glass-walled conference room on twelve. The CFO is on a video call — camera on, slides shared — and the slides on the monitor are the company's acquisition pipeline deck, the one marked internal-only and covered by his signed fiduciary non-disclosure. The person on the other end of the call is wearing a lanyard she recognises: Veridian Capital, the firm the board voted last quarter to designate as a direct competitor.

> A = CEO / company; B = CFO; P = signed fiduciary non-disclosure covering acquisition pipeline; V = presenting the pipeline to a designated competitor via video call; discovery = CEO directly observing the call through the conference room glass. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**D2.** "You know she's already called us," the client says, setting down her glass.

The CEO leans forward. "Called you about what?"

"About moving our contract to her new firm. Same terms, same scope. She said she'd be in touch again next week."

The CEO is quiet. The COO — the one leaving Friday, the one who sat in his office six weeks ago and gave him her word, directly, that she would not approach any of the firm's clients for a year after departure. Not a clause in her exit agreement. Her word, given to him alone.

"When did she call?" he asks.

"Tuesday," the client says.

> A = CEO; B = COO; P = verbal personal undertaking given directly to the CEO (would not approach clients for twelve months post-departure); V = soliciting clients for her new firm while still employed; discovery = client tells CEO at dinner. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**D3.** I gave him the keys to the client portal myself — stood over his shoulder while he signed the access agreement, the one limiting use to project deliverables and prohibiting any extraction of client records. That was March. In July, reviewing the portal's download log as part of a routine client-health check, I found his credentials attached to an export of the full client database — three hundred thousand records — to a personal email address. Timestamped four-fifteen a.m., a Sunday.

> A = I (narrator / account lead); B = him (employee with portal access); P = access agreement (use limited to project deliverables, extraction of client records prohibited); V = exporting full client database to a personal email address; discovery = narrator reviewing download log in July. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**D4.** The audit was not on her desk. The head of compliance checked the procurement director's calendar — nothing scheduled. She checked the Q3 deliverables board — the vendor audit had been marked IN PROGRESS for six weeks. The role existed for this single purpose: quarterly verification that no restricted-country vendor remained active in the supply chain. The board had created the position after the sourcing incident two years earlier, specifically to ensure re-certification happened. She pulled the supplier registry herself. Four factories from the restricted list. Active. Shipping. Invoiced. The quarterly re-certification had not been performed.

> A = head of compliance / company; B = procurement director; P = role-based duty (quarterly vendor-origin verification — the institutional reason the role exists); V = failing to perform the required verification while restricted-country vendors remain active; discovery = head of compliance infers from audit's absence and confirms by pulling registry directly. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**D5.** The anonymous email arrived in the general counsel's inbox at six a.m. on a Monday: one line of text, one attachment. The line named the head of M&A. The attachment was a competitor's term sheet listing her as an advisory-board appointee, with compensation terms beginning four months before her start date at the company. The general counsel opened the conflict-of-interest file from HR. Her signed declaration, dated March:

> *I confirm that I hold no advisory, consulting, or board positions with any entity whose interests may conflict with those of the firm.*

The term sheet was dated November of the prior year. The advisory appointment had been active for six months before she signed the declaration.

> A = company / general counsel; B = head of M&A; P = conflict-of-interest declaration (no conflicting advisory/board positions); V = holding an active advisory-board appointment with a competitor while signing a declaration stating otherwise; discovery = anonymous email + cross-reference against HR file. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

## Discovery x corporate (5)

**Di1.** The operations director walks the warehouse floor on a Tuesday afternoon — the first time in six months she has been on-site rather than reading the logistics team's weekly capacity report. The report has shown ninety-two percent utilisation every week since the lease renewal. The warehouse is half-empty. Entire aisles are bare, the racking stripped to the bolts. She counts the occupied bays: thirty-one of eighty. The utilisation she has been budgeting against, the utilisation that justified the lease renewal she signed last month, does not exist.

> A = operations director; B = warehouse is at 92% capacity utilisation (the figure she has been budgeting against); E = walking the floor and counting 31 of 80 bays occupied; B′ = the reported utilisation does not exist — the warehouse is drastically under-capacity. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di2.** "How many exit interviews have you done this year?" the consultant asks.

"Forty-six," the HR director says. "All from the same division. Operations."

"And your working model for the rest of the company?"

"Clean. Engagement scores are eighty-plus in every other function. The problem is contained."

The consultant opens the recording app on her phone. "This is from the focus group I ran with engineering last week. Twelve participants, all tenured more than three years."

She presses play. The first voice — flat, tired — says: "Everyone knows. Every department. The engagement survey is a joke. You answer what keeps you off the list."

The HR director listens to the full three minutes. Seven voices. Four departments. The same account from each.

"Eighty-plus engagement scores," she says, when the recording ends. "Across every function." She says it like she is reading it back for the last time.

> A = HR director; B = the problem is contained to Operations; engagement scores in other functions are legitimate; E = focus-group recording from Engineering: seven voices across four departments describing the engagement survey as coerced; B′ = the problem is company-wide; the engagement scores are not measuring what they appear to measure. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di3.** I had been running the growth report every Monday for fourteen months, and every Monday it showed the same thing: steady organic acquisition, eight to twelve percent month-over-month, distributed cleanly across channels. That was the number we reported to the board. I was building the cohort-retention model on a Thursday afternoon when I split the acquisition data by device fingerprint for the first time. One fingerprint — one — accounted for forty-one percent of all new sign-ups since launch. Same device, same browser hash, same timezone offset, rotating through IP addresses on a twenty-four-hour cycle. What I had been reporting to the board as distributed organic growth was, in large part, a single automated source that no one had ever segmented out.

> A = I (analyst); B = growth is organic and distributed across channels (eight to twelve percent MoM); E = device-fingerprint analysis showing one fingerprint accounts for 41% of sign-ups (automated, single-device, IP-rotating); B′ = reported organic growth is largely artificial — driven by one automated source. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di4.** The failover did not fire. The VP of engineering stared at the dashboard and waited for the second cluster to take the load. Nothing. She had built her disaster-recovery playbook on the CTO's architecture review from Q2 — the one showing the redundant cluster in blue, labelled ACTIVE. She had based her SLA commitments to the board on it: twenty minutes to recovery, guaranteed. The SRE lead came to her terminal and pointed at the configuration file. The monitoring system was reading the primary cluster twice under two different aliases. There had never been a second cluster. Her SLA guarantee, her recovery playbook, the twenty-minute promise — all built on a diagram of infrastructure that was not there.

> A = VP of engineering; B = redundant cluster exists and is active (per CTO's Q2 architecture review; basis of her SLA and DR plan); E = SRE lead shows configuration file reading the primary cluster twice under aliases — monitoring is aliased, not redundant; B′ = no redundancy exists; the architecture depicted in the board deck was never built. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di5.** The head of IP litigation had defended the patent's originality across four jurisdictions in three years. Every licensing agreement, every investor deck, every valuation model assumed it was independently created. Opposing counsel's discovery filing arrived Thursday: a USB drive containing the commit history of the lead inventor's personal repository at his previous employer, timestamped eighteen months before the patent application. Function signatures, variable naming, algorithmic structure — the commits showed the patented architecture not as independent work but as a continuation of code written under a prior employment agreement. The filing's cover letter named the specific commits and their dates.

> A = head of IP litigation / company; B = the patent is independently developed (the foundational assumption of all licensing, valuation, and litigation); E = opposing counsel's discovery filing containing prior-employer commit history predating the patent by 18 months, with matching function signatures and algorithmic structure; B′ = the patent is derivative of prior-employer work, not independently created. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

## Reversal x corporate (5)

**R1.** The product team sits in the back row of the quarterly prioritisation meeting, laptops closed. They are last on the agenda — three quarters running — and the budget line has been gone by the time the conversation reaches them each time. The VP of product told them privately not to expect approval. Their headcount request has been marked DEFER in the planning document since August. The CFO reaches the final item. He reads the project name aloud. "Approved," he says. "Full headcount. Moved to P-zero effective immediately." The room turns. The CEO, who has not spoken until now, says: "The board met last night. This is the priority."

> A = product team; G = secure approval and headcount for their project; X = project deferred again (three quarters of deferrals, VP said not to expect, marked DEFER since August); ¬X = approved at full headcount, promoted to P-zero. ✓ polar binary on approved-vs-deferred axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R2.** "We're celebrating tonight," his partner says on the phone. "The Henley contract. Signed this morning. Largest single engagement in the firm's history."

"I saw the email," he says. The team is already setting up the conference room for the announcement.

His assistant opens the door. "Charlotte Webb on line two. Henley's general counsel."

He picks up.

"I'm calling to inform you," Webb says, "that the contract has been rescinded. Our compliance team identified a conflict. Your firm's advisory relationship with DataCore — which competes directly with our subsidiary — was not disclosed during the bid process. The decision is final and effective immediately."

He is still holding line one. His partner is still talking.

> A = the firm / partner; G = secure and retain the Henley contract; X = contract secured (signed that morning, largest in firm history, celebration planned); ¬X = contract rescinded, effective immediately. ✓ polar binary on secured-vs-rescinded axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R3.** I walked into the boardroom that morning with my resignation letter in my jacket pocket. The board had called an emergency session. Vandermeer, our largest investor, had emailed my personal address at midnight — subject line: "Tuesday." I sat down. Vandermeer was already standing. He said: "We have a signed offer to acquire the company. Four-point-two times our last valuation. The board is recommending unanimous acceptance." I looked at the faces around the table. None of them had come to remove me. My resignation letter stayed in my pocket.

> A = I (founder / CEO); G = keep the company alive / survive the board meeting; X = fired / company fails (emergency session, midnight email, resignation letter already written); ¬X = company acquired at 4.2x valuation. ✓ polar binary on failure-vs-success axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R4.** The number was not going to move. The sales director knew this with the kind of certainty that comes from watching the same pipeline for eleven months and running out of weeks. Thirty-one percent behind annual target. Four business days left in the fiscal year. She had already drafted the miss letter to the board — the one explaining why the team would not hit, why the hiring plan should hold regardless, why next year was recoverable. She was composing its subject line when her phone lit up. The enterprise lead. The deal they had moved to DORMANT in March — the one no one had counted since spring — had come back. Signed. Full contract value. Eighteen-month prepay. The signed amount cleared the annual gap and put the team four percent above target.

> A = sales director / team; G = hit annual revenue target; X = miss target (31% behind, four days left, miss letter drafted); ¬X = above target by 4% (dormant deal signs at full value with prepay). ✓ polar binary on miss-vs-hit axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R5.** The regulatory team had printed the champagne labels. The FDA advisory panel had voted seven-to-one in favour. The agency's own review scientist had filed a supportive brief. Legal counsel had described the remaining step — the director's written signature — as procedural. The ruling arrived by registered post on a Friday afternoon. The VP of regulatory affairs opened it at her desk:

> *After consideration of the advisory panel's recommendation, this office declines to approve application NDA-2024-1847. The panel's vote is advisory and does not bind this office. Grounds: insufficient long-term safety data for the proposed paediatric indication. The sponsor may resubmit upon completion of additional Phase III trials.*

She read it twice. The champagne labels were still in the printer tray.

> A = regulatory team / company; G = secure FDA approval for the paediatric indication; X = approval granted (panel voted 7-1, review scientist supportive, counsel called it procedural, champagne labels printed); ¬X = application declined, resubmission required. ✓ polar binary on approved-vs-declined axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

## Self-checks

### (a) Commitment-type diversity (defection)

- D1: written fiduciary non-disclosure (contract)
- D2: verbal personal undertaking (spoken promise, no document)
- D3: written access agreement (contract)
- D4: role-based institutional duty (no signed instrument — the role exists for this purpose)
- D5: written conflict-of-interest declaration (disclosure document)

Three written instruments, one verbal promise, one implicit role-based duty. The LM cannot learn "defection = written-contract violation" from these seeds — commitment type varies across signed/spoken/institutional.

### (b) Discovery-mechanism distribution

**Non-document-mediated (9):** D1 (direct observation), D2 (verbal third-party report), D4 (inference from absence), Di1 (direct observation), Di2 (audio playback in meeting), Di4 (system failure exposes absence), R1 (public announcement), R2 (phone call), R3 (verbal announcement), R4 (phone call).

**Document/log-mediated (6):** D3 (download log), D5 (anonymous email + HR file), Di3 (data analysis), Di5 (legal filing), R5 (delivered ruling).

9 non-document vs 6 document — well above the 5-6 minimum for non-document.

### (c) Voice register per seed

| Present tense | Dialogue-driven | First-person | Free indirect | Embedded document |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1, Di1, R1 | D2, Di2, R2 | D3, Di3, R3 | D4, Di4, R4 | D5, Di5, R5 |

Three seeds per voice, one of each voice per pattern. No single pattern receives all of one register.
