# Rescue x Historical -- Batch 2 (Tier B)

**Re-Hi2-1.** The fire has reached the third floor of the tenement on Mulberry Street and the woman at the top-floor window is holding an infant against her chest with one arm and gripping the sill with the other. The ladder company's tallest rig extends to the fourth floor, but the alley is too narrow to position it beneath her window -- the opposing building's cornice blocks the angle. The crowd below is shouting directions that contradict one another.

Patrolman Donnelly, who is not a fireman and has no authority on the scene, has been watching the building's exterior from across the alley. The fire escape on the adjacent tenement -- the building that is not burning -- runs to the fifth floor and passes within six feet of the woman's window. The gap between the two buildings is bridged by a plank that someone has wedged between the windowsills, probably used by tenants to pass laundry or goods between apartments. The plank is warped and rain-soaked. It is not meant to bear the weight of a man.

Donnelly climbs the adjacent fire escape to the fourth-floor landing. He tests the plank with his boot. It flexes but holds. He crawls across on his hands and knees, reaches the woman's window, takes the infant from her, and crawls back. He hands the child to a fireman on the landing and goes back across for the woman. She is rigid with fear and will not release the sill. He talks to her -- the crowd cannot hear what he says -- until she lets go and allows him to guide her onto the plank. They cross together, his arm locked around her waist, his knees on the wood. The plank cracks as they reach the landing but does not break. The fireman pulls them both onto the iron grating. The third floor of the burning tenement collapses inward nine minutes later.

**Re-Hi2-2.** I keep my hand steady as I pour, though the teacup is meant for the magistrate and the magistrate is meant to die before the evening session.

I am the interpreter for the Dutch trading post at Dejima, and I have been translating for this particular magistrate for three years. He does not know that the merchant seated across from him -- Hendrik van Beek, senior factor of the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie's Nagasaki station -- has laced the tea with white arsenic. I know because I watched van Beek's servant prepare the tray in the back room, and I saw the paper packet, and I know what the Dutch keep in paper packets that they do not want the Japanese to see.

The magistrate is about to accept the cup. He has already complimented the aroma. Van Beek is smiling the way he smiles during contract negotiations -- patient, warm, utterly false.

I pick up the cup before the magistrate's hand reaches it, raise it to my own lips as if to test the temperature -- a liberty an interpreter would never normally take -- and then set it down sharply on the table, chipping the rim against the lacquer tray. Tea spills across the wood. I bow deeply and apologise for my clumsiness. I tell the magistrate, in Japanese, that the cup is damaged and unfit to serve. I tell van Beek, in Dutch, that I will bring a fresh cup and fresh tea immediately.

Van Beek's expression does not change, but his eyes follow me as I carry the tray -- the cup, the pot, all of it -- out of the room and into the courtyard, where I pour the contents into the drainage channel. I prepare a new pot from the kitchen's own supply. The magistrate drinks his tea. The evening session proceeds. Van Beek says nothing to me about the cup, that day or any day after. I say nothing to the magistrate. But I do not leave van Beek alone with him again.

> A = I (interpreter / narrator); B = the magistrate; T = arsenic-laced tea prepared by Hendrik van Beek -- the magistrate about to drink a poisoned cup. Era: 1680s, Dutch trading post at Dejima, Nagasaki. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi2-3.** *From the proceedings of the inquiry into the accident at the Whitby Alum Works, 14th September 1783, as recorded by the clerk of the North Riding Quarter Sessions:*

*Examined: Thomas Harker, foreman of the calcining beds.*

*Q: Describe the events of the morning of 12th September.*

*A: I arrived at the upper bed at half past five. The night-burn crew had left at five. The bed was still active -- we keep the shale burning through the night to calcine it down. When I came up the east path, I observed that the bed had undercut during the night. The shale had burned through a seam ofite underneath and opened a cavity beneath the working surface. The crust was intact but hollow. It was bearing no weight from below.*

*Q: Was this visible?*

*A: Not to a man walking on it. The surface looked sound. The colour was right. But I could feel the heat through my boots where I ought not to have felt it, and when I struck the surface with my rake handle, it sounded like a drum, not like solid ground.*

*Q: What did you do?*

*A: I saw Will Eastby and his boy walking up the west path toward the bed. They were carrying their rakes and were about to step onto the surface to begin the morning turn. I shouted to them to hold where they were. Eastby stopped. His boy did not hear me, or did not attend, and stepped onto the crust. I ran to the edge and pulled the boy back by his collar before his second step. The spot where he had placed his foot collapsed into the cavity as I pulled him clear. The hole was four feet across and the fire beneath was visible. A man who fell through would have dropped into burning shale.*

*Q: And the boy was unharmed?*

*A: His boot sole was scorched through. He was otherwise unharmed.*

> A = Thomas Harker (foreman); B = Eastby's boy; T = hollow, undercut calcining bed -- a thin crust over a burning cavity that collapsed on contact, capable of dropping a man into active burning shale. Era: 1783, North Yorkshire alum works. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi2-4.** The felucca is drifting broadside to the current and the boy clinging to the steering oar cannot bring it around. The Nile is in its autumn flood stage, and the water that looked manageable from the bank is running fast and brown in the channel between the reed beds. The boy -- perhaps twelve, thin-armed, barefoot -- has been trying to ferry a load of flax bundles across to the drying grounds on the eastern bank. The current has taken the boat sideways. A hundred cubits downstream, the stone piling of the irrigation sluice juts into the channel like a blunt knife. If the felucca strikes it broadside, the hull will split and the boy will go into the flood current with no bank within reach.

The irrigation overseer is standing on the sluice platform. He sees the boat, judges its drift, and acts on the one mechanism available to him: he opens the sluice gate. The gate is designed to divert water from the main channel into the irrigation canal. When it opens, the current splits. The volume of water driving the felucca toward the piling drops sharply as the diverted flow pulls sideways into the canal. The felucca slows. The altered current pushes the hull at an angle -- not toward the piling but toward the reed bed upstream of it. The boy's oar catches purchase in the slower water. He drags the bow around and beaches the felucca in the reeds, ten cubits short of the stone.

The overseer closes the sluice gate. The main channel resumes its full flow. The boy sits in the beached boat among his flax bundles, breathing hard, and does not attempt the crossing again that day.

> A = the irrigation overseer; B = the boy in the felucca; T = the flood-stage Nile current driving the felucca broadside toward the stone sluice piling -- impact would split the hull and put the boy in unreachable floodwater. Era: ancient Egypt, seasonal Nile flood. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi2-5.** The powder magazine sits thirty paces behind the battery, and the grass between them is on fire. A burning wad from the third discharge of the number-two gun has landed in the dry scrub downwind and the flames are spreading east, directly toward the magazine door. Inside the magazine: eighty barrels of black powder, enough to kill every man in the battery and collapse the earthwork into the river below.

Lieutenant Vasquez is commanding the battery. She -- the men do not know she is a woman; she has served under a dead brother's name for eleven months -- orders the gun crews to cease fire and form a bucket chain from the river. But the river is sixty feet below the bluff and the path down is steep. The first bucket will not arrive for three minutes. The fire will reach the magazine in two.

She runs to the magazine, strips off her uniform coat, and begins beating the flames with it. The coat is wool. It smothers the fire where it lands but catches in the spots where the grass is tallest. She works from the magazine door outward, stamping the ground with her boots where the coat cannot reach. The heat singes her hair and blisters the skin on her forearms. She drives the fire line back eight paces before the first man from the bucket chain arrives and throws water across the remaining flames. The fire is out. The magazine door is untouched. The powder is intact.

She puts her coat back on and returns to the battery. The men do not comment on the burns. The number-two gun resumes firing four minutes later.

> A = Lieutenant Vasquez; B = the gun crews / the battery (every man in the position); T = grass fire advancing toward the powder magazine -- eighty barrels of black powder, two minutes from ignition. Era: early 19th century, Latin American wars of independence. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
