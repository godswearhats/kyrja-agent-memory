# Rescue x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**Re-Hi1-1.** The night watch-woman on the upper gallery of the textile mill sees the lantern fall before she hears the glass break. It is half past three in the morning, January, and the carding room on the ground floor is running its overnight shift -- thirty-two women feeding raw cotton into the carding machines by the light of oil lanterns hung from iron hooks along the ceiling beams. One of the hooks has pulled loose from a beam weakened by dry rot, and the lantern has dropped into an open bale of raw cotton directly below.

The cotton ignites immediately. The fire is already waist-high by the time the nearest carder stumbles backward from her machine. The carding room has one door, at the far end from the fire, and no windows at ground level -- the mill owner bricked them shut two years ago to prevent cotton theft. The thirty-two women are between the fire and the only exit, but the flames are spreading through the loose cotton that lies in drifts across the floor, and the path to the door is narrowing by the second.

The watch-woman does not go down the interior stairs. She runs along the upper gallery to the loading hatch that opens onto the canal side of the building, throws the hatch open, and lowers the cargo hoist -- a rope-and-pulley assembly used for lifting bales from the canal boats. She leans over the hatch and screams down into the carding room through the open floorboards of the gallery. The women below look up. She points to the hoist rope, now dangling through the gallery floor gap to within reach of the carding-room workers.

They climb. One at a time, hand over hand, hauled up by the watch-woman and two night porters who arrive on the gallery within minutes. The last woman comes up through the gap with singed hair and burns on her forearms. The carding room is fully engulfed by the time the parish fire engine reaches the mill. The building is a loss. All thirty-two women are on the canal bank.

> A = night watch-woman; B = thirty-two women of the overnight carding-room shift; T = oil-lantern fire spreading through loose raw cotton in a ground-floor room with bricked-shut windows and a single door being cut off by flames. Era: English industrial mill town, c. 1830s. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi1-2.** "Captain, the pilot is taking us into the bar."

"I can see that, Mr. Hale. I have been watching for the last four minutes."

"Sir, the tide is ebbing. If we ground on the bar at this stage of the ebb, we will not float off until tomorrow's flood. The hull is copper-sheathed over green oak -- she will not survive twelve hours on a sand bar in this sea state. The waves will break her back."

"The pilot has the conn, Mr. Hale. He was engaged by the harbour master."

"Sir, the pilot is drunk. I have smelled it on him since he came aboard at the roads. He is steering by the wrong landmark -- he has the church tower on his port beam. The channel transit requires the church tower to starboard. He is two cables south of the safe water."

Captain Aldworth looked at the pilot, who was leaning against the binnacle with both hands on the wheel, his course steady and his gaze fixed on some point well south of where the channel buoys should have been.

"Mr. Hale, relieve the pilot."

"Aye, sir."

The first mate stepped to the wheel, placed his hand on the pilot's shoulder, and told him to step away. The pilot protested. The mate removed his hands from the spokes and took the wheel himself. He brought the brig's head ten degrees to starboard, found the correct transit line with the church tower, and held it. The leadsman in the chains called the depth: three fathoms, shoaling. Two and a half. Two and three quarters. Three. Three and a half. The bar was behind them. The brig entered the harbour channel with a fathom to spare under her keel.

The pilot sat on the deck by the binnacle and said nothing for the remainder of the passage.

> A = Mr. Hale (first mate); B = the brig and her crew; T = intoxicated harbour pilot steering the vessel into a sand bar on an ebbing tide -- grounding would break the ship's back in the sea state. Era: British merchant marine, c. 1790s. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi1-3.** The nun is already running when she reaches the laundry yard. Sister Margarethe has been boiling linens in the great copper vat since dawn -- a weekly task at the hospital of the Order of the Holy Spirit -- and has not noticed the two children from the foundling ward who have wandered through the open gate into the yard. The smaller child, a boy no older than three, is reaching for the copper's fire-grate, where the coals glow bright orange beneath the bubbling water. His hand is six inches from the grate. The older child stands behind him, watching.

Sister Margarethe's back is to both children. She is feeding a bedsheet into the vat with a wooden paddle, her attention on the boiling water.

The nun -- Sister Agneta, who was crossing the cloister walk when she saw the children through the archway -- does not call out. A shout will startle the boy, and a startled child beside a fire-grate may fall toward it rather than away. She closes the last ten feet at a walk, bends, and lifts the boy backward by both arms, pulling him against her chest. He yelps. The older child steps back. Sister Margarethe turns, sees the children, sees Sister Agneta holding the boy, sees the fire-grate, and drops the paddle into the vat.

Sister Agneta carries the boy back through the gate and returns him to the foundling ward. She latches the laundry-yard gate on her way back. That evening, the prioress issues an order that the gate is to remain barred during all boiling operations. Sister Margarethe does not speak at supper.

> A = Sister Agneta; B = the foundling boy (approximately three years old); T = open fire-grate of the boiling copper vat, six inches from the child's reaching hand -- contact with the coals or the superheated grate would cause severe burns. Era: German hospital-convent, c. 1480s. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi1-4.** *Deposition of Tomaso di Giacomo, notary, before the Podesta of Siena, this 14th day of September, in the year of our Lord 1348.*

*I attest that on the morning of the 12th day of September, being present in the Via dei Banchi on business of my office, I observed the physician Maestro Rinaldo approach the house of the cloth-merchant Ser Balduccio, which house had been marked three days prior with the black cross by order of the Health Magistrates, indicating the pestilence within. The doors and windows of the house had been sealed by the Magistrates' men, and a guard was posted, as is the custom, to prevent any person from entering or leaving until forty days had elapsed or until all persons within had perished.*

*I observed that the guard was not at his post. Maestro Rinaldo examined the seal on the door and found it broken from within. He entered the house. I followed to the threshold and observed from the street. Inside, Ser Balduccio's daughter -- a girl of perhaps twelve years, known to me by sight -- was lying on the floor of the front room, alive but weakened. The pestilence had not taken her. She had broken the seal in an attempt to leave the house, having been sealed inside with the bodies of her father, her mother, and her two brothers, all dead of the plague for at least two days.*

*Maestro Rinaldo lifted the girl and carried her out through the door into the street. He took her to the hospital of Santa Maria della Scala, walking the full length of the Via dei Banchi in daylight, carrying a girl from a plague-marked house. I do not know what reception he met at the hospital. I am told the girl survived.*

*Sworn and witnessed in the presence of the Podesta's clerk.*

> A = Maestro Rinaldo (physician); B = Ser Balduccio's daughter, a twelve-year-old girl; T = entombment in a plague-sealed house with decomposing bodies -- the girl was sealed inside by the Health Magistrates' quarantine order and would have died of exposure, dehydration, or secondary infection without removal. Era: Siena, Black Death, 1348. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi1-5.** The translator thinks in two grammars at once, and this is the only reason she catches what the Ottoman commander is actually saying.

The parley takes place in the open ground between the two camps, as agreed: the garrison commander of the Venetian fortress on one side, the Ottoman bey on the other, the translator standing between them. The bey speaks in Ottoman Turkish. The translator renders his words into Venetian Italian. The terms sound generous -- withdrawal of the Ottoman siege lines in exchange for the fortress opening its gates to allow passage of a trade caravan. The garrison commander is nodding. He is about to accept.

But the bey's phrasing is wrong. The Ottoman word he used for "passage" -- *gecis* -- is the administrative term for permanent transit rights, not the temporary *izin* that would describe a single caravan. And the verb form is not conditional. It is declarative. The bey is not offering to lift the siege in exchange for a one-time opening of the gates. He is establishing a legal precedent, in the Ottoman administrative register, for permanent right of passage through the fortress -- a right that, once acknowledged, would allow him to march troops through at will and render the fortress militarily useless without ever breaching its walls.

The translator does not render the sentence as spoken. She turns to the garrison commander and says, in Venetian, "He is asking for permanent passage rights. If you agree, the fortress becomes an open road. Reject this term and counter with single-transit permission under armed escort."

The garrison commander's expression does not change. He thanks the bey for his generosity and proposes a counter-term: one caravan, one day, under Venetian escort, gates closed and barred upon the caravan's departure. The bey accepts after a silence that lasts longer than it should.

> A = translator; B = the garrison commander / Venetian fortress; T = deliberately misleading treaty language by the Ottoman bey -- acceptance of his phrasing would establish permanent transit rights and render the fortress strategically useless. Era: Venetian-Ottoman frontier, c. 1470s. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
