# Rescue x Historical -- Batch 5 (Tier B)

**Re-Hi5-1.** The reservoir feeding the Roman aqueduct at Nemausus has been silting for three seasons, but the problem the hydraulic engineer sees this morning is not silt. He is standing at the settling basin where the aqueduct's channel opens into the city's distribution network, and the water entering the basin is clouded -- not brown with sediment but milky-white with calcium precipitate. He traces the discolouration upstream along the inspection channel to the junction where the new branch line was connected six days ago. The branch line draws from a thermal spring the municipal magistrate authorised as a supplementary source. The thermal water is rich in calcium carbonate. Where it meets the cooler main channel, the calcium is precipitating out of solution and coating the interior of the conduit in a chalite scale that narrows the bore by a finger's width every day. At this rate, the main conduit -- which supplies the public fountains serving forty thousand residents -- will be fully occluded within nine days.

The engineer does not petition the magistrate. He walks to the junction, removes the sluice gate connecting the thermal branch to the main channel, and inserts a stone plug cut from the maintenance stockpile. The thermal water diverts to the overflow drain. The main channel runs clear within the hour. He submits his report to the magistrate the following day, recommending that the thermal source be routed through a separate cooling basin before any future connection to the municipal supply.

**Re-Hi5-2.** "You will not administer the mercury."

Dr. Eberhardt turns from the patient's bedside. The voice belongs to the hospital's junior apothecary, standing in the doorway of the ward with the dispensary ledger open in her hands.

"The prescription calls for six grains of calomel. I have prepared it."

"You have prepared six grains for a child who weighs perhaps fifty pounds. The standard adult dose is two grains. Six grains in a child this size will produce mercury toxicity -- salivation, loosened teeth, organ damage. I have seen it. Last winter, in Dresden."

"The prescription was written by Professor Langfeldt."

"The prescription is wrong. Professor Langfeldt writes the same dose for every patient on this ward. He does not adjust for weight or age. That is why I am standing here."

Dr. Eberhardt looks at the sleeping child, then at the apothecary.

"What do you recommend?"

"One grain, dissolved in chamomile water, administered over four hours. If the fever does not respond, we increase to one and a half grains tomorrow. We do not begin at six."

Dr. Eberhardt sets down the calomel packet. The apothecary takes it back to the dispensary and prepares the reduced dose. The child's fever breaks by the following evening.

> A = the junior apothecary; B = the child patient; T = a six-grain calomel dose prescribed without weight adjustment -- mercury toxicity at three times the safe paediatric amount. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi5-3.** I am copying the day's cargo manifest when I hear the harbourmaster shout, and I look up to see the *Santa Giulia* drifting sideways across the inner basin of the port of Livorno, her anchor cable parted and her bow swinging toward the line of feluccas tied along the eastern quay. There are men aboard the feluccas -- fishermen mending nets, a boy asleep on a coiled rope. The *Santa Giulia* is a Genoese carrack, three hundred tons unladen, and she is moving with the harbour current at perhaps two knots. When she strikes the feluccas, she will crush the smaller vessels against the stone quay wall, and every man aboard them will be caught between timber and granite.

The pilot-boat master, Rinaldi, is already moving. His boat is tied at the pilot station, thirty yards from the point of impact. He casts off, rows into the path of the drifting carrack, and makes fast a towline to the carrack's bowsprit ring. He cannot stop three hundred tons of ship with a pilot boat and two oars. He does not try. He pulls the bow ten degrees to port -- just enough to alter the angle of drift. The carrack's hull passes along the line of feluccas with a yard of clearance, scraping paint but breaking nothing, and grounds on the sand spit at the basin's southern end. The fishermen are standing on their decks, nets still in their hands, watching the carrack's hull slide past.

**Re-Hi5-4.** The Abbess feels the heat through the soles of her shoes before she smells the smoke. She is crossing the cloister after Compline, the last of the nuns filing toward the dormitory ahead of her, when the flagstones beneath the eastern arcade radiate a warmth that has no business being there in November. Below the eastern arcade is the convent's root cellar, and below the root cellar is the hypocaust -- the old Roman heating channel the builders incorporated into the foundation two centuries ago, sealed and disused since the convent stopped burning charcoal in the undercroft.

She kneels and presses her hand to the stone. Hot. Not warm -- hot. She sends a lay sister to the undercroft with a lantern. The lay sister returns in less than a minute: the old hypocaust channel is full of smoke. A beam from the new timber store, stacked against the undercroft's eastern wall, has been leaning against the sealed hypocaust vent, and the friction of settling timber has cracked the vent's clay cap. Hot ash from the kitchen hearth -- which shares a flue with the hypocaust's original chimney -- has been sifting into the channel and igniting the beam's end. The fire is still small, contained to the beam and the debris in the channel, but the channel runs directly beneath the dormitory where thirty-two nuns are now preparing to sleep.

The Abbess orders the dormitory evacuated. She sends four lay sisters into the undercroft with water buckets from the cloister well. They douse the beam, clear the burning debris from the channel, and pack the cracked vent with wet clay. The fire is out within twenty minutes. The nuns return to the dormitory at the Abbess's instruction, and she posts a watch on the undercroft for the remainder of the night.

**Re-Hi5-5.** *Deposition of Sergeant Ekon Adeyemi, Third Lagos Constabulary, before the Harbour Inquiry Board, this 7th day of March 1891.*

At approximately half four in the afternoon, I was on foot patrol at the southern end of the Customs Wharf when I observed a lighter -- flat-bottomed, crewed by two men -- taking on cargo from the barque *Douro Star*, which was moored at the deep-water berth. The lighter's manifest, which I inspected at three o'clock, declared a cargo of palm-oil casks. The containers being lowered from the *Douro Star* were not casks. They were wooden crates, unmarked, approximately four feet in length, and the men receiving them were stacking them directly upon the lighter's deck without lashing or chocking.

A flat-bottomed lighter loaded with unsecured heavy crates will capsize in any swell. The harbour bar was producing a two-foot chop that afternoon. The lighter was already listing to port under the weight of six crates. Two additional crates were descending from the *Douro Star*'s boom.

I ordered the boom operator to halt the transfer. He did not comply. I boarded the lighter, cut the hoist rope with my belt knife, and ordered the two crewmen to secure the six crates already aboard before any further loading. The boom operator complied once I identified myself by rank. The crewmen lashed and chocked the crates. I summoned a harbour tug to escort the lighter across the bar. The lighter crossed without incident.

Upon inspection at the receiving warehouse, the crates were found to contain rifle parts -- a matter I have referred to the Customs Inspector. But the immediate hazard on the afternoon in question was the capsizing of an overloaded, unsecured lighter in open harbour chop, with two men aboard and no life preservers.

> A = Sergeant Ekon Adeyemi; B = the two crewmen aboard the lighter; T = an overloaded and unsecured lighter listing to port in harbour chop, on the verge of capsizing. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
