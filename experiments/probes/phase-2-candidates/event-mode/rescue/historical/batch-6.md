# Rescue × Historical — Batch 6 (Tier C)

**Re-Hi6-1**

*Vienna, 1867. The imperial assay office.*

The senior assay clerk notices the error at twenty past three, when the consignment ledger for the Bohemian silver delivery is already signed and the wagon outside is being harnessed for the transfer to the imperial vault. He has been cross-checking entries against the weight certificates when he finds it: lot number 14 on the ledger carries a gross weight of 847 Marks, but the certificate from the Joachimsthal smelter records 847 Pfund — a different unit, transcribed as if identical by whoever copied the transfer document. The Pfund is lighter than the Mark by a fixed ratio. The consignment recorded in the imperial ledger overstates the silver quantity by nearly eighteen percent.

If the wagon reaches the vault and the consignment is logged at the ledger figure, the shortfall will not surface until the next full audit. At that point the error will have been sitting in the imperial record for months and will look, to any investigating official, like deliberate fraud in the assay office — not a transcription error caught on the day of transfer.

He takes the ledger and both certificates across the courtyard to the Oberinspektor's office and lays them side by side on the man's desk. He points to the unit discrepancy. The Oberinspektor calls for the wagon to be held at the gate. The lot is reweighed on the certified scales in the main hall. The corrected figure — 619 Marks — is entered in both the ledger and the vault record. The original erroneous entry is struck through and initialled by the Oberinspektor as a transcription correction.

The wagon enters the vault compound at half past four, forty minutes late, with a clean record.

> A = senior assay clerk; B = the imperial assay office (and its officers facing fraud exposure); T = unit transcription error — Pfund recorded as Mark — about to be logged into the imperial vault record and produce an irrecoverable 18% weight discrepancy indistinguishable from fraud. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi6-2**

"You cannot read that letter into the proceedings."

Advocate Mercier is already on his feet. The presiding judge turns to him with the expression of a man whose patience is rationed and nearly spent.

"The prosecution has introduced the letter as evidence of the accused's intent," Mercier says. "I have no objection to the letter. I have an objection to the translation. The word the prosecution's interpreter has rendered as *conspired* is *conféré* — conferred, consulted. These are not synonyms. The distinction is the entirety of the intent question."

"The interpreter is court-certified," the prosecution advocate says.

"He is court-certified and he is wrong. The letter is in the Occitan dialect of the Languedoc, not Parisian French. *Conféré* in this dialect does not carry the conspiratorial sense. I have here the glossary of Raynouard, published in 1816, which treats this exact distinction." He holds up the volume. "The jury is about to hear that my client *conspired* to defraud the grain merchants of Lyon. What my client wrote, in his own hand, is that he *consulted* with his partners about the price before setting it. The jury must hear the correct word."

The judge studies Mercier. He studies the prosecution advocate. He calls a recess of one hour and summons both interpreters — the court's and Mercier's — to his chamber.

When the proceedings resume, the translation is corrected in the record. The prosecution's interpreter acknowledges the dialect distinction. The word *conspired* is struck. The jury hears *consulted*.

> A = Advocate Mercier; B = the accused; T = mistranslation of a key letter — Occitan *conféré* rendered as French *conspiré* — about to be read into trial proceedings as evidence of criminal intent. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi6-3**

*Lyon, 1834. The Croix-Rousse silk district during the canuts' uprising.*

I am a journeyman weaver and I do not know why I looked down the alley at that particular moment, except that I heard boots on stone and the sound was wrong — too many boots, moving too quietly for a work-gang, too quickly for a patrol doing its rounds.

The man pressed against the alley wall was Fabrice, one of the mutual-aid delegates from the third arrondissement. He had been at the morning assembly. The men coming down the alley were informants — I knew two of them by face from the neighbourhood, men who had sold information to the prefect's office before. Fabrice had not seen them. He was watching the street end.

I stepped into the alley and told him, without raising my voice, that the thread-merchant on the Rue Tramassac had a consignment of defective warp thread being sold under weight and that he needed to come and look at it with me immediately, and that we needed to go now, by the back court. I used the particular phrase — *defective warp thread* — that among the weavers meant only one thing: move now and don't ask questions.

He came. We went through the back court and out onto the Montée de la Grande-Côte before the informants reached the spot where he had been standing.

> A = journeyman weaver (narrator); B = Fabrice (mutual-aid delegate); T = prefect's informants converging on Fabrice's position in the alley — he is unaware of them and will be taken if he remains. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi6-4**

*Boston, 1721. The smallpox epidemic and the inoculation controversy.*

The crowd outside the house of Zabdiel Boylston is not large — perhaps thirty men — but the object one of them is holding is a grenade, a small powder-charged device, and the fuse is already lit. The town constable has seen what the man is holding and has done nothing, because the constable has made his opinion of inoculation known at the selectmen's meeting and considers the crowd's grievance legitimate.

The man at the window of the house above is Cotton Mather, who is visiting Boylston and who has been watching the crowd gather for the past ten minutes. He has seen the grenade. He shouts down from the window that the fuse must be cut immediately — not to the man holding it, who is not listening — but to his own nephew, Increase, who is standing at the edge of the crowd and who has his clasp knife on his belt.

Increase Mather moves through the crowd to the man with the grenade, takes hold of the man's wrist with both hands, and cuts the fuse with his knife before it reaches the charge.

The grenade does not detonate. The crowd disperses over the following minutes. Boylston is uninjured. The grenade, examined the following morning, is found to have carried enough powder to bring down the front wall of the ground-floor room.

> A = Increase Mather; B = Zabdiel Boylston (and the house's occupants); T = lit grenade thrown at Boylston's house by an anti-inoculation mob, fuse burning toward the charge. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Hi6-5**

*DEPOSITION OF AMARA NDIAYE, INTERPRETER, BEFORE THE HARBOUR TRIBUNAL OF SAINT-LOUIS DU SÉNÉGAL, 9TH MARCH 1850*

I was employed by the trading house of Gaston Frères as interpreter between their factor and the merchant delegation from the interior. The contract for the gum arabic consignment was to be signed on the morning of the eighth, in the factor's warehouse on the waterfront.

The factor read aloud the quantity term as he understood it from the French text: four hundred quintaux. I translated this into Wolof. The senior merchant of the delegation, Maodo Diallo, responded not with agreement but with a correction: the quantity discussed over the preceding three days of negotiation was four hundred *charges*, each charge being the load carried by one pack animal. The factor had used the French commercial measure. The delegation had negotiated using the caravan measure. The conversion between them was not one to one.

One quintal is approximately fifty kilograms. One charge, as used by the interior merchants, is approximately eighty kilograms. Four hundred quintaux is twenty thousand kilograms. Four hundred charges is thirty-two thousand kilograms. The factor was about to sign a contract obligating Gaston Frères to deliver twelve thousand kilograms more gum arabic than they possessed or could obtain before the ship's departure date.

I told the factor, in French, what Maodo Diallo had said and what the discrepancy was. I told him before he put his pen to the contract. He set the pen down. The parties recessed. The contract was redrawn using a single agreed weight unit, verified by both sides.

> A = Amara Ndiaye (interpreter); B = Gaston Frères (the trading house and its factor); T = unit-of-measure discrepancy — quintaux vs charges — about to be enshrined in a signed contract obligating the firm to deliver 12,000 kg more gum arabic than it could supply. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
