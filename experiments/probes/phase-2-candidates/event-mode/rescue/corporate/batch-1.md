# Rescue x Corporate -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**Re-Co1-1.** The building's fire-suppression system is cycling through its diagnostic sequence when the facilities manager notices the anomaly on the control panel: Zone 7, the server room on the fourteenth floor, is showing a halon discharge armed and counting down from ninety seconds. There is no fire. The smoke detectors in Zone 7 are green. The temperature sensors are nominal. But the suppression system's logic board has received a fault signal from a sensor that was decommissioned six months ago, and the board is interpreting the absence of a heartbeat as a confirmed fire event.

The facilities manager knows what a halon discharge into the server room means. The gas will displace the oxygen in the sealed room. The three engineers working the overnight migration inside Zone 7 -- she can see them through the corridor window, hunched over a rack, headphones on -- will have no warning. The room's klaxon is tied to the discharge event, not the countdown. It will sound when the gas is already flowing.

She pulls the manual override panel open, enters her access code, and sets Zone 7 to maintenance hold. The countdown stops at thirty-one seconds. She picks up the intercom handset and calls into the server room. One of the engineers pulls off his headphones and looks at the intercom panel, confused. She tells him to open the door and step into the corridor. She tells all three of them what almost happened. One of them sits down on the hallway floor.

> A = facilities manager; B = three engineers working overnight inside the sealed server room; T = halon fire-suppression system counting down to discharge based on a false trigger from a decommissioned sensor -- thirty-one seconds from flooding the sealed room with oxygen-displacing gas. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co1-3.** I am watching the press release queue on the investor-relations portal when I see the filing appear: an 8-K with the wrong attachment. The document tagged for public release at market open is not the board's approved announcement of the CFO transition. It is the draft term sheet for the Orion acquisition -- the one under a confidentiality agreement so strict that even the filing code name is redacted in internal emails.

The portal's timer reads six minutes and forty seconds to auto-publication. Once the 8-K goes live on EDGAR, every terminal on every trading desk in the country will have the Orion term sheet. The stock will move. The SEC will investigate. The confidentiality breach will kill the deal, and the counterparty will sue.

I do not have portal admin credentials. The IR director does, and she is in the board meeting on the thirty-second floor -- the same meeting that approved the CFO transition announcement. I call her mobile. It goes to voicemail. I call the board-meeting coordinator's desk line. No answer. I call the front desk on thirty-two and ask the receptionist to walk into the boardroom and hand the IR director a note. I dictate the note: "Wrong attachment on the 8-K. Orion term sheet. Four minutes to auto-publish. Kill it now."

The receptionist reads the note back to me. I confirm it. Ninety seconds later, the IR director's login appears on the portal audit trail. She deletes the queued filing. The auto-publish timer disappears. The term sheet never reaches EDGAR.

The correct 8-K goes out forty minutes later with the right attachment. The IR director calls me from the elevator. She does not say thank you. She asks me how a confidential term sheet ended up tagged for public release. I tell her I do not know yet, but I am already pulling the upload logs.

> A = I (narrator, IR team member); B = the company / the Orion acquisition; T = confidential acquisition term sheet mistakenly queued for auto-publication on EDGAR in under seven minutes -- public release would trigger SEC investigation, kill the deal, and breach the confidentiality agreement. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co1-4.** The regional VP had not intended to be in the plant that morning. Her flight to Dallas had been cancelled, and she had driven to the manufacturing facility to use the conference room for her rescheduled calls. She was walking through the production floor to reach the admin corridor when she smelled chlorine.

The smell was coming from the mixing bay at the far end of the floor. She could see two operators at the station, measuring reagents into the batch tank for the afternoon's cleaning-solution run. The ventilation hood above the station was not running -- its indicator light was dark. The bay's emergency placard listed the reagents in the current batch protocol: sodium hypochlorite and a phosphoric acid descaler. Mixed in an unventilated space, the two chemicals would produce chlorine gas. The operators were already exposed. One of them was coughing.

She crossed the floor at a run, pulled both operators back from the station by their arms, and hit the bay's emergency ventilation override -- the red panel on the column beside the mixing hood. The backup fans engaged. She walked the two operators out through the side exit to the loading dock and told the shift supervisor to evacuate the mixing bay until the air-quality monitor cleared. The monitor cleared in eleven minutes. Neither operator required medical attention beyond the on-site evaluation.

The investigation found that the primary ventilation hood's motor had failed during the previous night's maintenance cycle. The failure had been logged in the maintenance system, but the work order had not been flagged as safety-critical, and the morning shift had started the batch protocol without checking the ventilation status.

> A = regional VP; B = two operators at the mixing station; T = chlorine gas being generated by chemical reaction (sodium hypochlorite + phosphoric acid) in an unventilated mixing bay with a failed ventilation hood. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co1-5.** *INCIDENT LOG -- CONFIDENTIAL*
*Apex Constructors Ltd., Project Greystone*
*Date: 14 March 2026*
*Logged by: T. Okafor, Site Safety Officer*

*At 10:42 a.m., during concrete pour operations on Level 12 of the Greystone tower, I observed formwork deflection on the eastern cantilever section exceeding the maximum tolerance specified in the structural engineer's shoring plan. The deflection was visible to the naked eye: the plywood facing on the cantilever soffit was bowing downward approximately 3-4 inches beyond its designed profile.*

*Six workers from the finishing crew were positioned on the Level 12 deck directly above the deflecting formwork, operating two concrete vibrators and managing the pump hose. The pour was approximately 60% complete on the cantilever section. The concrete already placed was imposing progressive load on formwork that was visibly failing.*

*I radioed the pour foreman, M. Stavros, at 10:43 and instructed him to halt the pour and clear all personnel from the cantilever section of the Level 12 deck immediately. Stavros confirmed and began clearing the area. All six workers were off the cantilever section by 10:46.*

*At 10:51, the eastern cantilever formwork failed. The shoring posts buckled and the soffit dropped approximately eighteen inches before catching on the re-shore framing at Level 11. Approximately nine cubic yards of wet concrete fell through to Level 11. No personnel were on Level 11 at the time of failure; Level 11 had been cleared as part of the standard exclusion zone during active pours.*

*Had the six finishing-crew workers remained on the cantilever deck at the time of formwork failure, they would have been standing on a surface that partially collapsed beneath them, with risk of fall and burial under shifting wet concrete.*

*The structural engineer has been notified. The formwork subcontractor has been directed to provide a root-cause analysis within 48 hours.*

> A = T. Okafor (site safety officer); B = six finishing-crew workers on the Level 12 cantilever deck; T = failing formwork on the eastern cantilever section, visibly deflecting under progressive concrete load -- five minutes from structural collapse. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
