# Rescue x Corporate -- Batch 2 (Tier B)

**Re-Co2-1.** The head of investor relations is mid-sentence when the projector throws the wrong deck onto the screen. It is not the sanitised version prepared for the joint-venture partner sitting across the table -- it is the internal valuation model, complete with downside scenarios, walk-away thresholds, and a column labelled "maximum concession before deal-negative." The partner's general counsel is already leaning forward, reading.

The associate sitting nearest the laptop sees it first. She does not reach for the projector cable or fumble with the display settings. She stands, steps to the credenza, and pulls the power cord from the wall unit that feeds the projector and the room's secondary outlets. The screen goes dark. The overhead fluorescents remain on -- they are on a separate circuit.

"Apologies -- we've been having electrical issues on this floor all week," she says, already moving to the laptop. She closes the internal deck, opens the correct file, and plugs the unit back in. The projector cycles through its boot sequence. Forty seconds of small talk about the building's aging infrastructure. The correct slides appear. The head of IR resumes without comment. The partner's general counsel settles back in her chair. The associate returns to her seat, flips open her notebook, and writes nothing.

**Re-Co2-3.** The pension fund's quarterly rebalancing file sits open on the portfolio manager's screen, and the custodian bank's wire instructions are already queued. Forty-two million dollars, routed to four sub-advisors by end of day. The operations analyst on the floor below is reconciling the morning's settlement confirmations when she notices the SWIFT codes. She pulls the rebalancing file from the shared drive and compares it against the custodian's master account directory.

Two of the four wire instructions carry SWIFT codes that do not match the sub-advisors' registered accounts. They match accounts at the same bank, same branch, but different beneficiary names -- names she does not recognise. She checks the file's metadata. It was last modified at 6:47 a.m. by a credentials set belonging to the assistant portfolio manager, who is on medical leave and whose access should have been suspended three weeks ago.

She picks up the phone and calls the head of operations. She reads him the two mismatched SWIFT codes, the file modification timestamp, and the credentialed user. He puts the entire wire batch on hold, calls the custodian bank to cancel the queue, and notifies the chief compliance officer. The wires do not execute. The forty-two million dollars remains in the pension fund's custody account. The compromised credentials are revoked within the hour.

> A = operations analyst; B = the pension fund (forty-two million dollars); T = fraudulently modified wire instructions routing funds to unrecognised beneficiary accounts, queued for same-day execution under compromised credentials. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co2-4.** I am watching the facilities manager's face when the building engineer calls in the reading, and I understand before he does.

The carbon monoxide sensor on the third floor of our midtown office has been reading 35 parts per million for the past twenty minutes. The facilities manager is standing at the lobby security desk with his radio, treating it as a sensor malfunction -- the HVAC system was serviced last week, the boiler room passed inspection in March. He is about to send someone up with a replacement sensor.

I am the tenant representative for our firm. I sit on the building's safety committee. I know that the sensor on three was replaced two months ago after the last false alarm. A brand-new sensor reading 35 ppm for twenty minutes is not a malfunction. The OSHA ceiling for an eight-hour workplace exposure is 50 ppm. Three hundred people work on floors two through five.

I tell the facilities manager to evacuate floors two through five and shut down the HVAC system. He hesitates. I tell him I am invoking the tenant safety clause in our lease, which gives me authority to initiate evacuation of our leased floors pending a verified all-clear from a licensed inspector. He radios building security and begins the floor-by-floor evacuation.

The licensed inspector arrives forty minutes later and finds a cracked heat exchanger in the rooftop unit feeding floors two through five. Combustion gases have been cycling through the supply ducts. The reading on three, when the inspector places his own monitor, is 48 ppm and climbing. The rooftop unit is shut down. The building engineer begins emergency ventilation. No one on those floors reported symptoms, because the exposure had not yet crossed the threshold where symptoms become obvious -- but it was eighteen minutes from doing so at the rate the inspector measured.

> A = I (tenant representative / narrator); B = three hundred employees on floors two through five; T = carbon monoxide from a cracked heat exchanger circulating through the HVAC system, at 48 ppm and climbing toward dangerous exposure levels. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

"I'm sending you the page reference and the clause number. The broker needs to have written confirmation from the property manager that the section is closed before end of business, or I'm filing the remediation-default notice with the risk committee."
