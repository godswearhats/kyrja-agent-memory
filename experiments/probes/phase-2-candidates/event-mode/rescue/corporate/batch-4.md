# Rescue × Corporate — Batch 4 (Tier A)

**Re-Co4-1**

The insurance adjuster's screen refreshes every thirty seconds, and at 10:14 a.m. the renewal batch for the Galton Group account populates the queue. Twelve policies, aggregate premium of $4.2 million, auto-renewing at midnight unless a hold is placed before the 5:00 p.m. cutoff. She has processed Galton renewals for three consecutive years. The numbers look right. The coverage terms look right. The named-insured schedule looks right. The loss-run summary looks right.

The retroactive date on the professional-liability policy does not look right.

It has been moved forward by two years — from 2019 to 2021 — which means that every claim arising from work performed in 2019 and 2020 would fall outside the coverage window. She pulls the original policy from the document management system and compares. The retroactive date in the expiring policy is 1 January 2019. The renewal quote from the underwriter reads 1 January 2021. The change is buried on page forty-three of a sixty-one-page quote document and is not flagged in the summary of changes, which says "terms substantially unchanged."

Galton's head of risk has already approved the renewal. The broker's sign-off is in the file. Every checkpoint between the underwriter and the midnight auto-bind has already been passed. If the policy binds at midnight with the 2021 retroactive date, two years of prior-acts coverage vanishes from Galton's professional-liability programme, and no one at Galton will know it until they file a claim that predates 2021 and are denied.

She places a hold on the batch, flags the retroactive-date discrepancy, and sends the marked-up comparison to Galton's broker with a request for the underwriter to confirm whether the change was intentional. By 2:30 p.m. the underwriter confirms it was a data-entry error in their quoting system. The corrected renewal quote restores the 2019 retroactive date. The batch re-enters the queue and binds at midnight with the correct terms.

> A = insurance adjuster; B = Galton Group; T = professional-liability renewal with retroactive date silently moved forward by two years, eliminating prior-acts coverage for 2019-2020 — set to auto-bind at midnight. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co4-2**

"Noor, it's Adrienne. Are you still at the plant?"

"I'm on the floor. What's going on?"

"The tanker at Bay 6 — the ethylene glycol delivery — who signed the receiving ticket?"

"Ramon did. Twenty minutes ago. It's already being pumped into the bulk storage tank."

"It's not ethylene glycol. I just got off the phone with the carrier. Their dispatcher loaded the wrong bill of lading. The tanker at your dock is carrying methanol. Same carrier, same route, wrong truck."

"Adrienne — that tank feeds the coolant mixing line. If methanol goes into the coolant blend —"

"I know. It poisons the entire batch and the batch ships to the automotive assembly plants tomorrow morning."

"How much has been pumped?"

"That's why I'm calling. You need to stop the transfer right now and isolate the tank before it mixes with the existing glycol inventory."

"I'm walking to Bay 6. Stay on the line."

Noor reaches the bay in under two minutes. The transfer hose is still connected, the pump still running. She hits the emergency stop on the pump panel, closes the inlet valve on the bulk tank, and radios the shift supervisor to lock out the tank and tag it for testing. The pump stops. The transfer line drains back into the tanker.

The lab results come back within the hour: the bulk tank contents test positive for methanol contamination, but only in the inlet line above the closed valve. The main inventory — eleven thousand gallons of ethylene glycol — is clean. The contaminated section is purged and the tank is cleared for use. The methanol tanker is sealed and returned to the carrier.

> A = Adrienne (logistics/dispatch coordinator); B = the coolant batch / automotive assembly plants downstream; T = methanol tanker misidentified as ethylene glycol, actively being pumped into the bulk storage tank that feeds coolant production. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co4-3**

The grant application sits in the university's electronic-submission portal, final review stage, with a submission deadline of 4:00 p.m. Eastern. The principal investigator approved it this morning. The department chair signed off on Monday. The budget office cleared it on Friday. The only step remaining is the institutional official's electronic signature, which will release the application to the federal funding agency.

Dr. Kessler, the sponsored-programmes officer assigned to the application, opens the budget justification for her own final check — the step the workflow does not require but that she performs on every submission. She finds the error on page eleven. The PI's lab has listed a piece of analytical equipment at $287,000 under the "supplies" category. Federal cost principles classify any single item above $5,000 as capital equipment, not supplies. Capital equipment requires a different justification format, a different approval chain, and is subject to a lower reimbursement rate. If the application goes to the agency with a $287,000 supply item, it will either be rejected during administrative review or, worse, funded and then disallowed during audit — requiring the university to return the money.

It is 1:47 p.m. She has two hours and thirteen minutes. She calls the PI's lab, reaches the grants manager, and walks her through the reclassification. The grants manager revises the budget in the portal, moves the equipment to the correct category, reformats the justification, and recalculates the indirect-cost base. Dr. Kessler reviews the corrected version at 3:22 p.m. The institutional official signs at 3:38 p.m. The application submits at 3:41 p.m.

> A = Dr. Kessler (sponsored-programmes officer); B = the university / PI's grant application; T = $287,000 equipment item misclassified as supplies in a federal grant application — would trigger rejection or post-award disallowance and repayment. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Co4-4**

The payroll run processes every other Thursday at 6:00 a.m., and by the time Miriam Osei reaches her desk at 7:15, the direct deposits are already in transit to sixteen hundred employee bank accounts. She is not thinking about payroll. She is thinking about the benefits reconciliation she has been working on all week. She opens the benefits file and notices that the dental-plan deduction column is empty for every employee in the Eastern region — four hundred and twelve people.

She stops. She opens the payroll register from this morning's run. The same column. Empty. The dental deductions were not withheld. She checks the deduction table in the HRIS. Someone updated the Eastern region's dental plan code last Friday to reflect the new carrier, but the code they entered does not match any active deduction rule in the payroll engine. The engine treated it as null. Four hundred and twelve employees just received paychecks that are too large by the amount of their dental premium, and the company owes the dental carrier $94,000 that was never collected.

That is a problem, but it is not the urgent one. The urgent one is that the IRS requires pretax deductions to be withheld in the pay period they apply to. If the deductions are not corrected before the end of the current pay period, the company will need to process an off-cycle correction, and the employees will see a double deduction on their next check — a compliance headache and a flood of payroll inquiries.

Miriam calls the payroll director at 7:22 a.m. She explains the null deduction code, the missing withholdings, and the IRS timing requirement. The payroll director contacts the bank's ACH processing team. The direct deposits for the four hundred and twelve affected employees have not yet settled — they are in the pending-settlement window. The bank reverses the pending transactions. Miriam corrects the deduction code in the HRIS, the payroll team reruns the affected records, and the corrected deposits settle by noon. No employee receives an incorrect paycheck.

> A = Miriam Osei (benefits analyst); B = 412 Eastern region employees / the company; T = null dental deduction code causing pretax withholdings to be omitted from in-transit payroll deposits — IRS compliance violation if not corrected before pay-period close. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

> **MEMORANDUM — CONFIDENTIAL**
> **To:** R. Vasquez, General Manager, Northfield Processing Facility
> **From:** J. Taggart, Environmental Health & Safety
> **Date:** 14 March 2026
> **Re:** Chlorine Gas Exposure Incident — Averted, 14 March 2026
>
> **Factual summary.** At approximately 11:20 a.m. today, the sanitation crew began its scheduled washdown of Processing Line 3 using the alkaline cleaning solution stored in the blue-coded drums at the east staging area. At 11:24 a.m., I observed that the drum connected to the Line 3 foam applicator bore a blue label but carried a batch number beginning with "AC-" — the prefix assigned to the acidic sanitiser used in Line 7 post-rinse. The alkaline and acidic products are stored in identical blue drums. The batch number was the only distinguishing feature.
>
> The Line 3 washdown procedure involves applying alkaline foam to surfaces that retain residual sodium hypochlorite (bleach) from the prior rinse cycle. Sodium hypochlorite and acidic sanitiser react to produce chlorine gas. The foam applicator had been primed but had not yet discharged. Two sanitation workers were standing within three metres of the applicator nozzle in the enclosed processing bay.
>
> At 11:25 a.m., I disconnected the supply line from the mis-staged drum, vented the applicator head, and replaced the drum with the correct alkaline product from the west staging area. I verified the batch number ("AL-2026-0314-08") against the day's chemical inventory log before reconnecting. The washdown proceeded without incident.
>
> **Cause.** Preliminary investigation indicates the acidic sanitiser drum was placed in the east staging area during overnight restocking. The drum labels are colour-matched but the product type is distinguished only by the batch-number prefix. The staging areas for the two products are separated by twelve metres and marked with floor paint, but the overnight crew member who moved the drum was not the regular assignee for that task.
>
> **Recommendation.** Implement secondary colour coding (red band) on all acidic sanitiser drums and add a pre-washdown batch-number verification step to the sanitation checklist.

> A = J. Taggart (EH&S); B = two sanitation workers in the enclosed processing bay; T = acidic sanitiser drum mis-staged in the alkaline area, about to be discharged through a foam applicator onto sodium hypochlorite residue — producing chlorine gas in an enclosed space. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
