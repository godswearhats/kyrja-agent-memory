# Rescue x Sci-fi -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**Re-Sf1-1.** The habitat pressure alarm has not triggered, and that is the problem. Kessler is reading the atmospheric telemetry from Module 7 on her maintenance terminal in the service corridor, and the numbers are wrong: the partial pressure of oxygen inside the module is falling at a rate of 0.3 kPa per minute, but the total pressure reading is holding steady. That means something is displacing the oxygen -- filling the module's volume with a gas the pressure sensors register as equivalent. The CO2 scrubbers would catch carbon dioxide. The nitrogen ballast system would flag a nitrogen overpressure event. The only gas that would displace oxygen without triggering either alarm is argon from the cryogenic storage tanks on the module's lower deck, which vent through a pressure-relief system that feeds, by design, into the station's exterior hull void -- not into the habitable volume.

Unless a relief valve is stuck open and the interior baffle has failed. Which is what the telemetry is telling her.

There are nine people inside Module 7. The oxygen partial pressure is now below 18 kPa and falling. At 16 kPa, cognitive impairment begins. At 14, unconsciousness. None of the nine occupants will notice the change -- argon is odourless and produces no sensation of suffocation. They will simply become confused, then drowsy, then still.

Kessler seals the service-corridor hatch behind her, enters Module 7 through the emergency lock wearing a portable rebreather, and pulls the manual evacuation handle on the module's interior bulkhead. The handle activates the hard-seal partition between Module 7 and the station's central spine, opens the spine-side pressure door, and triggers the audible evacuation tone inside the module. The nine occupants -- three of whom are already visibly sluggish -- move through the pressure door into the central spine. Kessler does a headcount, confirms nine, and seals Module 7 from the spine side. She calls engineering to isolate the argon relief system.

The module vents to safe atmosphere over the next two hours. No one in Module 7 had been aware that anything was wrong.

**Re-Sf1-2.** "Relay, this is Docking Control on Ceres Station. I need you to abort the Pellegrini approach immediately."

"Docking Control, Relay. The Pellegrini is on automated final. She's at two kilometres and closing. What's the problem?"

"The problem is that Bay 11's magnetic grapple array is locked in a test cycle. Maintenance was running a diagnostic and left the array in calibration mode. If the Pellegrini enters the bay's capture envelope, the array will fire a full-power acquisition pulse at a ship that isn't oriented for hard dock. The pulse will torque her lateral thruster assembly past its gimbal limits. At her current mass and velocity, that's a hull-Loss event."

"Control, the Pellegrini's approach is locked. She's on auto. Her crew filed a zero-crew transit manifest -- there's nobody aboard to take manual override."

"I know. That's why I'm calling you. Relay, you have command authority over the approach corridor beacons. I need you to shift Beacon 11-Alpha to emergency standoff frequency. The Pellegrini's autopilot will read the standoff signal and hold position at five hundred metres until she receives a revised docking sequence."

"Shifting 11-Alpha to standoff. Confirm: emergency standoff, all traffic, Bay 11."

"Confirmed."

"Beacon is shifted. Pellegrini's telemetry shows deceleration -- she's responding to the standoff. Holding at five-two-zero metres."

"Copy. I'm sending a maintenance crew to Bay 11 to pull the grapple array out of calibration mode. I'll issue a revised docking sequence once the array is clear. Docking Control out."

**Re-Sf1-3.** I did not see the coronal mass ejection warning until I was already outside the hull.

The EVA was routine -- replacing a degraded thermal-radiator panel on the colony ship's sunward array, a task I had performed eleven times on previous panels during the transit. I was tethered to the array scaffold, helmet visor on automatic polarisation, hands inside the panel housing, when my suit's radiation monitor began climbing. Not the slow creep of background solar flux. A step function. The dosimeter jumped from 0.4 millisieverts per hour to 12 in under ten seconds and was still climbing.

I looked at the ship's hull and saw the warning lights on the emergency airlock cycling to red -- the CME protocol. The ship's radiation shelter is in the core module, behind the water-shield tanks. The crew would already be moving there. But I was outside, sixty metres from the nearest airlock, in a suit whose radiation shielding was rated for ambient solar conditions, not for a direct coronal mass ejection. At the rate the dosimeter was climbing, I had less than four minutes before I exceeded the acute exposure threshold.

Okonkwo's voice came through the EVA channel. She was at the airlock. She had seen the CME alert before I had -- her station in the navigation blister had a direct feed from the solar-monitoring array -- and instead of moving to the radiation shelter with the rest of the crew, she had suited up and come to the airlock. She told me to release the tether and push off the array toward her. I released. I pushed. The sixty metres took ninety seconds in microgravity with no propulsion beyond the initial push and a single correction from my suit's manoeuvring jets. Okonkwo caught my tether clip at the airlock rail, pulled me inside, and cycled the lock. We reached the radiation shelter eighteen seconds before the CME peak flux hit the ship's position.

My final dosimeter reading was 22 millisieverts. Without the shelter, the projection was over 800.

> A = Okonkwo (navigator); B = I (narrator, EVA technician); T = coronal mass ejection, dosimeter climbing toward acute radiation exposure threshold, less than four minutes from lethal dose outside the ship's radiation shielding. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**Re-Sf1-4.** The terraforming station's atmospheric processor draws methane from Titan's surface and cracks it into hydrogen and carbon black. The carbon is pressed into structural bricks. The hydrogen feeds the station's fusion pre-stage. The entire chain runs unattended for months at a time, monitored by the station's environmental AI, and this is the configuration in which Dr. Vasant Rao finds it when he arrives for the quarterly inspection: nominal on every display, green across every status board.

He opens the carbon-black hopper on the processor's output side and finds it empty. The processor has been running for ninety-one days. The hopper should contain eleven tonnes of carbon brick. It is clean. He checks the hydrogen output logs: the fusion pre-stage has received its expected hydrogen feed. The methane input logs confirm normal draw from the surface intakes. The carbon is being produced. It is going somewhere.

He follows the output conduit from the processor to the hopper and finds the diversion. A micro-fracture in the conduit wall, invisible from the outside, has been venting carbon-black particulate into the station's ventilation plenum for the full ninety-one days. The plenum feeds the crew habitation module where the four-person caretaker team lives and sleeps. He pulls the air-quality data from the habitation module's sensors. The sensors read clean. He pulls a sensor unit from the wall and examines it: the sensor's intake mesh is clogged solid with carbon black. It has been reading its own blockage as clean air.

The crew is inside the habitation module. He can see them through the compartment window -- eating, talking, one of them reading. They are breathing carbon-black particulate at a concentration he cannot measure because every sensor in the module is blinded. He seals the ventilation feed from the plenum to the habitation module, cutting off the contaminated air supply and switching the module to its emergency recirculation reserve. Then he enters the module and tells the crew to suit up with respirators. He hands them his own inspection kit's portable air-quality unit. It reads the habitation module's air at nineteen times the safe exposure limit for inhalable particulate.

The crew is evacuated to the inspection shuttle's clean-air cabin within the hour. Medical scans on the return transit show early-stage pulmonary inflammation in all four crew members -- treatable, not yet fibrotic.

> A = Dr. Vasant Rao (quarterly inspector); B = four-person caretaker crew; T = carbon-black particulate contamination venting into the habitation module at nineteen times safe exposure limits through a fractured conduit, with all air-quality sensors blinded by particulate buildup -- progressive pulmonary damage. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
