# Defection × Corporate — Batch 2 (Tier B)

**D-Co2-1**

The compliance dashboard is still open on Priya's screen when she notices the anomaly. The VP of Engineering — Marcus — has his build-pipeline credentials flagged in the access-audit module. Not for overuse. For routing. Three deployments in the past week pushed to Helios Group's staging servers instead of the company's own. Priya pulls the integration config Marcus filed when he onboarded the DevOps team: a signed infrastructure-use policy, paragraph four, stipulating that all proprietary build artifacts remain within company-controlled environments and that no pipeline output may be routed to external infrastructure without written board approval. She cross-references the deployment logs. The Helios staging endpoint is hardcoded into a custom script Marcus authored. The commits are timestamped to after-hours windows — eleven p.m., two a.m., midnight — and each one packages the recommendation-engine binaries the company spent fourteen months developing. The routing is deliberate, repeated, and unmistakable.

> A = Priya / company; B = Marcus (VP of Engineering); P = signed infrastructure-use policy (all proprietary build artifacts must remain in company-controlled environments; no external routing without board approval); V = routing proprietary recommendation-engine binaries to a competitor's staging servers via a custom script; discovery = Priya identifies the anomaly in the compliance dashboard and traces it through deployment logs. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**D-Co2-2**

"I need you to walk me through something," Dana says, closing the door to the small office behind her.

Jonah looks up from his laptop. "Sure."

"The Wexler account. You told me — in the pitch meeting, in front of the whole team — that you'd recuse yourself from any negotiation where your wife's consulting firm had a financial interest. Those were your words."

"I remember."

"Then explain why Lena Wexler's office just sent me a countersigned engagement letter naming your wife's firm as the subcontracted analytics provider. Your signature is on the referral line, Jonah. Dated last Thursday."

He is quiet. Dana sets the letter on the desk between them, the referral signature facing up.

"You referred the subcontract yourself," she says. "While sitting on our side of the table."

> A = Dana (team lead / company); B = Jonah; P = verbal personal undertaking given in a pitch meeting (would recuse himself from any negotiation involving his wife's consulting firm); V = signing a referral directing subcontract work to his wife's firm on the same account; discovery = client's office sends Dana the countersigned engagement letter with Jonah's referral signature. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**D-Co2-4**

She should not be reading the email, but the subject line is her own name, and the laptop belongs to her co-founder, and the screen is angled toward her across the café table while he is in the bathroom. The email is from a partner at Ridgeline Ventures — the firm they both agreed, three weeks ago over this same table, to reject. They had shaken hands on it. No Ridgeline. The terms were predatory, the cap table would be gutted, and they would lose the board seat they needed. That was the conversation. That was the agreement between them: decline Ridgeline, pursue the Altair round together. The email she is reading now is a term sheet. Her co-founder has signed a side letter granting Ridgeline a first-refusal clause on the Series A — binding on the company — without her knowledge, without board authorization, using his signatory power as co-CEO. The effective date is ten days ago.

> A = narrator (co-founder / co-CEO); B = her co-founder (co-CEO); P = mutual verbal agreement to reject Ridgeline Ventures and pursue the Altair round together; V = unilaterally signing a binding side letter granting Ridgeline first-refusal rights on the Series A using co-CEO signatory power; discovery = narrator reads the email and term sheet on her co-founder's open laptop. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**D-Co2-5**

The regional director is thinking about the quarterly safety numbers when the plant manager's report comes through, and the numbers look fine — zero incidents, full certification, all shift inspections logged as completed. It is the photograph that undoes it. A technician on second shift has posted a picture to an industry forum, asking for advice on a wiring configuration, and in the background of the image the safety-interlock panel is visible: three of the four breaker overrides are engaged, the lockout tags are missing, and the date on the whiteboard behind the panel reads yesterday. The regional director opens the plant manager's inspection log for that date. Every field is marked compliant. Every interlock is recorded as sealed. The plant manager's role — the reason the company stationed a credentialed manager on-site — is to perform these inspections personally and certify the results. The photograph shows a facility operating with overrides engaged and no lockout protection on the same day the log certifies full compliance.

> A = regional director / company; B = plant manager; P = role-based institutional duty (personally perform shift inspections and certify safety-interlock compliance); V = certifying full compliance on a date when safety interlocks were overridden and lockout tags were missing; discovery = regional director sees a technician's forum photograph contradicting the inspection log. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states
