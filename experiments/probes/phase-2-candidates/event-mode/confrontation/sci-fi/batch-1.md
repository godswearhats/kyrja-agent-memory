# Confrontation x Sci-fi -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**C-Sf1-1.** The station's atmospheric recycler hums at a pitch that Navigator Desh has learned to ignore, but the silence from Chief Engineer Vasik is harder to tune out. They are standing in the cramped operations bay of Relay Station Kessler-7, a waypoint facility orbiting the gas giant Maren-4, and the display between them shows the transit manifest for the fuel barge *Terrano* -- eighty thousand litres of deuterium slush, bound for the inner colonies. Desh has just told Vasik that she is rerouting the barge to the station's own reserves. Kessler-7's fuel supply dropped below the critical threshold six hours ago when a micrometeorite strike ruptured secondary tank three. Without the *Terrano*'s cargo, the station's orbit-correction thrusters will go dry in nineteen days, and the station will begin an uncontrolled descent into Maren-4's upper atmosphere.

Vasik tells her the barge cannot be rerouted. The deuterium is contracted to the Aurelian Colony Cooperative under a supply agreement administered by the Transit Authority. Diverting contracted fuel without Transit Authority authorisation is a seizure -- it triggers automatic sanctions against the station, including suspension of resupply priority. He tells her to file an emergency requisition through the Authority's distress protocol instead.

Desh says the distress protocol has a fourteen-day processing window. The station has nineteen days of orbital stability. A fourteen-day wait followed by a minimum five-day fuel delivery leaves a margin of zero. She is not gambling the station on a bureaucratic timeline. She tells Vasik to unlock the docking clamps and begin the fuel transfer when the *Terrano* arrives in six hours.

Vasik refuses. He tells her he will not unlock the clamps without Transit Authority clearance. The docking system requires his engineering override code, and he will not enter it. If Desh wants to file the emergency requisition simultaneously and begin the transfer the moment clearance arrives, he will support that. But he will not participate in an unauthorised seizure that puts the station's long-term resupply status at risk to solve a short-term crisis.

Desh pulls up the station's charter on the operations display. Article 14, Section 3: the senior navigation officer has authority to commandeer any vessel or cargo within the station's operational sphere during a declared emergency, provided the emergency is logged in the station's record and countersigned by a second officer. She declares the emergency. She asks Vasik to countersign.

Vasik reads Article 14. He reads it again. Then he enters his override code and countersigns the emergency declaration. The docking clamps are set to receive the *Terrano* on arrival.

> A = Navigator Desh (senior navigation officer, Relay Station Kessler-7); B = Chief Engineer Vasik; O = the deuterium cargo aboard the fuel barge *Terrano* (its diversion to the station's reserves); resolution shape (a) -- Desh prevails; Vasik countersigns the emergency declaration and unlocks the docking clamps after Desh invokes Article 14 of the station charter. All roles, all states.

**C-Sf1-2.** "I need you to hand over the sequencing data, Okonkwo."

"That data belongs to my lab."

"It belongs to the colony. Provision 6 of the Terraform Compact -- all genomic sequencing performed using colony-funded equipment is colony intellectual property. Your centrifuge, your gene printer, your culture vats -- all colony-funded. The data generated on that equipment is ours."

Dr. Lena Okonkwo does not move from her seat behind the lab bench. Across from her, Director Hallam of the Colonial Governance Board stands with a data-transfer chip in his outstretched hand. The lab is buried three levels below the surface of Kepler-442b, carved from basalt and pressurised to 0.9 atmospheres. Outside, the atmosphere is a toxic methane haze. Inside, the lab's bioreactors glow green with engineered algae -- algae designed from the sequencing data Hallam is demanding.

"Provision 6 covers raw sequencing data," Okonkwo says. "What you're asking for includes my annotated sequences, my splice protocols, and my expression models. Those are derivative works. Provision 9 grants the originating researcher exclusive rights to derivative works for a period of five standard years."

"Provision 9 has an exception. Clause 9.4 -- derivative works become colony property immediately if they are designated critical infrastructure by the Governance Board. I designated the algae strain critical infrastructure this morning. The designation is in your message queue."

"On what basis?"

"The algae is the colony's only viable atmospheric processor. If your lab is destroyed -- a rock fall, a pressure breach, a containment failure -- and the sequencing data and splice protocols are lost with it, the colony loses its single pathway to a breathable atmosphere. The Governance Board has a duty to ensure redundancy of critical infrastructure. The data must be copied to the colony's central archive."

Okonkwo considers this. She does not dispute the critical-infrastructure designation -- the algae is, in fact, the colony's only atmospheric processor, and she has argued for redundancy herself. What she disputes is the mechanism.

"Copy the data to the archive. Fine. But access remains restricted to my lab under Provision 9 until the five-year period expires. The archive copy is for catastrophic recovery only -- sealed, encrypted, accessible only if the Governance Board declares a continuity emergency by unanimous vote. Those are my terms."

Hallam looks at the chip in his hand. Sealed and encrypted, accessible only under a unanimity requirement. It would give the Board the redundancy it needs while preventing anyone from using Okonkwo's work to compete with her research.

"Agreed. I'll draft the access protocol and have it ratified at tomorrow's Board session. You transfer the data after ratification."

Okonkwo nods. Hallam pockets the chip and climbs the stairs out of the lab.

> A = Director Hallam (Colonial Governance Board); B = Dr. Lena Okonkwo (lead researcher); O = the genomic sequencing data and derivative splice protocols for the atmospheric-processing algae; resolution shape (b) -- both agree to a negotiated mechanism: data transferred to the colony archive under sealed, encrypted, emergency-only access terms, to be ratified by the full Governance Board before transfer occurs. All roles, all states.

**C-Sf1-3.** The message arrives on Captain Farouk's console at 03:17 ship-time, and it is not a request. It is a directive from the Ceres Maritime Tribunal, transmitted through the deep-system relay network, bearing the Tribunal's cryptographic seal:

> *DIRECTIVE CMT-2247-R. To: Captain Ilya Farouk, commanding vessel* Nightjar*, registry Ceres-0481. The Tribunal orders the immediate surrender of passenger manifest records for transit cycle 71 to the custody of Tribunal Investigator Maren Solberg, currently aboard* Nightjar *in an observer capacity. Failure to comply within 12 hours of receipt constitutes obstruction under the Ceres Spacefaring Code, Section 44.*

Farouk reads the directive twice. Investigator Solberg is standing in the bridge doorway, waiting. The *Nightjar* is a passenger liner, thirty-eight days out from Ceres on a transit to the Jovian stations, carrying four hundred and twelve passengers whose manifest records include biometric identifiers, travel histories, and financial guarantees -- data protected under the Jovian Transit Privacy Accord, which the *Nightjar* is contractually bound to uphold because its passengers purchased tickets under Jovian-jurisdiction terms.

Farouk tells Solberg that he cannot comply with the directive. The manifest records are governed by the Jovian Transit Privacy Accord, Article 11, which prohibits the release of passenger biometric and financial data to any governmental body without individual passenger consent or a Jovian court order. A Ceres Tribunal directive does not satisfy Article 11 -- Ceres and Jupiter operate under separate legal frameworks, and the *Nightjar* is currently in interplanetary transit, outside Ceres' jurisdictional sphere.

Solberg says the *Nightjar* is Ceres-registered. Registration binds the vessel to Ceres law regardless of its physical location. Article 11 of the Jovian Accord is a contractual obligation to passengers, not a jurisdictional shield against lawful Ceres process. She tells Farouk that she is required to report non-compliance to the Tribunal within twelve hours, and that the Tribunal has the authority to revoke the *Nightjar*'s Ceres registration, which would bar the ship from docking at any Ceres-affiliated port.

Farouk says that releasing the data without Jovian authorisation would expose the shipping line to passenger lawsuits under Article 11 and potential revocation of its Jovian operating licence. He is caught between two jurisdictions issuing incompatible demands.

He proposes a referral. The Interplanetary Commerce Court at Vesta has standing jurisdiction over conflicts between Ceres and Jovian legal instruments. He will transmit both the Tribunal's directive and the relevant provisions of the Jovian Accord to the Commerce Court and request an expedited ruling. The manifest records will remain on the *Nightjar*'s secured server, unmodified and undeleted, pending the court's decision. If the Commerce Court rules that the Tribunal's directive takes precedence, he will release the records immediately.

Solberg considers the timeline. The *Nightjar* is eighteen days from the Jovian stations. The Commerce Court typically rules within ten days on expedited filings. She tells Farouk she will accept the referral on one condition: the *Nightjar*'s secured server is placed under a joint cryptographic lock -- her key and his -- so that neither party can modify or delete the records while the ruling is pending.

Farouk agrees. They configure the joint lock on the bridge console before Solberg returns to her quarters to draft her report to the Tribunal.

> A = Investigator Maren Solberg (acting on behalf of the Ceres Maritime Tribunal); B = Captain Ilya Farouk (commanding the *Nightjar*); O = the passenger manifest records for transit cycle 71; resolution shape (b) -- both defer to the Interplanetary Commerce Court at Vesta for a binding ruling on the jurisdictional conflict, with records held under joint cryptographic lock pending the decision. All roles, all states.

**C-Sf1-4.** I am the gene-print auditor for the Ninth District on Callisto, and this morning I walked into the Promethan BioWorks facility to shut down their production line. The line manufactures synthetic neural tissue -- sheets of lab-grown neurons used in prosthetic implants across the Jovian settlements. Three weeks ago, a routine audit of their gene-print templates revealed a deviation: the template they are using to grow the tissue contains a proprietary enhancement -- a modified ion-channel gene sequence -- that is not present in the approved template on file with the District Regulatory Office. The modified sequence was never submitted for review. It was never approved. It has been printing into the neural tissue of every implant that left this facility for the past eleven months.

I found the facility director, Dr. Yael Fenn, in her office above the production floor. I told her the production line must halt immediately. Under the Callisto Biologics Code, Section 22, any gene-print template that deviates from the approved filing is considered an unregulated biological product. The facility's production licence is suspended until the modified template is submitted for full review, which includes a sixty-day safety assessment.

Fenn told me she would not halt the line. The ion-channel modification, she said, was an improvement -- it increased signal fidelity in the neural tissue by forty percent. The eleven months of implant data supported this. No adverse events had been reported. Shutting down the line would cut off supply to six Jovian hospitals that depend on Promethan as their sole neural-tissue provider. Patients waiting for implants would be harmed by the shutdown more than they had been harmed by the modification, which is to say not at all.

I told her the absence of reported adverse events did not constitute safety data. The modification bypassed the review process that exists precisely to evaluate whether unreported effects are accumulating. Section 22 does not contain a retroactive-safety exception. The line shuts down.

Fenn told me that if I suspended the licence, she would file an emergency appeal with the Jovian Biologics Board and request a temporary operating injunction allowing the line to continue during the sixty-day review. She said the Board had granted such injunctions twice before for sole-source suppliers.

I told her she was welcome to file. The suspension takes effect now. If the Board grants an injunction, the line restarts under the Board's authority, not mine. Until then, the production floor goes dark.

I placed the suspension order on her desk. She signed the acknowledgment, then picked up her console and began drafting the appeal. The production line fell silent before I reached the elevator.

> A = I (narrator / gene-print auditor, Ninth District, Callisto); B = Dr. Yael Fenn (facility director, Promethan BioWorks); O = the synthetic neural-tissue production line (its continued operation with the unapproved modified template); resolution shape (a) -- auditor prevails; production line is shut down immediately, though Fenn signals intent to seek an emergency injunction from the Jovian Biologics Board. All roles, all states.

**C-Sf1-5.** The hydroponic bay stretches the full length of Deck Nine, and Lira Tan thinks it is the most important room on the generation ship *Perseverance*. Twelve thousand soybean plants grow under full-spectrum panels, producing the protein base for sixty percent of the ship's food supply. The *Perseverance* is ninety-two years into a hundred-and-forty-year transit to Gliese 667C, and Lira is the fourth Chief Botanist to tend these plants since departure. She knows every cultivar, every nutrient cycle, every stress indicator the soybeans produce when something is wrong. And something is wrong now -- not with the plants, but with what the ship's Resource Allocation Council wants to do with them.

Councillor Brenn steps through the bay's pressure door and tells Lira that the Council has voted to repurpose forty percent of the hydroponic bay's growing area for an experimental biofuel crop. The ship's fusion reactor is degrading faster than projected. The engineering team has proposed a biological backup -- an algae-based fuel supplement that could extend the reactor's operational life by thirty years, well past arrival. The algae requires the same light spectrum, the same nutrient solution, and the same growing volume that the soybeans currently occupy. The Council voted six to one in favour. The refit begins next week.

Lira tells Brenn the refit will not happen. Removing forty percent of the soybean crop reduces the ship's protein output below the minimum nutritional threshold for the population. She pulls up the agricultural model on the bay's central display: at sixty percent of current growing capacity, soybean yield drops to a level that supports only seventy-one percent of the ship's caloric protein requirement. The remaining twenty-nine percent has no substitute -- the other food systems produce carbohydrates and vitamins, not complete protein. Within eighteen months of the refit, the ship will face a protein deficit that cannot be corrected without reducing the population's caloric intake below the medical minimum defined in the Ship's Charter, Article 31.

Brenn says the engineering projections are equally dire. Without the biofuel supplement, the reactor fails in forty-six years. The ship is forty-eight years from arrival. A two-year power gap at interstellar velocities means the loss of life support, navigation, and deceleration capability. Everyone dies. The protein deficit is manageable through rationing; a reactor failure is not.

Lira says rationing below the Article 31 threshold requires a population-wide referendum under the Charter's health-and-welfare provisions. The Council cannot impose it by a six-to-one vote. She demands that the refit be suspended until the referendum is held. If the population votes to accept the protein reduction, she will cooperate with the refit. If they do not, the Council must find another solution for the reactor.

Brenn tells her the Council has the authority to act on engineering emergencies without a referendum under Article 7, the emergency-powers provision.

Lira says Article 7 does not override Article 31. Article 31 was written specifically to prevent the Council from making unilateral decisions that reduce the population's nutritional baseline. The two articles were drafted together, and Article 31 contains an explicit clause stating that its protections survive any invocation of Article 7.

Brenn is quiet for a long moment, standing among the soybean plants. Then he tells Lira the Council will table the refit and schedule the referendum for the next civic assembly, fourteen days from now. The hydroponic bay remains untouched until the vote.

> A = Councillor Brenn (Resource Allocation Council); B = Lira Tan (Chief Botanist); O = forty percent of the hydroponic bay's growing area (its repurposing from soybeans to biofuel algae); resolution shape (a) -- Lira prevails; Brenn tables the refit and agrees to hold a population-wide referendum before any changes to the hydroponic bay. All roles, all states.
