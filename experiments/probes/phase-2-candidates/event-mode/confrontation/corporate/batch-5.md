## C-Co5-2

The safety officer walks onto the construction floor at eleven with the incident report from last night's shift still warm from the printer. A steel beam dropped eighteen inches during a crane repositioning. No injuries. But the crane's load sensor had been overridden -- someone had bypassed the automatic stop to lift a load fourteen percent above the rated capacity. The override required a supervisor's authorization code, and the shift log shows one name: Brannigan.

She finds Brannigan in the site office reviewing the next pour schedule. She tells him the crane is grounded, effective immediately, pending a full mechanical inspection and recertification of the load sensor. She tells him his supervisor override privileges are suspended until the investigation concludes. She tells him the override violated three provisions of the site safety plan that he signed during onboarding.

Brannigan tells her the beam had to move last night. The concrete pour was scheduled for six this morning. If the beam hadn't been repositioned, the pour would have been delayed by two days. A two-day delay on the critical path costs the project eighty thousand dollars a day. He made a field judgment.

She tells him field judgment does not extend to overriding automatic safety stops on lifting equipment. The site safety plan is not advisory. It is contractually binding, incorporated by reference into the general contractor's agreement with the owner. A violation exposes the company to liquidated damages under the owner's safety clause -- damages that dwarf the cost of a delayed pour.

He asks her to limit the suspension to the crane and leave his override privileges intact for other equipment. She refuses. The suspension is site-wide. He stares at the pour schedule, then picks up the radio and calls the crane operator. "She's grounded," he says. "Reschedule the pour for Thursday."

> A = safety officer; B = Brannigan (site supervisor); O = crane operations and Brannigan's supervisor override privileges; resolution shape (a) -- safety officer prevails; Brannigan capitulates and reschedules the pour. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Co5-3

I run the clinical data management group for a mid-size pharmaceutical company, and at nine this morning the biostatistics lead, Wen, walked into my office and told me she was pulling her team's validation sign-off from our Phase III submission dataset. The sign-off had been given two weeks earlier. The submission was due at the regulatory agency in six days.

I asked her why. She said her team had identified a discrepancy in the randomization table -- seventeen patients in the treatment arm had been assigned sequence numbers that did not match the randomization envelope log held by the independent pharmacy. The discrepancy did not change the primary endpoint result, but it meant the audit trail was broken for those seventeen records. She could not certify the dataset as submission-ready with a broken audit trail.

I told her the discrepancy was a documentation gap, not a data integrity issue. The patients received the correct treatment. The envelopes confirmed it. The mismatch was a numbering error introduced during migration from the old electronic data capture system.

She said she understood my interpretation. Her position was unchanged. A broken audit trail is a broken audit trail regardless of the underlying cause, and her signature on the validation report represented her professional certification that the dataset was traceable from source to submission. She would not certify what she could not trace.

I asked her what it would take. She said a reconciliation of all seventeen records against the pharmacy envelopes, documented in a formal amendment to the validation report, reviewed by the independent data monitoring committee. She estimated four weeks.

Four weeks would miss the submission window. I told her we would escalate to the chief medical officer for a risk-benefit determination on submitting with the discrepancy disclosed but unreconciled. She agreed to present her findings to the CMO that afternoon. The dataset remains unsigned pending the CMO's decision.

> A = narrator (clinical data management director); B = Wen (biostatistics lead); O = the validation sign-off on the Phase III submission dataset; resolution shape (b) -- both defer to the chief medical officer for a risk-benefit determination, meeting scheduled for that afternoon. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Co5-4

Rennick thinks the resignation letter changes everything, but the chair of the compensation committee is already explaining why it does not. They are in the boardroom. The letter is on the table between them -- Rennick's letter, tendered forty minutes ago, effective in thirty days. He wants to leave. He wants the accelerated vesting of his equity, as specified under the separation clause of his employment agreement for voluntary departure without cause.

The chair tells him the accelerated vesting will not be approved. The committee reviewed the separation clause that morning after receiving his letter. The clause requires that the departing executive not be under active investigation at the time of resignation. Rennick is under active investigation. The audit committee opened a review of his division's revenue recognition practices eleven days ago. He was notified. He signed the acknowledgment.

Rennick says the investigation is a fishing expedition. No findings have been issued. No determination of fault. The clause says "under investigation for cause," and no cause has been established. His lawyers read the language the same way.

The chair says the committee's outside counsel reads it differently. "Under investigation" is the trigger, not "under investigation with findings." The acknowledgment Rennick signed confirms the investigation is active. The clause is clear.

Rennick says if the vesting is denied, he will withdraw the resignation and force the company to terminate him -- at which point the termination-without-cause clause triggers a severance package worth three times what the equity is worth.

The chair looks at him for a long time. Then she tells him the board will accept his resignation on the terms offered or decline to accept it and place him on administrative leave pending the audit committee's findings. Those are the two options. She asks which he prefers.

Rennick picks up the letter from the table, folds it, and puts it in his jacket pocket. "I'll be at my desk," he says. The chair notes for the record that the resignation has been withdrawn.

> A = chair of the compensation committee; B = Rennick (divisional president); O = accelerated equity vesting under the separation clause; resolution shape (a) -- chair prevails; Rennick withdraws his resignation rather than accept the terms, equity remains unvested. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
