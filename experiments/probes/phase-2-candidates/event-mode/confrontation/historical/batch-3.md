# Confrontation × Historical — Batch 3 (Tier C)

## C-Hi3-1

*[Florence, 1497. Guild system. Third-person present tense.]*

The wool-guild's inspector arrives at the dyeing house on a Tuesday, unannounced. He walks the length of the vat room with a square of reference cloth in his hand — the standard for scarlet that the Arte della Lana has maintained since the previous century — and holds it against the bolts stacked along the east wall. The colour is off. Not dramatically, not visibly to an untrained eye, but off: the mordant concentration is low and the hue will fade within a season. He knows it and the dye-master, Andreotti, standing at the far end of the room, knows he knows it.

The inspector tells Andreotti he will not mark the consignment for export. Forty bolts, bound for the Lyon fair. He tells him the consignment must be re-dyed or remortdanted before it can bear the guild's certification seal, and that the certification is not optional — Lyon merchants have the right to reject uncertified Florentine scarlet, and any rejection will return as a demerit against the Arte's collective mark.

Andreotti says the mordant is within the acceptable range as he reads the guild's standard. The standard specifies a concentration of alum and tartar, not a ratio; his concentrations meet the letter of the specification. He shows the inspector his own records, the daily log of each batch, the measurements in his hand.

The inspector does not disagree that the numbers meet the letter. He says the standard was written to achieve a result and the result is not achieved. He pulls the reference cloth out again and sets it against the bolt. He asks Andreotti whether he would sell that bolt to his own mother.

The argument goes nowhere. Andreotti demands the matter go before the guild's master assessors, whose panel has final say on certification disputes. The inspector agrees. He marks the consignment as held pending panel review and seals the warehouse with the guild's wax. The panel convenes two days later and finds in the inspector's favour. The bolts are re-dyed before the Lyon fair.

> A = wool-guild inspector; B = Andreotti (dye-master); O = the export certification for forty bolts of scarlet cloth; resolution shape (b) — deferred to the guild's master assessors panel; panel finds against Andreotti, bolts re-dyed. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi3-2

*[Virginia Colony, 1675. Tobacco economy. Dialogue-driven.]*

"I want the indenture extended twelve months."

Patience did not move from the doorway. "My term ends in the spring. The contract says spring."

"The contract says four years from date of arrival. You arrived in November. Spring is five months, not four years."

"I arrived in November and I began work the first week of March. The indenture runs from first work. Mr. Hapworth told me so when I signed."

"Hapworth is dead. The indenture is mine now. The deed of transfer was recorded at the county court in August. I hold the original contract, and the original contract says date of arrival."

"Then I want to see the original."

He crossed to the chest by the window and took out a paper. She read it in the light from the door. The date of arrival was entered in a different ink from the rest of the document.

"This has been altered."

"It has not been altered. That is the original entry."

"The ink is different."

"The ink is from a different pen. Hapworth used two pens. The court recorded this document as presented. If you believe it was altered, you may petition the county magistrate."

"I will. I am telling you now that I will not work past the spring. If you attempt to hold me past spring, I will go to the magistrate with what I have just seen."

"You have seen nothing."

"I have seen a document with two different inks. The magistrate will see it too." She stepped back from the doorway. "Spring."

He filed the petition before she could. The magistrate heard both parties two weeks later. He ruled the contract as originally written was ambiguous and ordered it construed against the drafter. Patience's indenture ended in April.

> A = Patience (indentured servant); B = plantation owner; O = Patience's indenture term (spring release vs. twelve-month extension); resolution shape (a) — Patience prevails; magistrate construes ambiguous contract against the drafter, indenture ends in spring. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi3-3

*[Ottoman Istanbul, 1720s. Imperial household administration. First-person narration.]*

I had served as keeper of the household accounts for the Pasha for eleven years, and I knew the difference between a disbursement that had been authorised and one that had been constructed to appear so after the fact. The payment in question — four hundred and sixty kurush, recorded as a procurement of lamp oil and tallow for the outer kitchens — had no corresponding delivery record, no signature from the kitchen steward, and no entry in the supplier's ledger that I had obtained, discreetly, through the factor who handled all the Pasha's household accounts.

I brought the discrepancy to Kemal Efendi, who oversaw the outer household staff, and I told him directly: the payment was not supported and I required him to produce the delivery record, the steward's signature, and the supplier's receipt before I would certify the quarterly accounts. I was not asking him what had happened. I was telling him what I needed in order to proceed.

He told me the records had been lost in a reorganisation of the storeroom archives. He said the procurement had been properly conducted and that my insistence on documentation for a minor kitchen expense was an insult to his position and his years of service. He told me to certify the accounts as presented.

I told him I would not. I told him I would present the accounts to the Pasha's chief steward — not to the Pasha himself, but to the chief steward, which was the proper channel for unresolved disputes within the household administration — with a notation that the payment was unverified pending documentation. He could produce the records at any time before the steward's review and I would certify without further question.

He produced nothing. I presented the accounts with the notation. The chief steward conducted his own inquiry. The money was recovered from Kemal Efendi's private account within the month. He was dismissed from the household.

> A = I (narrator / household accounts keeper); B = Kemal Efendi (outer household overseer); O = the unsupported payment of 460 kurush in the quarterly accounts (its certification); resolution shape (a) — accounts keeper prevails; chief steward's inquiry recovers the money and Kemal Efendi is dismissed. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi3-4

*[Philadelphia, 1838. Abolitionist press. Embedded document.]*

---

*Office of the Pennsylvania Anti-Slavery Society, Fifth Street*
*To: Mr. Robert Purvis, President*
*From: Hezekiah Nourse, Printer*
*This 14th day of March, 1838*

Mr. Purvis,

I write to inform you that I am unable to continue the printing of the *National Enquirer* at the rate of twelve dollars per thousand sheets as we have heretofore agreed. The paper and ink suppliers with whom I contract have raised their prices in consequence of the threats visited upon establishments known to supply abolitionist publications. Two of my suppliers have declined to renew their contracts entirely. I require a new rate of eighteen dollars per thousand sheets to continue, effective with the April issue.

*H. Nourse*

---

*To: Mr. Hezekiah Nourse*
*From: Robert Purvis, President, Pennsylvania Anti-Slavery Society*
*This 16th day of March, 1838*

Mr. Nourse,

Our agreement runs through December of this year at the rates stated therein. A fifty-percent increase is not a modification I am able to accept mid-term, and I must respectfully decline it. The Society's means are not such that we may absorb such a change without prior arrangement.

If your costs have genuinely increased to the degree you describe, I am prepared to meet with you to review your supplier accounts in detail and to discuss a modest adjustment, not to exceed four dollars per thousand sheets, to be applied beginning with the June issue upon documentation of the increased costs. I am not prepared to accept eighteen dollars on the basis of a letter alone.

I remain willing to honour the existing agreement at twelve dollars through its natural term.

*R. Purvis*

---

*To: Mr. Robert Purvis*
*From: Hezekiah Nourse*
*This 18th day of March, 1838*

Mr. Purvis,

I regret that I cannot continue at twelve dollars. The April issue cannot proceed at that rate. If the Society cannot meet eighteen, I must terminate our arrangement as of this date and return the press time to other clients. I will return the paper stock already delivered to your offices within the week.

*H. Nourse*

---

Purvis secured a new printer, Merrihew & Gunn, within ten days, at a rate of thirteen dollars and fifty cents per thousand sheets, and the April issue appeared on schedule.

> A = Hezekiah Nourse (printer); B = Robert Purvis (Society president); O = the printing contract for the *National Enquirer* (its mid-term renegotiation to eighteen dollars per thousand); resolution shape (c) — breaking point; Nourse terminates the arrangement when Purvis declines eighteen dollars, Society engages a new printer. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi3-5

*[Nagasaki, 1855. Meiji-era Japan, Dutch trade post. Free indirect discourse.]*

The Dutch factor, Hendriks, had been in Dejima long enough to know that patience was a form of currency on the island, and he was running short of both. The delivery of the consignment of optical instruments — twelve transit telescopes ordered by the Shogunate's astronomical bureau and paid for in full eight months prior — had been held at the Nagasaki customs house for six weeks. The reason, when the customs superintendent finally agreed to meet with him, turned out to be the instruments' secondary function. Transit telescopes, the superintendent explained, could also serve as ranging instruments for military surveying. The Shogunate's standing orders on foreign instruments with military applications required an additional review by the Bureau of Military Affairs before customs could release them.

Hendriks produced the original order, countersigned by the Shogunate's own astronomical bureau. Military function was not a concern when the order was placed, he observed — the instruments had been commissioned by a branch of the Shogunate itself. The hold was the Shogunate reviewing a purchase it had made of itself.

The superintendent agreed that this was, perhaps, an irregularity. He did not, however, have authority to release goods flagged by the Bureau of Military Affairs. He had the authority to forward a written representation from the foreign factor to the Bureau, with a recommendation from his own office attached.

Hendriks was not pleased, but he recognized the shape of what was available. He prepared the written representation, Hendriks to the Bureau of Military Affairs via the customs superintendent, detailing the astronomical bureau's original order, the pre-payment, and the absence of any military purpose in the commission. The superintendent added his recommendation in the approved form. The Bureau released the instruments eighteen days later.

> A = Hendriks (Dutch trading factor); B = Nagasaki customs superintendent; O = the consignment of twelve transit telescopes held at customs; resolution shape (b) — deferred to the Bureau of Military Affairs via formal representation; Bureau releases the instruments after review. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
