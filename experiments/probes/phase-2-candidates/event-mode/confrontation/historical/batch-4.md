# Confrontation × Historical — Batch 4 (Tier A)

## C-Hi4-1

The censor arrives at the printing house on the Rue Saint-Jacques at midday, accompanied by two officers of the Parisian book trade police. It is the autumn of 1757. He carries a lettre de cachet authorising the seizure of all unsold copies of a work listed on the warrant as *Pensées sur l'interprétation de la nature*, along with the copper plates from which it was printed. The work is already on the Index. The censor's office has determined that the edition produced at this shop contains additional material not present in the version submitted for approval — two chapters inserted after the censorship review, neither bearing the royal privilege.

The master printer, Brullé, meets him in the composing room. The censor tells him to produce the plates and the remaining stock.

Brullé tells him the plates were melted three days ago. He shows the censor the foundry receipt — a document from Gauthier's type foundry on the Quai des Augustins, dated and stamped, recording the delivery of fourteen kilograms of scrap copper. The unsold copies, he says, were returned to the author. He has none in his possession. His shop holds nothing covered by the warrant.

The censor tells him the foundry receipt does not satisfy the warrant. The warrant requires the plates or a sworn declaration under oath explaining their absence, witnessed by a notary. A commercial receipt from a foundry is not a declaration. He tells Brullé to produce the sworn declaration within twenty-four hours or the censor's office will refer the matter to the Lieutenant General of Police, who will order a full search of the premises and an interrogation under the guild's disciplinary authority.

Brullé tells the censor he will produce the declaration. He tells him further that the two inserted chapters were set from manuscript delivered after the privilege was granted and that the responsibility for their inclusion lies with the bookseller who commissioned the edition, not the printer who set the type. The guild's regulations distinguish between the printer's mechanical role and the bookseller's editorial authority. If the censor wishes to pursue a case, the bookseller is the proper party.

The censor says the warrant names the printing house, not the bookseller, and the printing house will answer for what was printed in it. He leaves the officers at the door to observe the premises until the declaration is filed. Brullé has it notarised and delivered by the following morning. The censor's office accepts the declaration and transfers the investigation to the bookseller.

> A = censor (royal book trade police); B = Brullé (master printer); O = the copper plates and unsold stock of the unauthorized edition; resolution shape (a) — censor prevails; Brullé produces the sworn declaration, investigation transfers to the bookseller. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi4-2

"You will return the runaway to me, Sheriff. The law is plain."

Daniel Wilkes stands in the anteroom of the county courthouse in Christiana, Pennsylvania, in September of 1851, holding a certificate of ownership recorded in the state of Maryland. The sheriff, a heavyset man named Proudfoot, sits behind his desk with both hands flat on the surface.

"I will not."

"The Fugitive Slave Act requires you to assist in the recovery. You are a federal officer for purposes of this statute."

"I am a county sheriff. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania passed a personal liberty law in 1847 that prohibits state officers from assisting in the seizure of any person claimed as a fugitive. I am a state officer. I will not assist."

"The federal statute supersedes."

"That is a question for a federal judge, not for me. I am telling you what I will do. I will not lay hands on any person in this county on the strength of a Maryland certificate."

"If you will not act, I demand you produce the man. He was seen in the settlement north of Christiana two days ago. You know where he is."

"I know nothing of the sort. And if I did, the personal liberty law forbids me from disclosing the location of any person claimed as a fugitive."

"Then I will find him myself and bring federal marshals."

"Bring whomever you wish. The people of this county are not required to cooperate with you, and I will not order them to."

Wilkes leaves. He returns three days later with a federal marshal and a posse of men. What happens next is not part of this scene. What is part of this scene is Sheriff Proudfoot, still sitting at his desk, refusing to move.

> A = Daniel Wilkes (slaveholder from Maryland); B = Sheriff Proudfoot (county sheriff, Christiana, Pennsylvania); O = the return of the fugitive (his seizure and recovery under the Fugitive Slave Act); resolution shape (c) — breaking point; Proudfoot flatly refuses to act, Wilkes departs to seek federal enforcement, relationship between state and federal authority severed at the county level. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi4-3

I am the warden of the leper hospital of Saint Mary Magdalene, outside the walls of Norwich, and it is the year of our Lord 1348. The Bishop's almoner has come to me this morning and told me that the Bishop requires the hospital to receive forty new patients from the city — men and women taken ill with the pestilence that has been moving through East Anglia since Michaelmas.

I told him the hospital cannot receive them. Our foundation charter, granted by the Bishop's predecessor, establishes this house for the care of lepers and lepers alone. The pestilence is not leprosy. The charter specifies the affliction, the number of beds — twenty-six — and the income to support them. We have twenty-two patients. Our stores are calculated for twenty-six.

He told me the Bishop's authority supersedes the charter in times of extraordinary need, and that the pestilence constitutes such a time. He showed me a letter in the Bishop's hand invoking the canonical doctrine of necessitas non habet legem — necessity knows no law.

I told him the doctrine is invoked at the Bishop's discretion, but it does not compel me to destroy the house I am sworn to maintain. Forty additional patients with a different disease, housed among lepers, will consume our stores in a fortnight and expose our existing patients to the new sickness. I am their warden. I took an oath to protect them. I will not admit the forty.

He asked me what I proposed.

I proposed that the hospital's outer barn, which stands apart from the wards, be converted to a temporary sick-house for the pestilence patients, with its own stores and its own attendants, funded from the Bishop's own charitable revenues — not from the hospital's endowment. I told the almoner I would open the barn and manage the conversion, but only under a written agreement that the hospital's charter patients and stores remain untouched.

He said he would put this to the Bishop. The Bishop agreed, with modifications — he provided the stores but required the hospital to supply three attendants. I supplied them. The barn opened within two days.

> A = Bishop's almoner (acting for the Bishop of Norwich); B = I (narrator / warden of the leper hospital); O = the admission of forty pestilence patients to the leper hospital; resolution shape (a) — warden prevails in substance; compromise reached preserving the charter patients and stores, with the barn as a separate facility. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi4-4

The light in the mapmaker's workshop comes from a single high window facing north, and it falls across the table where the finished plate for the new chart of the Moluccan passage sits in its acid bath, nearly complete. Pieter van Doorn, master engraver for the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, has been working on it for five months. It is the most detailed chart of the passage yet attempted, compiled from the sailing reports of six separate voyages, and it will be printed under the VOC's official cartouche.

The Heren XVII's commercial secretary, Dekker, enters the workshop without knocking. He places a written instruction on the table beside the acid bath. The instruction directs van Doorn to alter the chart before printing: the depth soundings along the northern approach are to be increased by two fathoms, and the reef marked at the channel's midpoint is to be shifted half a league to the east. The purpose, the instruction states, is to protect the Company's navigational advantage by introducing deliberate inaccuracies that will mislead competing traders who obtain copies of the chart.

Van Doorn reads the instruction. He tells Dekker he will not alter the chart. The guild oath he swore upon receiving his master's certification obliges him to produce accurate charts. Deliberate falsification of navigational data is a violation of the oath and, under the city's guild statutes, grounds for revocation of his certification and expulsion from the Engravers' Guild. He will not risk his livelihood and his standing in the guild to protect the Company's commercial interests.

Dekker tells him the Company's authority over its commissioned works supersedes the guild's authority over the craftsman's methods. The chart was commissioned by the VOC. The VOC owns the data. The VOC directs how the data is presented.

Van Doorn tells him the Company may own the data, but it does not own his guild certification. If Dekker wants a falsified chart, he may find another engraver willing to make one. Van Doorn will deliver the accurate plate as contracted, and if the Company rejects it, he will return the commission fee and retain the plate as his own work under guild custom, which grants the engraver ownership of rejected commissions.

Dekker takes the instruction back. He tells van Doorn the Company will find another engraver. Van Doorn finishes the plate and delivers it. The Company pays for it but does not print it. The falsified version is engraved elsewhere.

> A = Dekker (VOC commercial secretary); B = Pieter van Doorn (master engraver); O = the Moluccan passage chart (its deliberate falsification before printing); resolution shape (c) — breaking point; van Doorn refuses to alter the chart, Company accepts the accurate plate but commissions a falsified version from another engraver. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi4-5

*Proceedings of the Worshipful Company of Apothecaries, London, 11th day of February, 1704.*

Before the Court of Assistants this day appeared Mr. Thomas Harwick, Liveryman of this Company, presenting a complaint against the College of Physicians in the person of Dr. James Gardiner, Censor of the College.

Mr. Harwick states that Dr. Gardiner entered his shop on Cheapside on the 8th inst. and demanded he cease compounding and dispensing a preparation of Peruvian bark which Mr. Harwick sells under his own label for the treatment of intermittent fevers. Dr. Gardiner stated that Mr. Harwick's preparation constituted the practice of physick, as it was sold with written directions specifying dosage and indication, and that prescribing dosage and indication is reserved to the College of Physicians under its Royal Charter.

Mr. Harwick refused. He stated to Dr. Gardiner that the Apothecaries' charter of 1617 grants members of this Company the right to compound and dispense medicines, and that written directions for dosage are customary and necessary to the safe dispensing of any preparation. He stated that the distinction between dispensing with directions and prescribing is well established in the practice of this Company and that Dr. Gardiner's interpretation would make every apothecary in London a trespasser upon the Physicians' privilege.

Dr. Gardiner, being invited to respond by letter, writes that the distinction between dispensing and prescribing collapses when the apothecary specifies the disease for which the preparation is indicated. An apothecary may compound and label; he may not diagnose and direct. The written directions on Mr. Harwick's label name the disease. This is diagnosis.

The Court of Assistants, having considered the complaint and the Censor's letter, finds the matter to exceed the jurisdiction of this Court and refers it to arbitration by a joint committee of three members of this Company and three nominees of the College, as provided for in the agreement of 1701 between the two bodies. Both parties are directed to submit their evidence to the joint committee within fourteen days.

So ordered.

> A = Dr. James Gardiner (Censor of the College of Physicians); B = Thomas Harwick (Liveryman of the Worshipful Company of Apothecaries); O = Harwick's Peruvian bark preparation with written dosage directions (its continued sale); resolution shape (b) — deferred to a joint arbitration committee of the Apothecaries' Company and the College of Physicians under the 1701 agreement. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
