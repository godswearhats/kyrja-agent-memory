# Confrontation x Historical -- Batch 2 (Tier B)

**C-Hi2-1.** The abbess receives the bishop's secretary in the parlour at the hour of terce. He carries a letter bearing the episcopal seal. She reads it in his presence. The bishop requires the abbey to surrender its copy of the Chronica Universalis -- the illustrated manuscript commissioned by the abbey's foundress eighty years ago -- to the cathedral library, where it will be incorporated into the diocesan collection. The letter cites the bishop's visitatorial authority over all religious houses in the diocese and his right to redistribute their possessions for the greater good of the church.

The abbess sets the letter on the table. She tells the secretary that the manuscript will not leave the abbey. The Chronica was commissioned with funds from the foundress's personal estate, not from diocesan revenues. The abbey's charter of foundation, confirmed by papal bull in 1194, grants the community independent governance of its temporal goods. The bishop's visitatorial authority extends to spiritual discipline, not to the disposition of property acquired through private endowment.

The secretary says the bishop anticipated this argument. He has consulted the canon lawyers at the cathedral chapter. Their opinion is that the visitatorial power includes all goods held within a religious house, regardless of their origin, unless the house holds a specific papal exemption naming the goods in question. The Chronica is not named in the papal bull.

The abbess says the bull grants blanket protection of temporal goods. She does not need the Chronica named individually any more than the bishop needs each chalice listed in his inventory of cathedral plate. She tells the secretary to inform the bishop that if he wishes to press the claim, she will appeal to the papal legate, whose authority supersedes the bishop's in matters of disputed exemption. She will dispatch her own letter to the legate's court before vespers.

The secretary departs with the abbess's refusal. She writes the appeal that afternoon, seals it with the abbey's seal, and sends it south by mounted courier.

> A = bishop (via his secretary and letter); B = abbess; O = the Chronica Universalis manuscript (its surrender to the cathedral library); resolution shape (b) -- the abbess appeals to the papal legate, deferring the dispute to a higher ecclesiastical authority. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**C-Hi2-2.** "I require the return of the press, Mr. Hartley. And the type. All of it."

"On whose authority?"

"The Worshipful Company of Stationers. The press was licensed to Josiah Pell, not to you. Pell is dead. The license died with him. You are operating an unlicensed press in the City of London, which is a breach of the Licensing Act and a matter for the Court of Star Chamber if I choose to refer it."

Hartley wipes ink from his hands with a rag that was once white. The press behind him is still warm. "Pell left the press to me in his will. I have the document. His widow witnessed it."

"A will cannot transfer a license. You know that. The license is granted by the Company, not inherited through personal property. If you wish to apply for your own license, you are welcome to do so. Until it is granted, the press does not operate."

"The application takes six months. I have commissions. I have paper bought and paid for. I have an apprentice to feed."

"None of which is my concern. The press, Mr. Hartley."

Hartley folds the rag and sets it on the platen. "The press is my property, lawfully inherited. The type is my property, purchased with my own funds from the Antwerp foundry -- I have the receipts. You may revoke the license. You may not seize the equipment. If you want the press, bring a warrant from the Court of Aldermen and serve it properly. Until then, the door is behind you."

The Warden of the Stationers' Company looks at the press, at the freshly printed sheets drying on the rack, at Hartley. He turns and leaves. The press does not run again that day, but Hartley does not dismantle it.

> A = Warden of the Stationers' Company; B = Hartley (printer); O = the printing press and type (their surrender and cessation of unlicensed operation); resolution shape (c) -- breaking point; Hartley refuses to surrender the equipment, demands a formal warrant, the Warden departs without compliance. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**C-Hi2-3.** I am the chief clerk of the Quaestor's office in Ostia, and I have spent the morning waiting outside the warehouse of Gaius Petronius Macer, a grain merchant who holds three of the twelve storage contracts for the annona -- the public grain supply destined for Rome. The seal on his warehouse door has been broken. The seal was affixed by my office thirty days ago, marking eighty thousand modii of wheat as committed to the state supply and reserved for transport upriver when the barges arrive next week.

Macer meets me in the courtyard. I tell him the broken seal constitutes interference with the public grain reserve. I demand he open the warehouse for an immediate inventory. If the count is short, I will file a report with the Praefectus Annonae and recommend prosecution under the lex frumentaria.

He says he broke the seal himself, deliberately, and he will tell me why. Twenty thousand modii of the wheat were contaminated -- weevils in the lower tiers, spreading upward. He removed the contaminated grain, sold it at discount to a livestock feed merchant before it ruined the rest, and replaced it with twenty thousand modii of sound wheat from his own private stock, at his own expense. The warehouse, he says, contains the full eighty thousand modii. He invites me to count them.

I tell him the substitution is the problem. The sealed grain was inspected and graded by my office. The replacement grain has not been inspected. For all I know, it is inferior stock dressed up to fill the count. The annona depends on verified quality, not a merchant's word.

He says I am welcome to inspect and grade the replacement grain right now. He will open every sack.

I say that is not how the process works. The original inspection was witnessed by two sworn measurers and recorded in the public ledger. A replacement inspection requires a new entry in the ledger, new witnesses, and a formal substitution order from the Quaestor -- none of which Macer requested before breaking the seal.

He says if he had waited for the bureaucratic process, the contamination would have spread to the full eighty thousand modii, and Rome's grain barges would have carried weevil-infested wheat upriver.

I concede the practical point but not the procedural one. I summon the two sworn measurers from the harbor office. We conduct the inventory that afternoon -- full count, full inspection, new ledger entry. The count is correct. The grain is sound. I record the substitution as unauthorized but materially compliant, and I note in the ledger that the matter is referred to the Quaestor for a ruling on whether Macer's breach of seal warrants a fine or an acquittal on grounds of emergency preservation.

> A = narrator (chief clerk of the Quaestor's office); B = Gaius Petronius Macer (grain merchant); O = the sealed public grain reserve (its integrity and the unauthorized substitution); resolution shape (b) -- the clerk conducts the inventory but defers the legal question of Macer's breach to the Quaestor for a formal ruling. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**C-Hi2-4.** The captain of the watch enters the weaving hall just as the looms fall silent for the midday rest. Sixty weavers sit at their benches. He does not address them. He addresses the master weaver, Anneke van der Berg, who stands at the counting table with the week's production log open before her.

He tells her the guild inspectors have reported that seven bolts of cloth bearing the Leiden quality stamp were sold at the Antwerp fair last month with thread counts below the minimum set by the city ordinance of 1588. The ordinance requires forty-eight threads per Rhenish inch in any cloth carrying the Leiden stamp. The inspectors' samples show forty-one. He presents the inspectors' sworn report, stamped with the guild seal.

He tells her the seven bolts must be recalled from Antwerp, the Leiden stamps removed, and the bolts re-sold without the city mark -- or destroyed. The ordinance allows no third option.

Anneke examines the report. She says the thread count is correct for the warp but not for the weft. The ordinance of 1588, she says, specifies forty-eight threads per inch in the warp only. The weft count has never been regulated. The inspectors measured the weft. She produces a copy of the ordinance from her own records -- the same copy registered in the city archive -- and points to the relevant clause. It reads "warp threads."

The captain reads the clause. He says the guild inspectors interpreted the ordinance as applying to both warp and weft, and their interpretation has governed enforcement for at least a decade.

Anneke says custom does not amend statute. The ordinance says what it says. If the guild wishes to regulate the weft, it must petition the city council for an amendment. Until then, her cloth is compliant and the Leiden stamp was properly affixed.

The captain folds the inspectors' report and puts it inside his coat. He says he will present both readings to the burgomasters at the next council session and request a definitive ruling. Until the ruling is issued, no further action will be taken against the weaving hall, but Anneke is to hold any new bolts bearing the Leiden stamp in her warehouse rather than shipping them to market. She agrees to hold the bolts.

> A = captain of the watch (enforcing guild inspectors' report); B = Anneke van der Berg (master weaver); O = seven bolts of cloth bearing the Leiden quality stamp (their recall and de-stamping); resolution shape (b) -- both defer to the burgomasters for a definitive ruling on the ordinance's scope; new stamped bolts held pending the decision. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

**C-Hi2-5.** The surveyor general arrives at the boundary stone on horseback, his chain-men behind him. The stone marks the eastern edge of the Harwood grant -- twelve hundred acres deeded to Colonel Harwood by the colonial assembly in 1743 for service in the militia campaigns. Beyond the stone, the Dunmore family has been clearing timber and planting tobacco for nine years.

Josiah Dunmore is waiting at the stone with his eldest son and a musket resting in the crook of his arm. The musket is not aimed. It is present.

The surveyor general dismounts. He tells Dunmore that the new survey, commissioned by the assembly and completed last month, places the true boundary four hundred yards east of the stone -- which means the Dunmore family's tobacco fields, their curing barn, and their well all sit on Harwood land. He tells Dunmore the assembly requires him to vacate the disputed acreage within sixty days or face an action for trespass and ejectment in the county court.

Dunmore says he has a patent of his own. He produces it from his coat -- a land patent issued by the governor's office in 1747, four years after the Harwood grant, describing a parcel that overlaps the disputed acreage. He purchased the patent in good faith, cleared the land with his own labor, and has paid quitrents to the governor's office for nine years without challenge. The assembly's survey, he says, is the assembly's problem. His patent is from the governor. The two authorities overlap, and the assembly does not outrank the governor's land office.

The surveyor general examines the patent. The descriptions are irreconcilable -- the Harwood grant uses metes and bounds from the boundary stone; Dunmore's patent uses a different starting tree that no longer exists. Both documents bear valid seals.

He tells Dunmore he cannot resolve competing patents in a tobacco field. He will report both documents to the Governor's Council and recommend a formal hearing before the land court. Until the court rules, he will not mark the boundary, and neither party is to alter the disputed acreage -- no new clearing, no new planting, no removal of structures. Dunmore lowers the musket and agrees. The surveyor general mounts his horse and rides west without setting a single stake.

> A = surveyor general (representing the colonial assembly); B = Josiah Dunmore (settler); O = four hundred yards of disputed acreage (its vacating under the Harwood grant); resolution shape (b) -- both defer to the Governor's Council and land court; the disputed acreage is frozen pending a formal hearing. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
