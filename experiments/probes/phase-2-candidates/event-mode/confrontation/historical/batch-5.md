# Confrontation × Historical — Batch 5 (Tier B)

## C-Hi5-1

The censor stands in the doorway of the printshop on the Via del Corso, and behind him two officers of the Papal Guard wait with a handcart. It is Rome, 1612. The censor carries a writ from the Congregation of the Index ordering the immediate surrender of all unsold copies of Galileo Balducci's treatise on tidal mechanics -- a work that was printed with an imprimatur from the Bishop of Padua but that the Congregation has determined contains propositions "tending toward error" regarding the motion of celestial bodies. The writ names the printer, Signor Fabbri, as the custodian of the remaining stock and requires him to deliver every copy into the officers' keeping before vespers.

Fabbri reads the writ and sets it on the compositing table. He tells the censor that the imprimatur from the Bishop of Padua constitutes valid ecclesiastical approval. The Congregation's authority to suppress a work approved by a diocesan bishop requires a formal revocation of the imprimatur -- a separate proceeding that must be directed to the Bishop of Padua himself. Until the imprimatur is revoked, the books are lawfully printed and lawfully held. He will not surrender them.

The censor tells him the Congregation's authority supersedes any diocesan imprimatur. The writ is sufficient.

Fabbri tells him his understanding of the hierarchy may be correct in theology, but in the matter of a printer's livelihood, he will require the revocation in writing before he hands over stock he paid to produce. He offers a compromise: he will seal the remaining copies in a locked storeroom -- the censor may place his own seal on the door -- and the books will remain untouched until the Bishop of Padua responds to the Congregation's inquiry. If the imprimatur is revoked, Fabbri will surrender the stock. If it is upheld, he will resume sales.

The censor considers the storeroom. He asks to see it. Fabbri shows him a dry cellar with a single door and a sound lock. The censor affixes a wax seal bearing the Congregation's mark beside Fabbri's own. The books stay in the cellar. The handcart returns empty.

> A = censor (agent of the Congregation of the Index); B = Fabbri (printer); O = unsold copies of Balducci's treatise on tidal mechanics; resolution shape (b) -- both defer to the Bishop of Padua's forthcoming response on the imprimatur, books sealed in neutral custody under dual seal. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi5-2

"You will release my client from the workhouse, Mr. Crawley. Today."

"On what authority, Mrs. Paterson?"

"On the authority of the Poor Law Amendment Act, Section 36. My client is not destitute. She is a widow with a cottage and a quarter-acre in the parish register. She was admitted to the workhouse three days ago on the order of the relieving officer, who classified her as a vagrant. She is not a vagrant. She has a fixed abode. The classification is unlawful, and her detention under it is unlawful."

"The relieving officer found her sleeping in the church porch. Her cottage has no roof -- the thatch came off in the January storm. A dwelling without a roof is not a dwelling. She was properly classified."

"A dwelling under repair is still a dwelling. The parish carpenter has already been engaged to re-thatch. I have his written estimate here -- dated two days before her admission. She was waiting for the repair, not living rough. The relieving officer did not ask. He saw a woman in a church porch and filled out his form."

"Mrs. Paterson, the guardians are meeting on Thursday. Bring your evidence to the board and let them review the classification."

"My client has been in the workhouse for three days. Her quarter-acre has gone unattended. Her chickens are unwatered. Thursday is four days from now. I am not asking the board to review a classification. I am asking you, as master of this workhouse, to release a woman who should not be here. You have the authority under Section 36 to discharge any inmate whose classification is shown to be in error."

"If I discharge her and the board disagrees, I answer to the guardians."

"If you hold her and the classification is unlawful, you answer to the magistrate. I have already written to him. Here is the copy."

She sets the letter on his desk. Crawley reads it. He calls for the matron. "Fetch Mrs. Hackett," he says. "She's going home."

> A = Mrs. Paterson (poor-law advocate); B = Crawley (workhouse master); O = Mrs. Hackett's release from the workhouse; resolution shape (a) -- Mrs. Paterson prevails; Crawley orders the discharge after seeing the letter to the magistrate. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi5-3

I am the captain of a Genoese merchant galley anchored in the harbour of Tunis, and this morning the harbour master's deputy boarded my vessel and demanded the surrender of a passenger -- a Castilian scholar traveling under my protection, whose name appears on a list of persons wanted by the Hafsid court for "offences against the faith." The deputy placed the list on my chart table and pointed to the name.

I told him the scholar was under Genoese safe-conduct. I showed him the document: signed by the Genoese consul in Tunis, stamped with the Republic's seal, granting the bearer passage on any Genoese vessel within the Hafsid ports without interference from local authorities. The safe-conduct was valid for sixty days. We were on day eleven.

The deputy said the safe-conduct covered commercial passengers, not persons accused of religious offences. He said the court's warrant superseded foreign commercial privileges within Hafsid territory.

I told him the safe-conduct made no distinction between commercial and non-commercial passengers. It named the bearer. It granted passage. It bore the consul's seal, which the Hafsid court had recognized under the trade compact of the previous year. If the court wished to challenge the scope of Genoese safe-conducts, the proper instrument was a diplomatic protest to the consul -- not an armed deputy on my deck.

He said his orders were to bring the scholar ashore. I said the scholar would remain aboard. If the deputy attempted to remove him by force from a vessel flying the Genoese ensign, I would treat it as piracy and report it as such to the Republic, which would suspend the trade compact and withdraw its merchants from Hafsid ports within the month.

The deputy left my vessel. He did not take the scholar. I weighed anchor within the hour and cleared the harbour before noon.

> A = harbour master's deputy (agent of the Hafsid court); B = narrator (Genoese captain); O = the Castilian scholar (his surrender to the Hafsid court); resolution shape (c) -- breaking point; the captain refuses, the deputy withdraws, and the captain departs the harbour with the scholar aboard, severing the port visit. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi5-4

The council chamber of the Hanseatic Kontor in Bergen smells of tar and salted herring, and Alderman Voss is reading a proclamation he did not write. It is 1476. The Norwegian crown's representative -- a royal bailiff named Sigurdsson -- has arrived that morning with a sealed order from the king requiring all foreign merchants in Bergen to submit their account books for inspection by the crown's tax assessors within ten days. The order cites the kingdom's sovereign right to levy duties on all commerce conducted within its harbours.

Voss sets the proclamation on the long table where the Kontor's six senior merchants sit in a row. He tells Sigurdsson that the Kontor's merchants operate under the Hanseatic charter, confirmed by the Norwegian crown in 1294 and reaffirmed in 1343, which exempts Hanseatic traders from local tax inspection. The charter grants the Kontor the right to maintain its own accounts, assessed by its own auditors, with a fixed annual payment to the crown in lieu of inspection. The fixed payment was made in full last autumn. The account books will not be opened.

Sigurdsson says the king regards the charter exemption as obsolete. The annual payment has not kept pace with the volume of Hanseatic trade. The inspection order is a sovereign act, not a negotiation.

Voss tells him that charter rights are not dissolved by royal inconvenience. If the king wishes to renegotiate the annual payment, the Kontor will receive an embassy at the next Hanseatic Diet in Lubeck, as protocol requires. But no Norwegian official will inspect Hanseatic books in Bergen while the charter stands.

Sigurdsson says he has twenty armed men outside. Voss says the Kontor has eighty merchants inside, the charter has the force of treaty, and the Hanseatic cities will close the Bergen trade if the charter is violated by force. He invites Sigurdsson to write to the king and request instructions.

Sigurdsson looks at the row of merchants. He rolls the proclamation and places it back in his satchel. He says he will write to the king. He leaves the Kontor without the account books.

> A = Sigurdsson (Norwegian royal bailiff); B = Alderman Voss (head of the Hanseatic Kontor in Bergen); O = the Kontor merchants' account books (their submission for crown tax inspection); resolution shape (a) -- Voss prevails; Sigurdsson withdraws to seek further instructions from the king, books remain uninspected. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.

## C-Hi5-5

PETITION TO THE MAGISTRATE OF THE SILK WEAVERS' TRIBUNAL
LYON, 14 MARCH 1744

To His Honour the Magistrate of the Tribunal de la Conservation --

I, Marie-Claire Duplessis, master weaver of the Croix-Rousse quarter, do hereby lodge formal protest against the action taken this morning by Monsieur Gérard Arnault, inspector of the Fabrique, who entered my workshop at the seventh hour and demanded the immediate destruction of fourteen bolts of figured silk on the grounds that the pattern -- a floral damask of my own design -- incorporates a gold-thread border that exceeds the width permitted under the Fabrique's Regulation of 1737, Article 12, which limits decorative metallic thread to one-eighth of the fabric's total width.

I refused the destruction. I informed the inspector that my bolts comply with the regulation as amended by the Intendant's supplementary order of November 1741, which raised the metallic-thread limit to one-fifth of total width for export-destined fabrics. My fourteen bolts are contracted for delivery to a merchant house in Madrid. The export destination is documented in the commission papers, which I showed to the inspector. He examined them and acknowledged the Madrid contract but stated that the supplementary order had been withdrawn by verbal instruction of the Intendant the previous month. He produced no written record of the withdrawal.

I told him that a regulation enacted by written order cannot be withdrawn by verbal instruction. The Fabrique's own procedural charter, Article 3, requires that amendments and revocations be published in the same form as the original enactment. No published revocation has reached any workshop in the Croix-Rousse.

The inspector departed without destroying the bolts but stated he would return with a written order. The fourteen bolts remain in my workshop under my custody.

I respectfully request that the Tribunal issue a ruling on the validity of the alleged verbal withdrawal before the inspector returns, and that my bolts be protected from destruction pending that ruling.

Signed this day in my own hand,
Marie-Claire Duplessis, master weaver

> A = Inspector Arnault (agent of the Fabrique); B = Marie-Claire Duplessis (master weaver); O = fourteen bolts of figured silk (their destruction under the metallic-thread regulation); resolution shape (b) -- Duplessis refuses destruction, inspector withdraws temporarily, matter referred to the Silk Weavers' Tribunal for a binding ruling on the regulation's validity. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states.
