# Confrontation x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**C-Hi1-1.** The harbour master of Bruges walks down the quay on a grey morning in 1394 and stops at the berth where the Venetian galley *Santa Lucia* is being loaded. Sixty bales of English wool sit stacked on the dock, each stamped with the mark of the Florentine merchant house of Albizzi. The harbour master has received word from the Aldermen's Council: no wool bearing a foreign merchant's stamp is to leave the harbour until the Albizzi house settles the anchorage tax it has owed the city since Michaelmas. The debt stands at forty-two Flemish pounds.

He tells the Venetian captain that the wool cannot be loaded. The ship may depart when ready, but the sixty bales remain on the quay under the city's lien until the Albizzi debt is cleared.

The captain produces a bill of lading signed in Venice. The wool, he says, is no longer Albizzi property. It was purchased by the Venetian state galley system three weeks ago in Antwerp, transferred through lawful sale, and the bill of lading reflects the new ownership. The Albizzi stamp is a remnant of the original packing, not evidence of current title. Venice owes Bruges nothing. He orders his crew to continue loading.

The harbour master tells his port wardens to block the gangway. He says the Aldermen's Council placed the lien on the goods, not on the merchant. The wool was in the harbour when the lien was filed. A subsequent sale does not extinguish a civic lien -- Bruges' harbour ordinances, confirmed by the Count of Flanders in 1387, hold that goods under lien remain under lien regardless of transfer until the underlying debt is settled or the Aldermen vote to release them.

The captain says he is operating under Venetian maritime law, which does not recognise foreign municipal liens on goods lawfully purchased in open market. He tells the harbour master that if the wool is not released, he will send word to the Venetian consul in Bruges, and the consul will lodge a formal protest with the Count's court.

The harbour master says the captain is welcome to do so. The wool stays on the quay. He posts two wardens at the berth and walks back up the quay.

The captain sends a runner to the Venetian consul's house before the morning bells finish ringing.

> A = harbour master of Bruges; B = Venetian galley captain; O = the sixty bales of English wool (their release for loading vs. retention under the city's lien); resolution shape (a) -- harbour master prevails; the wool remains on the quay under lien, and the captain's recourse is an appeal through the Venetian consul to the Count's court. All roles, all states.

**C-Hi1-2.** "You will remove the pamphlets from your shop, Mr. Curll."

"On whose authority?"

"On mine. I am the Surveyor of the Press, appointed by the Lord Chamberlain's office. The Licensing Act gives me authority to seize any printed material that has not been entered in the Stationers' Register."

"The Licensing Act expired two years ago, Mr. Fraser. Parliament let it lapse in '95. You have no authority to seize anything. You are a man with an old title and no statute behind it."

The two men stand in the narrow shop on Paternoster Row. It is a March afternoon in 1697. The pamphlets in question -- a satirical account of certain proceedings in the House of Lords -- are stacked in bundles of fifty on the counter between them. Fraser has not touched them.

"The Act may have lapsed," Fraser says. "The common-law offence of seditious libel has not. The pamphlet names three sitting peers by their initials and accuses them of bribery. That is actionable regardless of the Licensing Act. I am instructing you to withdraw the pamphlets voluntarily. If you do not, I will refer the matter to the Attorney General for prosecution."

Curll picks up one of the pamphlets and turns it over in his hands. "The pamphlet names no one. It uses initials. The reader may speculate, but speculation is not identification. My counsel's opinion is that the innuendo doctrine requires proof that reasonable readers would identify specific persons, and initials alone do not meet that threshold."

"Your counsel is giving you a defence, not a guarantee. The Attorney General may disagree. And while the matter is being argued, your shop will be searched and your stock impounded."

"Then let the Attorney General disagree. I will not withdraw a legal publication on the threat of a prosecution that may never come. If you wish to refer the matter, refer it. The pamphlets remain on my counter until a court orders otherwise."

Fraser nods once, turns on his heel, and walks out of the shop. The bell above the door rings as it closes behind him.

> A = Fraser (Surveyor of the Press); B = Edmund Curll (bookseller); O = the satirical pamphlets (their withdrawal from sale); resolution shape (c) -- breaking point; Curll refuses to withdraw, Fraser leaves to refer the matter to the Attorney General, and the pamphlets remain on sale pending any future prosecution. All roles, all states.

**C-Hi1-3.** I am a physician attached to the military hospital at Scutari, and this morning I told the Purveyor that I needed clean bedding for the surgical ward. Three hundred men lie on the floor of the corridor outside my ward. The sheets beneath them have not been changed in eleven days. The linen storeroom, I have been told, contains six hundred fresh sheets -- requisitioned, shipped from Constantinople, and delivered to the hospital last Thursday. The Purveyor has not released them.

I found him in his office on the second floor, behind a desk stacked with requisition forms and supply ledgers. I told him I needed the six hundred sheets distributed to the wards by the end of the day. Men were developing secondary infections from the filth of their own bedding. The sheets existed. They were in the building. The only thing between the sheets and the patients was his signature on a release form.

He told me the sheets could not be released. The requisition had been filed by the hospital's superintendent, but the Purveyor's standing orders from the War Office required that all supply releases above a value of ten pounds be counter-signed by the Inspector General of Hospitals. The Inspector General was in Varna. He was not expected in Scutari for another week. The Purveyor's hands, he said, were tied by regulation.

I told him the regulation was designed for peacetime supply chains, not for a hospital where men were dying of infections contracted from their own bedding. I asked him to release the sheets on his own authority and obtain the counter-signature retrospectively when the Inspector General arrived.

He refused. He said that issuing supplies without proper authorisation had cost the previous Purveyor his position. He would not expose himself to the same risk. He suggested I write to the Inspector General requesting an expedited counter-signature.

I told him I would do no such thing. I told him that if the sheets were not released by evening, I would write directly to the Secretary of State for War, with a copy to the Times correspondent who was quartered in the hospital, detailing the exact contents of the storeroom, the exact condition of the wards, and the name of the official who chose to let men rot rather than risk a procedural reprimand.

The Purveyor looked at me for a long time. Then he opened the storeroom ledger, signed the release form, and handed it to an orderly. The sheets reached the wards before nightfall.

> A = I (narrator / military physician at Scutari); B = the Purveyor; O = six hundred clean sheets held in the linen storeroom (their release to the wards); resolution shape (a) -- narrator prevails; Purveyor signs the release after the threat of public exposure through the press and the Secretary of State for War. All roles, all states.

**C-Hi1-4.** The morning light falls through the high windows of the Chapter House at Canterbury, and Prior Benedict is reading the letter aloud. It is the year 1170, the week before Advent. The letter bears the seal of the Archbishop and was delivered by his chaplain, who stands by the door with his hands folded.

The letter instructs the prior and monks of the cathedral chapter to restore to the Archbishop's household the revenues of the manor of Saltwood, which the chapter has administered since the Archbishop's exile six years ago. The Archbishop has returned to England. The revenues are his by ancient right, confirmed in the cathedral's own foundation charter. He requires them immediately to sustain his household and to fund the distribution of alms he has promised for Christmas.

Prior Benedict sets the letter down. He addresses the chaplain, knowing his words will be carried back exactly. He says the chapter will not surrender the Saltwood revenues. During the six years of the Archbishop's absence, the chapter has used those revenues to repair the cathedral roof, fund the scriptorium, and feed the poor of the precinct. The chapter undertook these obligations in good faith, under authority granted by the papal legate during the Archbishop's exile. Surrendering the revenues now would leave the chapter unable to meet commitments already made -- debts to craftsmen, contracts with suppliers of vellum and lead, and the weekly dole to the poor that two hundred people depend upon.

The chaplain asks whether the prior is refusing a direct instruction from the Archbishop.

Prior Benedict says he is not refusing. He is invoking the chapter's right, under the Rule and under canon law, to refer a dispute over shared revenues to the bishop's court -- in this case, the court of the Bishop of London, who serves as metropolitan deputy in revenue disputes within the province. He proposes that both parties present their claims to the Bishop of London within a fortnight, and that the Saltwood revenues remain in the chapter's account, unspent beyond existing commitments, until the bishop rules.

The chaplain says he will carry the prior's response to the Archbishop. He leaves without further word.

> A = the Archbishop (via letter and chaplain); B = Prior Benedict (prior of the Canterbury cathedral chapter); O = the revenues of the manor of Saltwood; resolution shape (b) -- Prior Benedict invokes canon law to refer the dispute to the Bishop of London's court, with revenues held unspent pending the ruling. All roles, all states.

**C-Hi1-5.** The factory floor of the Lowell No. 2 Mill smells of machine oil and wet cotton in the August heat of 1836. Sarah Bagley stands at the end of the spinning room, where fifty-eight women work the frames in twelve-hour shifts. The overseer, Mr. Kimball, has just posted a notice on the board by the door: effective Monday, the piece rate will be reduced by fifteen percent. The speed of the frames will be increased to compensate -- the company expects the same output per worker at the new rate, achieved through faster machine operation.

Sarah pulls the notice from the board, walks the length of the spinning room to where Kimball stands by the counting desk, and sets it in front of him. She tells him the women of the No. 2 spinning room will not work at the reduced rate. The original rate was agreed upon when the women signed their contracts at the start of the season. The contracts specify the piece rate. Reducing it mid-season, without renegotiation, is a breach.

Kimball tells her the contracts contain a clause -- paragraph seven -- allowing the company to adjust rates in response to market conditions. The price of finished cotton has dropped eighteen percent since May. The rate reduction keeps the mill profitable. He tells her to put the notice back on the board.

Sarah says paragraph seven requires thirty days' written notice before a rate adjustment takes effect. The notice was posted today. Monday is four days away. Even under the company's own clause, the reduction cannot take effect for another twenty-six days.

Kimball pauses. He looks at the notice, then at Sarah, then at the fifty-eight women who have stopped their frames and are watching in the silence that follows the machines' shutdown.

He tells Sarah the thirty-day provision is an administrative guideline, not a binding commitment, and that the superintendent has authority to waive it under urgent market conditions.

Sarah tells him that if the notice is not withdrawn and reissued with the proper thirty-day window, every woman in the spinning room will exercise the termination clause in her contract -- paragraph twelve -- which allows voluntary departure with forfeiture of the current month's boarding credit but no further penalty. Fifty-eight women walking out of the No. 2 spinning room in the middle of the August production run will cost the mill far more than the rate reduction saves.

Kimball removes the notice from the desk and tells her he will speak to the superintendent. He does not post a new notice. The women restart their frames.

> A = Sarah Bagley (mill worker and spokeswoman); B = Mr. Kimball (overseer of the No. 2 spinning room); O = the piece-rate reduction notice (its withdrawal or enforcement); resolution shape (a) -- Sarah prevails; Kimball withdraws the notice to consult the superintendent, and no rate change takes effect. All roles, all states.
