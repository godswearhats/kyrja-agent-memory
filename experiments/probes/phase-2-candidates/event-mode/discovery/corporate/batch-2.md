# Discovery × Corporate — Batch 2 (Tier B)

**Di-Co2-1.** The CFO pours coffee in the breakroom and overhears two junior accountants talking about the Shenzhen entity. She knows the Shenzhen entity. It is the subsidiary they wound down eighteen months ago — she signed the dissolution filing herself, watched the bank account close, filed the final tax return. One of the accountants says, "The Shenzhen wire cleared this morning, same as last month." The other says, "Forty-two thousand, right? It always posts on the fifteenth." The CFO sets her mug on the counter. She pulls the treasury ledger on her phone, filters by entity code. Twelve outbound wires over twelve months, each forty-two thousand dollars, each routed to an account prefix she does not recognise, each booked under the entity she personally dissolved. The subsidiary she closed is still moving money — or something is moving money under its name.

> A = CFO; B = the Shenzhen subsidiary is fully dissolved (she signed the dissolution, closed the account, filed the final return); E = overhearing the wire discussion and confirming twelve monthly $42K outbound transfers still booked under the dissolved entity's code; B' = the entity — or its credentials — remain active and are routing funds through a channel she did not authorise and does not control. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Co2-2.** "Walk me through the talent pipeline," Priya says, pulling up the org chart on the conference-room screen. She has spent three quarters building it: forty-two engineers, eight team leads, three senior architects, structured into the pod system she designed and staffed herself.

"About that," her VP of engineering says. He does not sit down. "I need you to look at the badge-access data from building four."

"Why?"

He turns his laptop toward her. The access log shows swipe counts by floor, by week, for the last ninety days. The engineering floors — three and four — show a steady decline. Not attrition. Attendance. The current week's count: eleven unique badges across both floors. Eleven people physically present out of a forty-two-person department.

"They're remote," Priya says. "We're hybrid."

"The hybrid policy requires three days on-site. Eleven badges, Priya. For the whole week." He scrolls to the bottom. "And nine of the eleven are on your facilities and IT teams. Two are engineers."

The org chart is still on the screen behind her. Forty-two boxes, eight pods, three layers of architecture. The department she built exists on paper. Two engineers come to the building.

> A = Priya (engineering director); B = she has a functioning 42-person engineering department operating in a hybrid pod structure; E = badge-access data showing only 11 unique badges on engineering floors for the entire week, 9 of which are facilities/IT — only 2 are engineers; B' = the department as a physically present, collaborating organisation effectively does not exist — it is a paper structure with near-total absenteeism. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Co2-3.** Marcus is presenting the quarterly customer-satisfaction results to the executive team. He has run this programme for two years. Net Promoter Score: seventy-one, up from sixty-eight last quarter. He built the survey instrument himself, validated the sampling method, and he trusts the number. It is the centrepiece of his performance review and the basis of the retention-investment case he is bringing to the board next week. The chief product officer interrupts him mid-slide. "Marcus, what's your response rate?" Marcus checks his notes. "Twelve percent." The CPO nods. "And who are the twelve percent?" She pulls up the CRM segmentation on her own laptop, projects it beside his slide. The twelve percent who respond to Marcus's survey are, almost entirely, users on the enterprise tier — the tier with dedicated account managers who email survey reminders personally. The free and mid-tier users, who make up eighty-one percent of the user base, return surveys at under two percent. His seventy-one NPS is not measuring the customer base. It is measuring the segment that receives white-glove service.

> A = Marcus (customer-satisfaction programme lead); B = NPS of 71 reflects genuine customer satisfaction across the user base; E = CPO's CRM segmentation showing the 12% response rate is almost entirely enterprise-tier users with dedicated account managers, while 81% of the user base responds at under 2%; B' = the NPS score measures only the white-glove segment, not the customer base — it is a sampling artefact, not a satisfaction signal. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Co2-4.** FROM: facilities-monitoring@corp.internal
TO: Diane Xu, Head of Workplace Experience
SUBJECT: Monthly air-quality report — Building C, April

Diane,

Attached is the April sensor summary for Building C. All floors within acceptable range on CO2, particulate, and humidity, as expected. One anomaly flagged for your review.

The occupancy sensors on floors 6 through 9 — the four floors you designated as the "wellness wing" in the January refit — are registering continuous occupancy between 11 PM and 5 AM, seven days a week. The HVAC system has been running full cycle on those floors around the clock to compensate. I cross-referenced with badge access: no after-hours badges. No cleaning crew scheduled past 10 PM. The motion sensors are firing, Diane. Something is triggering them, and it is not credentialed personnel.

I pulled the thermal imaging from the floor-8 corridor camera as a spot check. The images show sleeping bags, personal items, and what appears to be a camp stove near the east stairwell.

The wellness wing you built as a daytime amenity for employees is being used as overnight shelter by people entering the building through an unsecured loading dock. The facilities budget you allocated for that wing — climate control, cleaning, wear — is running at roughly triple the projected rate, and none of it is serving employees.

— Ray Chen, Facilities Operations

> A = Diane Xu (Head of Workplace Experience); B = the wellness wing (floors 6-9) is a daytime employee amenity operating within its designed budget and usage parameters; E = facilities report showing continuous overnight occupancy with no badge access, thermal imaging of sleeping bags and personal items, and HVAC running at triple projected cost; B' = the wellness wing is being used as overnight shelter by unauthorised occupants entering through an unsecured dock, and its operating costs reflect that unintended use, not employee amenity load. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Co2-5.** Tomoko Ando, the firm's managing partner, sits across from the client she has personally managed for eleven years. She has always understood this account as the firm's anchor relationship — three-point-two million in annual billings, renewed every January without negotiation, the reference client she names in every pitch. The client slides a single sheet of paper across the table. It is a fee comparison: Tomoko's firm beside two competitors, line by line, service by service. Every line shows Tomoko's firm billing between thirty and sixty percent above market rate. But that is not what stops her. At the bottom of the sheet, in the client's handwriting, is a note: "We have not used your litigation team since 2022. We have not used your tax advisory since 2021. The only active engagement is regulatory compliance — approximately $410K annually." Tomoko looks at the number. Three-point-two million is the contracted retainer. Four hundred and ten thousand is what the client actually consumes. The anchor relationship she has been citing in every pitch is a client paying three-point-two million for four hundred and ten thousand dollars of work — and the client now knows it.

> A = Tomoko Ando (managing partner); B = the anchor client relationship is a robust, fully-utilised $3.2M engagement (the basis of her pitch narrative and the firm's reference account); E = client's fee-comparison sheet showing only $410K of active services consumed, with litigation unused since 2022 and tax advisory since 2021; B' = the relationship is a massively over-billed retainer — the client pays $3.2M for $410K of actual work, and the client is now aware of the discrepancy, making the "anchor" a liability rather than an asset. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.
