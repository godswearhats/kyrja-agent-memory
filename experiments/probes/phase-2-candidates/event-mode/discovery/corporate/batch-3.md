# Discovery × Corporate — Batch 3 (Tier C)

**Di-Co3-1**

The regional sales director has spent the last two quarters defending her territory's numbers to the VP. Her argument has always been the same: pricing is competitive, the product is strong, and the decline is a market-wide softening. She has said it in three town halls and put it in two written updates. On Wednesday afternoon the VP sends her a forwarded thread — no comment, just a forward — between two of her largest accounts and a competitor's account manager. The thread is six weeks old. The first message is from the competitor: "We've heard you're having service issues with your current provider. We can match on price and guarantee ninety-six-hour implementation." The second is from her account, a distribution company she has held for four years: "We've had three missed SLAs in eight weeks. We're listening." She had not known about a single missed SLA. Her team had not escalated one. The churn she had attributed to macroeconomic softening was coming from something her own operations were doing — something no one had told her, and that she had not thought to ask about.

> A = regional sales director; B = territory decline is caused by market-wide softening, not internal factors; E = forwarded email thread between two accounts and a competitor showing accounts defecting due to three missed SLAs in eight weeks — service failures never escalated to her; B′ = the decline is driven by internal service failures, not market conditions. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Co3-2**

"Walk me through the onboarding funnel," the product manager says. She is standing at the whiteboard. Across the table, the lead UX researcher has his laptop open.

"Step four is where we lose them," she says. "Forty-two percent drop-off at the permissions screen. We've tested four copy variants. Nothing moves the needle."

"When did you last watch a session recording of step four?"

She pauses. "The UX team handles recordings."

He turns the laptop toward her. The recording is from yesterday — a user on a tablet, not a phone, not a desktop. Step four does not show a permissions screen. It shows a blank white rectangle where the permissions screen should be. The user taps it twice, waits, taps again, and exits. He loads the next recording. Same device class, same blank rectangle. He loads a third. "Roughly thirty percent of your volume is coming from mid-range Android tablets," he says. "The permissions component doesn't render on that screen resolution. There's no copy variant that fixes a component that isn't there."

The product manager looks at the recordings. She had been treating this as a messaging problem for eleven weeks.

> A = product manager; B = the 42% drop-off at step four is caused by ineffective copy/messaging on the permissions screen; E = UX session recordings showing the permissions component does not render at all on mid-range Android tablets (the device class of ~30% of users); B′ = the drop-off is a rendering failure, not a messaging problem — the permissions screen is invisible on a large share of devices. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Co3-3**

I had managed the vendor relationship for three years and had never requested an itemised invoice. The master service agreement set a flat monthly fee, and the finance team processed it without comment every quarter. The external audit came as a compliance formality — I expected it to confirm what we already knew. When the auditors sent the itemised billing extract, I went through it line by line out of obligation rather than suspicion. The licensing tier we were paying for was rated to five hundred concurrent users. Our peak concurrent usage, logged by the vendor's own telemetry and included as an appendix, had never exceeded sixty-one. We had been contracted to a tier we had never approached, paying for capacity we had no evidence of needing and had never been advised to reconsider. The contract's auto-renewal clause had rolled it forward twice. No one had ever checked whether the tier matched the load.

> A = I (vendor relationship manager); B = the flat monthly fee reflects an appropriate service tier for our usage (assumption embedded in three years of unquestioned payments); E = auditors' itemised billing extract showing the licensed tier covers 500 concurrent users against peak actual usage of 61, per vendor's own telemetry appendix; B′ = the contracted tier is drastically over-specified for our actual usage; payments have been inflated for years with no review. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Co3-4**

The CFO is on the phone with the investment bank when her assistant slides a printed page onto her desk and taps it twice. She holds up one finger — one minute — and keeps talking through the accretion model. Then she picks up the page. It is a Companies House filing, printed twelve minutes ago, dated yesterday. The acquisition target she has been valuing for six weeks has transferred its primary UK operating subsidiary to a new holding company registered in Jersey three days before the exclusivity window opened. The subsidiary is the one carrying the contracts that account for eighty percent of the target's attributed revenue. She has been building her model on the assumption that the operating assets and their associated revenue are consolidated under the UK parent — the structure the management presentation described. The UK parent is now a holding shell. What she agreed to value is not what she is being sold.

> A = CFO; B = the acquisition target's operating assets and attributed revenue are consolidated under the UK parent company (as represented in the management presentation); E = Companies House filing showing the primary UK operating subsidiary was transferred to a new Jersey holding company three days before exclusivity opened; B′ = the UK parent is a holding shell; the assets underlying the revenue are no longer part of the entity being acquired. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Co3-5**

The compliance officer sets the training completion dashboard on the left monitor and the incident log on the right. She is preparing the board report and wants both open while she writes the summary. The training dashboard shows ninety-four percent completion across all mandatory modules — the highest rate in four years, and the number she plans to lead with. On the right monitor, the incident log is sorted by date. She is about to minimise it when something catches her: the incident type field. She sorts by type instead of date. Fourteen of the eighteen data-handling incidents in the past six months are categorised as "incorrect procedure — trained employee." She pulls the completion certificates for the names on those fourteen incidents. All fourteen hold current, valid completion records for the modules that govern the procedures they violated. A ninety-four percent training completion rate has been measuring whether employees have sat the training. It has not been measuring whether the training changes what they do.

> A = compliance officer; B = high training completion rate (94%) indicates the compliance programme is working — employees are learning and following required procedures; E = cross-referencing incident log with completion certificates shows 14 of 18 data-handling incidents were committed by employees with current, valid training completion records; B′ = training completion rate measures attendance, not behaviour change; the programme is not producing compliance. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states
