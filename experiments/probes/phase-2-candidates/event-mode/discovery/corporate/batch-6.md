## Di-Co6-1

The merger integration lead, Priya Sundaram, had spent ten days building the headcount reconciliation on the assumption that the acquired company ran a single payroll. That was what the due-diligence summary said: one payroll provider, one legal employer, four hundred and twelve employees. She was mapping benefit plans when the acquired company's head of HR mentioned, offhandedly, that the contractors in the Bratislava office processed their invoices through a separate entity — a Slovak shell that the founders had set up years ago and never wound down. Priya asked how many contractors. The HR lead checked her phone. One hundred and thirty-one. Priya set down her pen. The four hundred and twelve figure she had been reconciling against was headcount only; total working population was five hundred and forty-three — and the contractors carried their own benefit structures, pension obligations, and notice entitlements under Slovak labour law, none of which appeared in any document she had been given.

> A = Priya Sundaram (merger integration lead); B = the acquired company has 412 employees on a single payroll — the complete working population; E = HR lead's offhand disclosure that 131 contractors in Bratislava invoice through a separate Slovak entity not captured in the due-diligence summary; B′ = total working population is 543, with a separate legal employer, distinct benefit obligations, and labour-law entitlements that were absent from all provided documentation. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

## Di-Co6-2

"Pull up the active user count for Enterprise tier," the growth analyst, Daniel Park, says.

The data engineer runs the query. "Fourteen thousand two hundred and eighteen seats licensed. Eleven thousand nine hundred and forty-one showing activity in the last thirty days."

"That's the number I've been using. What's the seat-utilisation breakdown by account?"

She filters the table and turns her screen toward him. The top thirty accounts by seat count are all showing utilisation below fifteen percent. One account — Meridian Financial, eight hundred seats — shows six active users.

"They renewed last quarter," Daniel says.

"Auto-renewed," the data engineer says. "The renewal triggers when the contract date passes, not on usage review."

Daniel stares at the utilisation column. The eleven thousand nine hundred active users he had been presenting as proof of product stickiness were spread across accounts where most licensed seats had not been touched in months. Renewal was not a signal of value. It was a billing artefact.

> A = Daniel Park (growth analyst); B = the 11,941 active users and renewal rates indicate genuine product stickiness and customer value; E = seat-utilisation breakdown showing top accounts below 15% utilisation, with auto-renewal triggering on contract date rather than usage review; B′ = renewal figures are a billing artefact, not a value signal; the active-user count is distributed across largely idle accounts. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

## Di-Co6-3

I had led the procurement function for three years before anyone told me the preferred-vendor list was maintained by the VP of operations, not by my team. I thought I owned it. Every tender I ran, I ran against that list — forty-seven approved suppliers, vetted for compliance, rated by performance. On a Tuesday morning I was onboarding a new category manager and pulled up the list to walk her through the vetting criteria. She asked why Kortekaas Logistics was on there. I told her they had passed the environmental audit two years prior. She said she had processed their last eleven invoices and the company address on every one was a residential street in Schiedam. I pulled up the Companies House record. Kortekaas Logistics had been dissolved fourteen months ago. The entity invoicing us was a different registration — same name, same bank account, different VAT number. Forty-seven approved suppliers. I had no idea what I actually owned.

> A = procurement lead (I); B = the preferred-vendor list is accurate and all 47 suppliers are compliant, active, vetted entities; E = category manager flags that Kortekaas Logistics invoices show a residential address; Companies House lookup shows the original entity dissolved 14 months ago and invoices coming from a different registration with a different VAT number; B′ = at least one "approved" supplier on the list is a dissolved entity being impersonated by a different registration — the vetting process has not caught this, and the list's integrity is unknown. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

## Di-Co6-4

The editorial director of the publishing house, Lucinda Voss, has run the midlist review every spring for seven years. The model is simple: titles with fewer than two thousand net sales in their second year get remaindered. She is halfway through this year's list when the rights manager sets a printout on her desk — a royalty statement for a title she had already flagged for remaindering, the debut collection by a poet named Anya Czernova. Net sales: one thousand four hundred. Lucinda has already written the remainder recommendation. The rights manager points to the licensing column. The collection has been set to music by a composer in Warsaw; the recording has four hundred thousand streams; the sync rights have been licensed to two film productions; and a Bulgarian translation is in its second edition. The book earns less from retail than any other title on the list. It is also the house's most licensed intellectual property by number of agreements. Lucinda's two-thousand-net-sales threshold measures one revenue pathway. It does not measure the asset.

> A = Lucinda Voss (editorial director); B = net retail sales are the operative measure of a title's commercial viability; a title with 1,400 net sales in year two is a candidate for remaindering; E = royalty statement showing Czernova's collection has generated sync licensing for two films, a foreign-language translation in second edition, and a musical adaptation with 400,000 streams — making it the house's most-licensed IP by agreement count despite low retail sales; B′ = the net-sales threshold measures only one revenue pathway; the Czernova title is commercially significant on dimensions the review model does not capture. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

## Di-Co6-5

INTERNAL AUDIT MEMO — RESTRICTED DISTRIBUTION
To: CFO, General Counsel
From: Internal Audit, M. Achterberg
Re: Expense Reconciliation, Executive Travel — Q1–Q3

At the request of the CFO, Internal Audit reviewed the executive travel and entertainment expense line following anomalies flagged by the automated reconciliation system. The CFO's working assumption, conveyed to the audit team at the outset of this review, was that the anomalies reflected a miscategorisation of conference sponsorship payments as personal T&E — an administrative error corrected in Q2.

The review established the following: forty-three expense submissions from a single employee (Director of Strategic Partnerships, R. Holt) across the three-quarter period show receipts from the same four vendors. All four vendors were registered within a fourteen-month window. None have identifiable web presence, premises, or third-party transaction history. Bank records obtained via the reconciliation interface show that payment from the company to each vendor settled within forty-eight hours to a single intermediary account before onward transfer. The pattern is inconsistent with a miscategorisation of conference costs. It is consistent with a structured disbursement scheme.

> A = CFO; B = the T&E anomalies are an administrative miscategorisation of conference sponsorship payments, corrected in Q2; E = Internal Audit memo documenting that 43 expense submissions from R. Holt reference four recently registered vendors with no verifiable existence, and that payments flow through a single intermediary account — a pattern inconsistent with miscategorisation; B′ = the anomalies are not an administrative error but evidence of a structured disbursement scheme. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states
