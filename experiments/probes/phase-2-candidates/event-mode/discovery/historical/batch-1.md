# Discovery x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

**Di-Hi1-1.** The harbour master of Guangzhou stands on the customs wharf in the autumn of 1637, watching the Dutch merchantman unload its cargo. He has served the Ming maritime inspectorate for nine years, and in that time every European vessel arriving at Guangzhou has carried the same class of goods: woolens, clockwork, silver bullion. The trade pattern is so consistent that his tariff schedule, submitted to the provincial governor last spring, assumes European imports are luxury manufactures and precious metals — nothing that competes with domestic production. The Dutch captain's manifest lists five hundred bolts of Indian cotton, dyed in patterns that imitate Fujian textile designs. The harbour master inspects the bolts himself. The weave is coarser than Fujian cloth but the price, listed on the manifest, is less than half. He has been calculating tariffs on the assumption that European trade brings goods China does not produce. The cotton on the wharf is not a European luxury. It is a cheap substitute for a domestic staple, priced to undercut the Fujian weavers whose tax revenue funds the inspectorate itself.

> A = harbour master of Guangzhou; B = European imports are luxury goods and precious metals that do not compete with domestic production (basis of his tariff schedule); E = direct inspection of a Dutch cargo of 500 bolts of Indian cotton imitating Fujian designs at half the domestic price; B' = European trade now carries goods that directly undercut domestic staple production — the tariff framework is built on an obsolete model of what Europeans bring. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Hi1-2.** "How many of the wells are still producing?" asks the Roman military tribune, standing at the edge of the Numidian garrison's cistern.

"All six," the praefectus castrorum replies. "The garrison draws twelve amphorae per day, same as the original survey."

"Then why is the cistern at this level?"

The tribune points. The waterline sits a hand's breadth above the intake channel — the channel that feeds the bathhouse, the hospital, and the officers' mess. The cistern should be full; it is the wet season, and six producing wells should overfill it.

"Show me the well-heads," the tribune says.

They walk the perimeter. The first well has a rope and bucket; water comes up murky but present. The second, the same. The third well's rope is dry. The tribune lowers a stone on a cord and counts. No splash. He tries the fourth. Dry. The fifth. Dry. Four of the six wells are exhausted. The daily twelve amphorae the garrison has been reporting comes from two wells, not six. The praefectus has been recording all six as active because the well-heads are maintained — ropes, pulleys, covers — though only two still reach water.

"You have been garrisoning four hundred men on two wells," the tribune says. "Your supply margin does not exist."

> A = Roman military tribune; B = the garrison has six producing wells supplying adequate water (twelve amphorae per day, per the praefectus's reports — basis of the garrison's supply margin); E = direct testing of well-heads shows four of six wells are dry — only two produce water; B' = the garrison's water supply comes from two wells, not six — the reported supply margin is fictional and the garrison is critically undersupplied. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Hi1-3.** I am the chief assayer of the Royal Mint in the third year of Henry VIII's coinage, and I have staked my professional reputation on the fineness of the new silver issue. Every test I have run on the bullion delivered to the Tower has met the sterling standard — eleven ounces two pennyweight of fine silver per pound troy. I have certified four shipments on this basis. This afternoon I am training a new apprentice on the cupellation furnace when he makes an error: he heats the sample too long, past the point where the lead separates, and the bead that remains is far smaller than it should be. I repeat the test myself, carefully, on the same ingot. The bead is small again. I test a second ingot from the current shipment. Smaller still. I return to the ingots I certified last month and pull a sample from each. The cupellation results are consistent: the silver content is eight ounces per pound troy, not eleven. The bullion I have certified as sterling for four shipments has been adulterated, and my own assay procedure — the one I have used for every certification — has been producing false readings because I have been heating the samples to a temperature too low to fully separate the base metal. My apprentice's mistake, running the furnace too hot, performed the assay correctly. My method has been wrong, and the coinage I guaranteed is debased.

> A = chief assayer of the Royal Mint; B = the new silver coinage meets the sterling standard (eleven ounces two pennyweight fine silver per pound troy, certified across four shipments); E = an apprentice's accidental over-heating in cupellation produces a small bead; repeating the test correctly on current and past ingots consistently shows only eight ounces per pound troy; B' = the bullion is adulterated and the assayer's own standard procedure was conducted at too low a temperature to detect the debasement — all four certifications are invalid. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Hi1-4.** The morning post brings a letter to Florence Nightingale at Scutari, January 1855. It is from Dr. John Hall, the principal medical officer, and it is not addressed to her — it was enclosed, by accident or carelessness, in a packet of supply requisitions meant for her stores. The letter is addressed to Sidney Herbert at the War Office. In it, Dr. Hall reports that the mortality rate among wounded soldiers at Scutari has fallen steadily since October and attributes the improvement to his surgical protocols. Nightingale has her own figures. She has been tabulating deaths by cause on a ward-by-ward basis every week since her arrival. Her tabulation shows the opposite: mortality has risen, not fallen, and the primary cause is not surgical failure but infectious disease contracted inside the hospital itself. She compares the two accounts. Hall's figure counts only soldiers who die on the operating table. Her figure counts every soldier who enters the hospital and does not leave alive. Hall is reporting surgical mortality. She is measuring hospital mortality. The number reaching the War Office — the number shaping policy, supply allocation, and the decision about whether to expand or close the facility — excludes the deaths that are driving the actual crisis.

> A = Florence Nightingale; B = (implicit, as the starting assumption she is about to revise) that the mortality figures reaching the War Office reflect the hospital's actual death toll; E = Dr. Hall's misdirected letter showing he reports only surgical-table deaths to the War Office, while her own ward-by-ward tabulation shows rising overall mortality driven by in-hospital infection; B' = the War Office's mortality data excludes the dominant cause of death (nosocomial infection) — policy decisions are being made on a metric that systematically undercounts the crisis. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.

**Di-Hi1-5.** DEPOSITION OF CAPTAIN JAMES COLQUHOUN, taken before the Admiralty Board of Inquiry, Portsmouth, 14th August 1797.

I was appointed to command HMS Resolute in March of this year, relieving Captain Harding, who had held the ship for three years. I received from Captain Harding his muster book, which listed one hundred and sixty-four able seamen, forty-two ordinary seamen, and twenty-one marines. I reported these figures to the Port Admiral and drew provisions accordingly. On the 9th of June, preparing the ship for Channel patrol, I ordered all hands mustered on deck for inspection. I counted the men myself. Able seamen present: ninety-one. Ordinary seamen: twenty-six. Marines: fourteen. I sent my first lieutenant below to account for the sick, the watch, and any man with legitimate absence. He returned with a list of eleven men in the sick berth and four on harbour watch. The remaining ninety-one names on the muster book corresponded to no living person aboard. I examined the pay records and found wages drawn against each of the ninety-one names through the most recent quarter. Captain Harding's muster book, which I had accepted as accurate and on which I had based my crew strength, my watch rotations, and my readiness report to the Admiral, carried ninety-one fictitious entries, each drawing pay.

> A = Captain James Colquhoun; B = HMS Resolute is crewed at full complement per the inherited muster book (164 able seamen, 42 ordinary, 21 marines — the basis of his readiness report and provisions); E = on-deck muster count and subsequent accounting show 91 names on the book correspond to no living person aboard, yet wages have been drawn for each; B' = the muster book is fraudulent — the ship is critically undermanned, and the previous captain was drawing pay for fictitious crewmen. ✓ all four roles, ✓ all three states.
