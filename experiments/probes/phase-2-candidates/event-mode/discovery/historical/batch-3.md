**Di-Hi3-1**

*[Rome, 2nd century CE — present tense]*

The grain curator Publius Aemilius Rufus stands at the river dock at Ostia and watches the unloading. He has administered the annona for seven years; the fleet returns from Alexandria in late August, and by his reckoning — his very careful reckoning, which he has given to the prefect and which the prefect has given to the emperor — this convoy carries enough grain to sustain the city through the winter and well into spring. He has made his estimate from the manifest totals that arrived by courier three weeks ago: forty-two ships, each at standard lading. He begins to count as the vessels come in. He reaches fourteen before he notices that the hull-marks on the fifteenth vessel match the hull-marks he already logged on the third. He returns to the dock register and checks. Twelve of the forty-two ships on the manifest are the same twelve vessels, tallied twice — once as they were loaded in Alexandria and a second time as they passed the lighthouse at Pharos, each listing producing a separate entry in the courier's total. Twenty-six ships, not forty-two. The winter calculation he has given to the prefect is built on a phantom convoy that does not arrive.

**Di-Hi3-2**

"The inoculation protects against the confluent form," Dr Letitia Marsham said. She was addressing the board of the Foundling Hospital, London, and it was the winter of 1796. "I have administered it to sixty-three children in the eastern ward over the past fourteen months. Not one has taken the confluent form during the two outbreaks since."

"And the discrete form?" asked the board's physician-in-ordinary.

She paused. "The inoculation protects against smallpox. It protects against both forms."

"I ask because I examined six of your ward children last Tuesday, Mrs Marsham — I beg your pardon, Dr Marsham. Three of the six showed healed pock-scars consistent with discrete-form presentation. They had not been reported to the register."

She asked to see his examination notes. He produced them. Three children, inoculated in the first week of her trial, each with healed scarring on the face and forearms dating — by the tissue pattern — to eight or ten months prior. They had contracted the discrete form and recovered, and the ward staff had not entered it as a smallpox case because the children had survived and the confluent form had not presented. Her study had been measuring lethality, not infection.

> A = Dr Letitia Marsham; B = the inoculation protects against smallpox in both its confluent and discrete forms — no inoculated child has contracted the disease; E = physician-in-ordinary's examination notes showing three inoculated children with healed pock-scars from discrete-form smallpox that occurred during the trial period and were not reported; B′ = the inoculation may protect against the lethal confluent form but does not prevent discrete-form infection; the trial was measuring lethality, not infection incidence. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Hi3-3**

I had commanded the third company of the 42nd at Quatre Bras and I knew what I had done there. My men had held the crossroads from two in the afternoon until darkness, and when the regimental account was submitted to the Horse Guards I had given the casualty figure myself: forty-one other ranks killed or wounded, seven officers. That number went into the dispatch. Three days after Waterloo, in a barn outside Nivelles where my company was billeted during the first chaos of the pursuit, the paymaster brought the muster roll for me to certify. I went through it man by man. When I reached the end of the roll I had counted eighty-six names marked dead, wounded, or missing since the fourteenth. I went back through the roll assuming a clerical error. It was not a clerical error. In the smoke and the movement of the afternoon at Quatre Bras, I had not seen what was happening at the flanks of my own company. The figure I had given to the colonel — the figure the Horse Guards had dispatched to London — had counted only the men I could see from the centre of the line.

> A = company commander (I), 42nd Regiment; B = the company's casualties at Quatre Bras were forty-one other ranks and seven officers; E = muster roll certification showing eighty-six names marked dead, wounded, or missing since the fourteenth — nearly double the submitted figure; B′ = actual casualties were approximately double the reported number; the submitted figure counted only men visible from the centre of the line and missed flank casualties. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Hi3-4**

The ship's surgeon aboard the barque *Medora*, twelve days out of Liverpool in October 1847, believed the fever in the forward hold was ship fever — typhus, the emigrant ships' affliction, carried in the lice that no amount of sulphur fumigation could fully suppress. He had seen it four crossings running and he had prepared accordingly: isolating the afflicted, distributing the vinegar wash, writing the daily morbidity count in the medical log. On the twelfth day he was summoned to examine a new case and found, for the first time, the characteristic rice-water stool. He stood over the bunk and looked at the presentation and then went to his case notes for the prior eleven cases. He read them with the new possibility in front of him. Eight of the eleven had described the same symptom in the margins, where he had recorded their complaints in their own words — he had noted it as a secondary digestive disturbance consistent with poor shipboard rations and had not returned to it. The fever was not typhus. The epidemic spreading through the forward hold was cholera, and it moved by a different pathway entirely and required different containment.

> A = ship's surgeon of the *Medora*; B = the forward hold epidemic is typhus (ship fever), spread by lice; E = presenting rice-water stool on the twelfth case, combined with re-reading prior case notes showing the same symptom in eight earlier patients, recorded as a minor digestive complaint; B′ = the epidemic is cholera, not typhus — transmitted by a different pathway, requiring different containment measures. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states

**Di-Hi3-5**

The tax commissioner for the district of Edirne, in the spring of 1683, sat down with the household registers from the last imperial census to calculate the expected yield from the poll-tax. The registers gave him 4,400 taxable households. He had used that figure in his preliminary report to the Porte and had promised a collection accordingly. The sub-district headmen came in on the third morning of the collection to submit their village tallies. He added the tallies as they came in, expecting the numbers to approach his projection. By the end of the third morning he had tallies from all eighteen sub-districts, and the total was 2,960 households. He sent for three of the headmen whose tallies differed most from the register and questioned each separately. All three gave the same account: since the census of the prior decade, plague had taken an estimated third of the adult male population in the eastern villages, and the subsequent population movement had been noted in the local waqf records but had never been transmitted to the provincial capital for re-enumeration. The census he had been issued was a decade old and had never been corrected. He had promised the Porte a collection from households that no longer existed.

> A = tax commissioner for the district of Edirne; B = the district contains 4,400 taxable households (per imperial census registers); E = headmen's village tallies totalling 2,960 households, confirmed by three separate headmen citing decade-old plague deaths and population movement recorded only in local waqf records; B′ = the taxable population is approximately 2,960 households, not 4,400; the census registers are a decade out of date and were never corrected for plague mortality. ✓ all roles, ✓ all states
