# Reversal × Sci-fi — Batch 5 (Tier B)

**R-Sf5-1.** The decommissioning sequence is already running. Station Acheron-9, a deep-space relay platform at the edge of the Oort shell, has been offline for eleven months. Its fusion core is cycling down in controlled stages — the automated process takes seventy-two hours and cannot be interrupted once begun. Relay Coordinator Vasquez watches the telemetry feed from the regional hub, two light-hours away, as the core temperature drops through each threshold. At hour fifty-eight, with the core fourteen hours from permanent shutdown, her console flags an incoming transmission routed through Acheron-9's antenna array. The signal is not from any registered vessel or beacon. It is structured, repeating, and originates from a bearing that contains no catalogued objects. Vasquez runs the signal through three filters. It is not noise. It is not echo. The pattern is too regular for natural emission and too complex for a simple transponder. She reaches for the abort command on the decommissioning sequence. The system informs her that the abort window closed at hour forty-eight. Acheron-9's antenna — the only receiver oriented toward the signal's origin — will go dark in fourteen hours, and there is no other relay within range. The signal continues to arrive on her screen, structured and patient, and she can do nothing but record it.

**R-Sf5-2.** "It's malignant," Dr. Wen says, pulling off the imaging glove. "Metastatic. Stage four equivalent on the Callisto scale." She is standing in the medbay of the generation ship *Perseverance*, looking at the scan of Chief Botanist Ade Okonkwo's left lung. The tumour has spread to the lymphatic analogues. On Earth, or at any orbital station with a fabrication bay, a targeted nanophage culture could be grown in six hours. The *Perseverance* does not have a fabrication bay. It has a surgical suite, broad-spectrum radiation, and drugs that were loaded sixty years ago at departure. Wen has already begun composing the palliative-care protocol when the ship's pharmacist calls her on the intercom. During a routine inventory of deep-storage bay seven — a bay that has not been opened since launch — he found a sealed crate mislabelled as agricultural supplements. Inside: forty-eight vials of programmable nanophage base culture, with a shelf life of two hundred years, packed by a medical officer whose name appears nowhere in the crew manifest. Wen holds the vial up to the medbay light. The culture is viable. She begins programming the targeting sequence for Okonkwo's tumour.

**R-Sf5-3.** I had the coordinates locked and the burn plotted. Fourteen months of prospecting in the belt — scanning, sampling, moving on — and I had finally tagged an asteroid with enough rare-earth oxides to pay off my ship and fund three more expeditions. My claim beacon was broadcasting on the registered frequency. I transmitted the assay data to the Ceres Mining Authority and received confirmation of a provisional claim, valid for ninety standard days pending physical extraction. I pointed my ship toward Ceres to file the permanent claim and hire a drilling crew. I was calculating the profit margin — conservatively, four hundred percent return on fuel costs alone — when my nav system flagged a new entry in the Authority's public registry. Someone else had filed a prior claim on the same body. Filed it two days before mine, from a transponder I had never detected. A prospector named Gaida, operating a ship too small to carry a drill rig, had tagged the asteroid on a flyby and transmitted a claim I never saw. My provisional status was void. The Authority's confirmation message was already greyed out on my console, replaced by a single line: CLAIM SUPERSEDED.

The board recommended permanent revocation of colony citizenship and deportation to the orbital transit station for reassignment.*

*Evidence presented by the Conduct Board was entered without objection. Ms. Torvik's assigned advocate presented no witnesses and filed no counter-evidence, noting for the record that Ms. Torvik declined to participate in her own defence.*

Lund further stated that the seven quota violations arose from Ms. Torvik diverting her personal water allocation to seedling beds that are now the colony's primary source of cold-weather protein crops.*

*The tribunal denies the petition for revocation. Ms. Torvik retains full citizenship. The Conduct Board is directed to review its quota-infraction criteria in light of this testimony.*

**R-Sf5-5.** The rescue shuttle is close enough now that Ensign Kaur can read its hull markings through the viewport of the damaged freighter. She has been drifting for nine hours since the engine failure, her distress beacon pulsing on the emergency band. The shuttle — dispatched from Waystation Tau — is decelerating on approach, running lights visible, hailing on the standard frequency. Kaur has already suited up and depressurised the cargo hold for transfer. She responds to the hail: "Copy, Tau Rescue. Ready for docking." The shuttle pilot's voice comes back, but the words are wrong. "Freighter nine-seven, we are reading a contamination flag on your hull — biohazard class three, tagged by an automated buoy we passed forty minutes ago. Quarantine protocol is in effect. We cannot dock or approach within one hundred metres. Confirm your last port of call." Kaur confirms: Refinery Station Gant, where a cargo of industrial solvents was loaded. The shuttle pilot is quiet for a moment, then: "Gant is under quarantine lockdown as of six hours ago. Bacterial bloom in their water supply. Your hull was flagged on departure. We're going to hold at distance and relay to Waystation Tau for instructions. Do not — repeat, do not — open your docking port." The shuttle hangs in the void, close enough to touch, and goes no closer. Kaur stands in her suit in the depressurised hold, watching it through the viewport.
