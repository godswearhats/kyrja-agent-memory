# Reversal × Sci-fi — Batch 1 (Tier A)

**R-Sf1-1.** The colony transport *Adelheid* drifts at four kilometres per second toward the orbital insertion point for Kepler-442b, carrying eleven hundred sleepers in cryogenic suspension. Chief Navigator Yuen watches the approach telemetry on the bridge. Every instrument reads nominal — trajectory within two-hundredths of a degree, fuel reserves at eighteen percent above minimum, the orbital corridor cleared by the advance probe six months ago. The colony directorate on Earth transmitted their final confirmation yesterday: the landing site is prepared, the atmospheric processors are cycling, the first greenhouse domes have been pressurised. Yuen has already composed her arrival broadcast. She reaches for the transmit key. The ship's autonomous collision system fires a full lateral burn without warning, slamming Yuen against her restraints. The main display redraws. Where the cleared orbital corridor was, a dense field of debris now fills the insertion path — fragments of the advance probe, of the atmospheric processors, of the greenhouse domes. The planet's surface readings have changed. Sulphur dioxide at forty times the level recorded six months ago. The colony site does not exist. The autonomous system has pushed the *Adelheid* into a parking orbit that will hold for ninety-two hours on remaining fuel. After that, there is nowhere to go.

**R-Sf1-2.** "It's breathing," Osei says. He pulls up the biosensor feed on the sterile-field display. The xenobiological sample — a filament structure recovered from the subsurface ocean of Enceladus — has been inert for the entire fourteen-month transit back to Ceres Station. Every test the team ran in transit returned the same result: dead tissue. No metabolic signature, no chemical exchange, no response to stimuli across eleven protocols. Osei filed his final report two days ago: "Sample ENC-2247 is non-viable. Recommend archival and return of lab allocation to active projects." The department head approved the decommission order this morning. The sample was scheduled for cryogenic storage in forty minutes.

"It's not breathing," Lian says from across the lab. "It's been dead for a year."

"Come look at this."

Lian crosses to the display. The filament is moving. Not drifting — contracting and expanding in a rhythmic cycle, four seconds on, four seconds off. The biosensor shows a metabolic signature where yesterday there was none. Gas exchange. Thermal output. The sample is alive. It has been alive, the sensor logs now suggest, for approximately six hours — since the lab's environmental system cycled to Ceres Station's local atmospheric mix after docking.

Lian reaches past Osei and cancels the decommission order.

**R-Sf1-3.** I had thirty-one seconds of air left when I stopped kicking. My suit's rebreather had failed during the EVA — a seal rupture in the secondary CO2 scrubber that cascaded into the primary. The tether had snapped when the station's solar array pivoted during a scheduled reorientation that nobody told me about. I was drifting at half a metre per second away from the station, which is not fast until you consider that nothing out here slows you down. My suit radio was working. I had used it to tell Commander Vasquez that I was going to die, and she had confirmed this with the kind of calm that astronauts use when the math is settled: the station's rescue drone was powered down for maintenance, the secondary airlock was cycling a cargo transfer and could not be interrupted, and no one on board had a suit rated for unscheduled EVA. Thirty-one seconds. Then twenty-four. Then the hull beside the cargo airlock blew outward — a controlled decompression, manual, someone inside had vented the cargo bay. Vasquez. She had dumped eighteen tonnes of atmospheric pressure into space and the blast wave caught me like a sail. I hit the station's outer hull at a speed that cracked two ribs and knocked me unconscious. I woke up inside the cargo bay. The inner door was sealed. Someone had repressurised. My suit timer read zero, but I was breathing station air through a cracked faceplate.

**R-Sf1-4.** AUTOMATED LOG — TERRAFORM CONTROL, KEPLER-62F NORTHERN STATION
TIMESTAMP: SOL 1,447 / 03:22 LOCAL

Atmospheric conversion has stalled. O2 concentration has held at 4.1% for 340 sols despite continuous reactor output. The microbial seeding program, which was projected to push concentration past the 8% breathability threshold by sol 1,200, has produced no measurable change since sol 1,107. Director Holst transmitted her assessment to the Colonial Authority on sol 1,440: "Terraforming of the northern hemisphere has failed. The microbial cultures are not propagating in the regolith. Recommend evacuation of Northern Station and reallocation of reactor fuel to Southern Station, which remains on trajectory." Evacuation transports are scheduled for sol 1,450. Packing orders have been issued to all 214 station personnel.

AMENDMENT — SOL 1,447 / 11:06 LOCAL

Sensor grid detected a step-change in atmospheric O2 at 10:47 local. Concentration jumped from 4.1% to 6.3% in nineteen minutes. The microbial cultures are propagating — not in the surface regolith, where the seeding program targeted them, but in a subsurfaceite layer sixty metres below the station that the original geological survey did not map. Gas spectrometry confirms biogenic oxygen. Projection models, recalculated with subsurface propagation rates, place the 8% threshold at sol 1,580. Director Holst has cancelled the evacuation order. The packing crates are being unloaded.

**R-Sf1-5.** The quarantine officer can see the freighter through the observation port — the *Huang He*, bulk carrier, forty-two crew, inbound from the Jovian processing platforms with a hold full of refined helium-3. It has been sitting at the quarantine perimeter for nine hours while the station's biosecurity AI analyses the crew health telemetry. The *Huang He*'s captain has been transmitting increasingly agitated messages: his fuel margins are thin, his crew is overdue for rotation, and his cargo contract includes a penalty clause that activates in six hours. The quarantine officer understands. She also understands what the biosecurity AI has flagged: a respiratory anomaly in three crew members that matches the early signature profile of the Callisto hemorrhagic pathogen — the one that killed four hundred people on Rhea Station two years ago. She has drafted the denial-of-entry order. She is reviewing it one final time when the AI updates its analysis. The respiratory anomaly is not pathogenic. It is an artefact of the *Huang He*'s atmospheric recycler, which uses a non-standard carbon filter that produces a distinctive and harmless metabolic signature in long-haul crew. The AI has reclassified the flag from BIOHAZARD to EQUIPMENT VARIANCE. The quarantine officer deletes the denial order and transmits docking clearance. The *Huang He* begins its approach burn.
