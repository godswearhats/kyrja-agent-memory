**R-Hi6-1**

The creditors are in the outer room. There are four of them — a cloth merchant, two factors from the Medici bank, and a notary who has come to attach the furniture — and they have been waiting since Prime. Fra Giacomo knows what they are waiting for. The Priory of San Benedetto al Monte has carried a debt of six hundred florins since the last failed harvest, and the prior has tried four times to negotiate extension, and four times the Medici factors have declined. This morning is the final call. The prior is in the chapel. Fra Giacomo stands at the door of the outer room, preparing to admit the creditors, when a lay brother arrives at a run from the road: a rider from Florence, bearing a sealed letter from the Abbot General. Fra Giacomo takes it inside. The prior reads it, then reads it again, then stands. He comes to the outer room himself. "Gentlemen," he says, "the debt has been retired." The Abbot General, it emerges, has transferred funds from the order's Roman account against the full principal. The notary's pen does not touch the furniture ledger. The creditors leave without the prior's signature on any attachment.

**R-Hi6-2**

"Will they hold?" Captain Vargas asks.

The sergeant looks at the slope. It is Talavera, the afternoon of the twenty-eighth of July, 1809, and the ridge they are defending is the one Wellesley said could not be given up. The French columns — Sebastiani's division — are two hundred yards out and coming fast. The British battalions on the right have already begun to give ground. Vargas can see the gap opening. "They will not hold," the sergeant says. This is not pessimism; it is arithmetic. The gap is fifty yards wide and growing. Vargas orders the reserve company forward to plug it. He watches them go. Then from the right, from behind the French column, there is artillery — British artillery, three guns that had been counted as overrun, their crews apparently neither dead nor captured, who have somehow brought the pieces back into action on the French flank. Sebastiani's column hesitates. The hesitation is enough. The gap stops widening. The ridge holds.

**R-Hi6-3**

I had rehearsed my testimony for three weeks. It was not testimony in my favour — I had none to give — but testimony in favour of my master, Captain Aldridge, who was before the court-martial for the loss of HMS *Resolute* on the rocks off Nantucket in February 1743. I knew what had happened: the first mate had given a wrong bearing in heavy fog, and the captain had trusted it, and the ship was lost. I also knew that courts-martial do not often forgive captains for trusting wrong bearings. The court assembled in the great cabin of the *Yarmouth* at ten in the morning. I was the third witness called. I gave my account. The court president — Admiral Fortescue, a man whose expression I could not read — asked me one question: whether the first mate had informed the captain of his uncertainty about the bearing. I said: no, sir, he had stated it as certain. The court recessed. When it returned, the verdict named the first mate, not Captain Aldridge. The captain was restored to his rank without censure. I had not expected this. Neither had Aldridge — he looked at the document as though he could not parse it.

> A = Captain Aldridge; G = survive the court-martial without losing his rank; X = cashiered / censured (ship lost on his watch, courts-martial rarely acquit captains in such circumstances); ¬X = fully acquitted, first mate found responsible, rank restored without censure. [Setting: Royal Navy, 1743.] ✓ polar binary on censured-vs-acquitted axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi6-4**

*Deposition of Wŏn Yŏng-sik, merchant of Kaesŏng, taken before the Board of Punishments, third month of the fifteenth year of Sejong, as recorded by the board's clerk:*

*I had come before the board to answer a charge of selling adulterated silk to an official of the Ministry of Rites. The adulteration charge carried, as I understood, a penalty of flogging and confiscation of goods, and my counsel had told me plainly that the board's precedents gave me little prospect of acquittal, as the inspector's seal had been affixed to the complaint and the ministry official was of the fifth rank. I prepared my family accordingly. When the board convened, the presiding magistrate called the ministry official before my turn to speak. The official recanted the complaint in full. He stated that his own steward had substituted the silk after it left my hands, and that this had come to light through an accounting discrepancy discovered by the Ministry's own clerks. The complaint was withdrawn. The board directed that my goods, which had been held at the customs house, be returned to me without condition. I left the board chamber with my goods intact and my back unstripes.*

> A = Wŏn Yŏng-sik (merchant); G = avoid flogging and confiscation; X = convicted (inspector's seal on complaint, fifth-rank official as accuser, counsel gave poor prospects); ¬X = complaint withdrawn, goods returned, fully exonerated. [Setting: Joseon Korea, Sejong's reign, 15th century.] ✓ polar binary on convicted-vs-acquitted axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi6-5**

The Paris Salon jury meets in the anteroom of the Grand Palais, 1905. Édouard Flessart stands in the corridor with the other rejected painters, each of them carrying the card that says *refusé*, each of them too proud or too numb to put it away. Flessart has been refused three consecutive years. His style — heavy impasto, colour unmediated by academic shading — has been called juvenile by one juror in print. He has come this year not with hope but because his dealer told him the application fee was already paid and there was nothing else to do. From the anteroom comes a voice: one of the junior jury members, hurrying out, who stops when he sees Flessart's name on the card. "There's been a correction," the man says. He takes the card. "Lecomte misread the ballot. Your landscape — the one with the ochre field — was accepted. Four votes to two." He produces a different card. Flessart reads it twice, and the word on it is *admis*, and he is still holding the refusal card in his other hand.

> A = Flessart (painter); G = have his painting accepted to the Salon; X = rejected (refused three years running, style publicly criticised, submitted without expectation); ¬X = accepted, ballot miscount corrected. [Setting: Paris, 1905.] ✓ polar binary on rejected-vs-accepted axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.
