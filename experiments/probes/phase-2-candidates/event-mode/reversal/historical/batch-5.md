# Reversal × Historical — Batch 5 (Tier B)

**R-Hi5-1.** The magistrate sits in the tribunal hall of Edo, autumn of 1702, brushing the final characters of the sentencing order. The accused — a minor samurai named Takeda Jiro — kneels on the wooden floor with his hands bound, charged with drawing his blade within the grounds of a temple. Three witnesses testified. The temple's head priest identified Takeda by name and described the cut that scarred the sliding door of the inner hall. The magistrate's secretary has already prepared the execution order for the district headsman. Takeda has not spoken in his own defence; to do so without invitation would compound his disgrace. The magistrate sets down his brush and asks, as form requires, whether the accused has any final statement. Takeda speaks for the first time: the man he cut was a known thief who had entered the temple to rob the offertory. The head priest shifts on his cushion. The magistrate asks the priest directly whether anything was reported stolen from the temple that month. The priest is silent for a long time. Then he admits that the offertory box was found empty the following morning and that he did not report the loss. The magistrate lifts the sentencing order, reads it, and tears it in half.

> A = Takeda Jiro; G = avoid execution / be acquitted; X = executed (three witnesses, priest's identification, bound and kneeling, execution order prepared, no defence offered); ¬X = charges collapsed, sentencing order destroyed (new testimony reveals the act was justified, priest's concealment exposed). ✓ polar binary on condemned-vs-acquitted axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi5-2.** "She will not see you," the lady-in-waiting says through the half-open door. It is Hampton Court, January 1536, and Thomas Cromwell stands in the corridor outside Queen Anne's apartments, a sheaf of papers under his arm. He has come to present the queen with a draft of the new poor-relief statute for her endorsement — a formality, but one that signals her continued influence over domestic policy. The lady-in-waiting does not meet his eyes. "Her Grace is indisposed." Cromwell knows what indisposed means in this corridor. He has heard the whispers since Christmas: the king dined with the Seymour family twice this week. The queen's miscarriage three days ago is known to every servant in the palace. Cromwell turns to leave. Before he reaches the end of the corridor, a page catches his sleeve. The king requires him. Now. In the privy chamber. Cromwell enters and finds Henry alone, standing at the window. "I want a solution," the king says. "To the marriage." He does not say which marriage. He does not need to. Cromwell came to the palace that morning believing the queen's position weakened but intact. He leaves knowing it is finished.

> A = Queen Anne Boleyn (via Cromwell as observer); G = maintain her position as queen; X = position weakened but intact (Cromwell bringing routine policy document for her endorsement, still treated as queen); ¬X = position finished (king demands dissolution of the marriage). ✓ polar binary on retains-position-vs-loses-position axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi5-3.** I am the last bidder standing, and the auctioneer knows it. It is the Caffè degli Inglesi in Rome, 1764, and I have spent six months tracking this piece — a marble torso attributed to Praxiteles, dug from a vineyard outside Tivoli. The other English collectors dropped out at two hundred scudi. Lord Pemberton, my only real competitor, left for Naples a week ago. I am alone in the front row. The reserve is three hundred. I have four hundred in a letter of credit from my banker in Florence. The auctioneer opens the bidding. I raise my hand at three hundred and ten. Silence. I wait for the hammer. The auctioneer pauses, looks past me, and nods. A voice from the back of the room — in Italian, not English — offers five hundred. I turn. A man I do not recognise, seated with the papal antiquary. The auctioneer looks at me. I have nothing above four hundred. The hammer falls at five hundred scudi to the unknown bidder. The Praxiteles torso passes to a Roman collection. I sit with my letter of credit in my coat and my empty seat in the front row.

> A = I (the English collector); G = acquire the Praxiteles torso at auction; X = wins the auction (only bidder left, competitor departed, holds sufficient credit above reserve, no opposition visible); ¬X = loses the auction (late anonymous bidder backed by papal antiquary outbids him beyond his means). ✓ polar binary on wins-vs-loses axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi5-4.** *From the log of the barque Dorado, anchored off Recife, Brazil, 14th of March, 1648, kept by the ship's master, Pieter van den Hoek:*

*This morning the Governor's secretary came aboard and inspected our cargo — four hundred casks of refined sugar, bound for Amsterdam per the charter of the West India Company. He affixed the Company seal to the manifest and pronounced the shipment cleared for departure. The crew has loaded fresh water and provisions for eight weeks. The wind is fair and south-easterly. I gave the order to weigh anchor at the turn of the afternoon watch.*

*At two bells of the afternoon watch, a pinnace flying the Governor's pennant pulled alongside. The secretary returned, accompanied by two soldiers. He presented a written order revoking the clearance. A Portuguese fleet of twelve sail has been sighted off Cabo de Santo Agostinho, less than a day's sailing to the south. The Governor has ordered all Company vessels to remain in harbour and prepare for possible siege. The crew has re-set the anchor. The sugar remains in the hold. We do not sail.*

> A = Pieter van den Hoek / the barque Dorado; G = depart Recife with the sugar cargo for Amsterdam; X = departure proceeds (cargo cleared, Company seal affixed, provisions loaded, fair wind, anchor-weighing ordered); ¬X = departure revoked, ship confined to harbour (Portuguese fleet sighted, siege preparation ordered). ✓ polar binary on departs-vs-confined axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Hi5-5.** The coffin maker has already taken the measurements. It is a cholera ward in Calcutta, 1832, and Dr. James Anstruther leans over the cot of a Bengali clerk named Ramprasad Ghosh, whose pulse he can no longer find. Ghosh has not taken water in two days. His skin is cold, his eyes are sunken to the orbits, and his limbs have the blue-grey colour that Anstruther's training at Edinburgh taught him to associate with the final stage. The orderly has pulled the sheet to the man's chin. Anstruther is writing the death certificate — name, age, cause — when the pen stops. Ghosh's hand is moving. Not a spasm; Anstruther has seen enough spasms this month to know the difference. The fingers are reaching for the cup of rice water on the side table. Anstruther grabs the man's wrist. There is a pulse. Faint, irregular, but present. Ghosh opens his eyes. "Water," he says, in English. Anstruther tears the death certificate in half and calls the orderly back.

> A = Ramprasad Ghosh (and Dr. Anstruther as attending physician); G = survive cholera; X = dies (no pulse detectable, not taken water in two days, terminal presentation, coffin measured, death certificate being written); ¬X = revives (pulse returns, patient conscious and speaking, death certificate destroyed). ✓ polar binary on dies-vs-survives axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.
