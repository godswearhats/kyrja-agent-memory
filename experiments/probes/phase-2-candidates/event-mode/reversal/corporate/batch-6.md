**R-Co6-1**

The severance paperwork is already in Hargrove's outbox. HR had sent him the template three days ago — the standard termination package for a Director of Engineering, twelve weeks plus COBRA — and he had filled it in and printed it without being asked, because that was the kind of manager he was, and because Marcus Chen had missed four consecutive sprint commitments, the last one in front of the CTO. Hargrove calls Marcus into the conference room at nine. He sets the folder on the table between them, face down. He does not open it. Before he can speak, Marcus opens his laptop and turns it toward him. A dashboard. Real-time numbers. "The Lattice migration," Marcus says. "It completed at two this morning. Six weeks ahead of schedule. The infrastructure team asked me not to say anything until it was confirmed stable." The dashboard shows green across every node. The CTO had flagged it in Slack at 6 a.m. — Hargrove had not yet seen the notification. The folder stays face down on the table.

> A = Hargrove (manager); G = terminate Marcus Chen's employment; X = termination executed (paperwork filled and printed, meeting called, folder on the table); ¬X = termination abandoned (Lattice migration completed successfully, CTO recognition rendered termination impossible). ✓ polar binary on terminated-vs-retained axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Co6-2**

"Tell me the number," Priya says.

The account manager, Yusuf, looks at his phone. "Still nothing. It was supposed to hit at close of business yesterday."

"We know. The wire didn't clear. We're assuming it didn't clear." Priya has been assuming this since eight o'clock. The transfer from Helix Ventures — the Series B tranche, four-point-one million — was due by end of day Thursday. It is now nine-fifteen on Friday morning. The payroll run is at noon. She has a bridge loan term sheet open on her other monitor, and the founder's personal guarantee drafted, and she has spent the last hour doing the arithmetic on what a missed payroll does to the cap table conversation. "Check again."

Yusuf looks at his phone. His face changes.

"It cleared," he says. "Six-forty-seven this morning. The full amount."

Priya does not close the bridge loan term sheet for another minute. She just looks at the number.

> A = Priya (CFO / company); G = receive the Series B tranche in time to make payroll; X = wire failed to clear (overdue by a business day, payroll at risk, bridge loan being arranged); ¬X = full transfer cleared that morning. ✓ polar binary on failed-vs-cleared axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Co6-4**

The appeals panel convenes at two in the afternoon. Delphine has been through three rounds of performance management in eighteen months, and she knows how panels like this go — the HR business partner reads the timeline, the line manager confirms the warnings, the panellist from another department asks a clarifying question and then votes with the line manager, and the outcome is ratified. She has retained her own employment solicitor, who is not permitted in the room. She has a folder of evidence: emails, Slack logs, a recording she made on her phone. The HR business partner reads the timeline. The line manager confirms the warnings. The panellist from another department — a woman named Okonkwo, whose name Delphine did not know before today — asks to see the project attribution records from Q3. There is a long silence after she sees them. Okonkwo says: "These outputs are credited to the wrong person." She turns to the line manager. "All of them." The panel does not vote. The HR business partner adjourns the meeting. When they return, it is not Delphine's employment that is under review.

> A = Delphine; G = retain her employment through the appeals panel; X = dismissal upheld (three rounds of performance management, panel voting pattern established, outcome expected); ¬X = dismissal reversed, line manager's conduct under review instead. ✓ polar binary on dismissed-vs-retained axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Co6-5**

I had done the math on my own redundancy six weeks before the announcement. The reorg had been telegraphed since Q2 — three product lines collapsing into one, my role duplicated by the incoming GM's existing team, my headcount not in the new org chart that had been circulating in draft. I knew what the envelope on my desk was when I walked in on a Tuesday morning and found it. I picked it up. I opened it. It was not a notice of redundancy. It was a revised employment contract: new title, Director of Consolidated Product, expanded remit, twenty-two percent pay increase. A handwritten note from the incoming GM was clipped to the front. *Your tribal knowledge of this product family cannot be replaced. I asked the board to create this position specifically. Please confirm acceptance by Friday.* I stood at my desk for a long time before I sat down.

> A = I (product director); G = keep my position through the reorg; X = made redundant (role duplicated, org chart circulated without my name, envelope found on desk); ¬X = offered new directorship created specifically for me at higher pay. ✓ polar binary on redundant-vs-retained axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.
