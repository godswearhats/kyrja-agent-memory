# Reversal × Corporate — Batch 2 (Tier B)

**R-Co2-1**

The integration is dead. Nakamura's engineering team has been trying to close the API partnership with Conduit Systems for nine months, and the last three technical reviews have each ended with Conduit's architecture board requesting further modifications. The most recent rejection arrived Tuesday: "Fundamental incompatibility with our security model. We recommend both parties pursue alternative integration paths." Nakamura forwarded the email to her VP with a one-line summary — "Conduit is a no" — and began reassigning her engineers to other projects. She is typing the internal post-mortem when her Slack lights up. It is the Conduit CTO, writing directly. "Our board chair overruled the architecture review this morning. He wants the integration live by Q3. We're sending a signed LOI within the hour. Full scope, no modifications required on your end." Nakamura reads the message twice, then looks at the reassignment tickets she has already filed.

> A = Nakamura / engineering team; G = close the API partnership with Conduit Systems; X = partnership dead (nine months of failed reviews, explicit rejection email, engineers being reassigned, post-mortem underway); ¬X = partnership approved at full scope with signed LOI incoming. ✓ polar binary on rejected-vs-approved axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Co2-2**

"They're going to promote you," Diane says, leaning against the doorframe. "Everybody knows. Huang told me himself — the shortlist is one name long."

I nod. I have heard this from four separate people this week. The new director role was posted internally three months ago, and my skip-level told me in our last one-on-one that my candidacy was, in her words, "the obvious outcome." I have already spoken to my wife about the relocation. The moving company sent an estimate yesterday.

The all-hands is at two. Huang takes the podium and thanks the team. He reads the role title. He reads the name.

It is not mine. It is someone from the Atlanta office I have never met. Huang does not look in my direction. I sit in the third row with the moving estimate still open on my phone.

**R-Co2-4**

The layoff list is pinned to Tomas's second monitor in a spreadsheet only he and the CHRO can access. Forty-two names. His name is not on it — he built the list. He has spent the last six weeks calibrating it with legal, running the adverse-impact analysis, scheduling the notification meetings. He knows the script because he wrote it. **R-Co2-5**

The pitch deck sits closed on the table. Roshan does not open it. The three partners across from him have not touched their water glasses, have not smiled, have not asked a single follow-up question in fourteen minutes. The senior partner checked her phone twice during his market-size slide. Their associate has been typing — notes for the rejection email, Roshan assumes. He has seen this posture before, at eleven other firms over the past five months: the polite waiting-out-the-clock before the "we'll circle back." He is composing the text to his co-founder — "another pass, moving on" — under the table when the senior partner sets her phone down and says, "We'd like to lead. Twenty million. We can have a term sheet to you by Thursday." The associate stops typing. Roshan's thumbs are still on his phone screen. The text to his co-founder is unsent.

> A = Roshan (founder); G = secure funding from the VC firm; X = rejected (no engagement, phone-checking, no follow-up questions, five months of passes, rejection text being composed); ¬X = firm offers to lead the round at $20M with a term sheet by Thursday. ✓ polar binary on rejected-vs-funded axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.
