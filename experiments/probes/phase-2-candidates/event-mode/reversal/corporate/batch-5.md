# Reversal × Corporate — Batch 5 (Tier B)

**R-Co5-1.** The insurance claim sits in the adjuster's queue with a red flag and a note from the fraud detection algorithm: pattern match, 94th percentile similarity to known staged-loss filings. Dana Worthington pulls it up on her screen. The claimant — a warehouse distributor outside Toledo — reported a total loss from electrical fire. The photographs show the kind of uniform burn pattern the training module called textbook. She has already drafted the denial letter and flagged it for the Special Investigations Unit. Her supervisor signed off that morning. She is attaching the referral documents when the fire marshal's office sends its final report directly to the claims system. Independent origin confirmed: faulty transformer owned by the municipal utility, cited in three prior incidents on the same block. No accelerants. No anomalies. The utility has already accepted liability in a separate filing. Dana stares at the fraud score on her screen — 94th percentile — and opens a new claim-approval form.

**R-Co5-2.** "We're going to lose her," Priya says, closing the conference room door. "I talked to her yesterday. She's already interviewing. Two offers on the table, both above what we can match."

Marcus pulls up the retention budget on his laptop. It is empty — used in Q1 on three other departures.

"Can we get an exception from Geller?" Priya asks.

"Geller told me last week that no more exceptions are coming this fiscal year. Direct quote: 'The number is the number.'"

They sit with it. Priya begins drafting the transition plan — who absorbs which accounts, which clients need personal calls, how to frame the departure for the team. She is two paragraphs into the document when her phone buzzes. A message from the engineer in question: *Turned down both offers. Staying. I talked to my wife and we don't want to relocate. Can we just pretend the last two weeks didn't happen?*

Priya reads it aloud. Marcus closes the retention budget spreadsheet.

> A = Priya and Marcus / management team; G = retain the senior engineer; X = engineer departs (already interviewing, two competing offers, no retention budget, no exceptions available, transition plan being drafted); ¬X = engineer stays (declines both offers for personal reasons). ✓ polar binary on departs-vs-stays axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

**R-Co5-3.** I am reading the terms of the acquisition offer for the third time when the lawyer across the table clears his throat. It is a good offer. Our board unanimously recommended acceptance two days ago. The purchasing company's due diligence team finished their review last week with no material findings. Their CEO and ours shook hands at a dinner I attended. The signing copies are fanned across the mahogany in front of us — eight sets, tabbed and flagged. I am the CFO; my signature goes on pages eleven and forty-two of each. Their general counsel picks up her pen. Then her phone vibrates on the table. She looks at it. Sets the pen down. "I need to step out," she says. She is gone for four minutes. When she returns, she does not sit. "Our board has voted to withdraw the offer," she says. "Effective now. I'm not authorized to discuss the reasons." She collects the unsigned copies. I am still holding my pen.

> A = I (CFO) / the company being acquired; G = close the acquisition deal; X = deal closes (board unanimously recommended, due diligence clean, handshake dinner, signing copies laid out); ¬X = acquiring company withdraws the offer at the signing table. ✓ polar binary on deal-closes-vs-deal-withdrawn axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.

*FROM: Managing Partner K. Whitfield*

*K. Whitfield*

**R-Co5-5.** The warehouse is full. Pallets of the new product line — six months of production, pre-positioned for the holiday season — stretch from the loading docks to the back wall under halogen lights. Retail buyers from four national chains have signed purchase orders. The VP of operations walks the floor with the distribution manager, confirming the ship dates printed on the manifest board. Everything leaves Monday. The distribution manager's radio crackles. She steps away, listens, comes back. Her face is different. "That was legal," she says. "The Consumer Product Safety Commission filed an injunction thirty minutes ago. Mandatory recall. All units. Something in the coating — they're saying it fails the new lead-content threshold for children's products." She looks down the long rows of shrink-wrapped pallets. "None of this ships." The VP of operations stands in the middle of the warehouse, purchase orders in his hand, surrounded by product that now has to travel in the opposite direction.

> A = the company / VP of operations; G = ship the holiday product line to retail partners; X = product ships on schedule (warehouse full, purchase orders signed, ship dates confirmed, distribution Monday); ¬X = mandatory recall, no units ship (CPSC injunction, lead-content violation). ✓ polar binary on ships-vs-recalled axis, ✓ expectation set before resolution, ✓ inversion acknowledged within event.
