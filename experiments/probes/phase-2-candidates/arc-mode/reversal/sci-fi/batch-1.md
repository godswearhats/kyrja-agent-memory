# Reversal arc x Sci-fi -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## R-arc-Sf1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A terraforming station's chief climatologist designs a controlled atmospheric seeding sequence to raise the surface temperature of a colony world by two degrees -- the final threshold for open-air agriculture. After three years of modelling, a successful small-scale trial, and the colonial authority's formal approval, the full-scale seeding triggers a runaway albedo feedback: the ice-cap retreat exposes dark regolith that absorbs heat faster than the models predicted, and the temperature overshoots the target by eleven degrees, rendering the agricultural zones uninhabitable.

> A = chief climatologist (Dr. Yael Maron); G = raising surface temperature by exactly 2°C to enable open-air agriculture; X = controlled warming to target (success); not-X = runaway warming, agricultural zones destroyed (failure).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The operations centre of Kepler-442 Station, a prefab dome on the colony world's northern plateau. Maron stood at the display wall with the colonial governor and the station's systems engineer.

"Two degrees," Maron said. She pointed to the atmospheric model on the screen -- a three-dimensional projection of the planet's temperature gradient, surface albedo, and cloud-cover dynamics. "The surface is currently 1.8 degrees below the threshold for open-air grain cultivation. We seed the upper atmosphere with a sulfur-hexafluoride mix that traps longwave radiation in the 8-to-12 micron band. The temperature rises. Two degrees, over eighteen months. We stop seeding. The atmosphere stabilises at the new equilibrium."

The governor studied the model. "And the small-scale trial?"

"Sector Seven," Maron said. "We seeded a twenty-square-kilometre zone last year. Temperature rose 1.9 degrees over fourteen months. Stable. No overshoot. The agricultural test plots in Sector Seven produced their first viable grain harvest two months ago."

"What's the risk of overshoot at full scale?" the governor asked.

Maron pulled up the sensitivity analysis. "We modelled overshoot scenarios extensively. The primary risk factor is ice-cap albedo feedback -- if the warming melts polar ice faster than projected, the exposed surface absorbs more heat, which accelerates warming. But the model shows ice-cap retreat beginning at the three-degree mark, and we're stopping at two. We have a one-degree buffer."

The systems engineer nodded. "The dispersal drones are calibrated for eighteen months of continuous seeding. We can halt the programme at any point by grounding the fleet."

The governor signed the authorisation on the console. "Two degrees. Not three. Not two and a half. Two."

"Two," Maron confirmed.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The operations centre, nine months into the seeding programme. Maron reviewed the quarterly monitoring report with her team -- two atmospheric scientists and the systems engineer.

The numbers were clean. Global mean temperature had risen 0.9 degrees since seeding began -- exactly on the model's projected curve. Atmospheric sulfur-hexafluoride concentrations were within target ranges. Cloud-cover dynamics matched the simulation. The polar ice caps showed no measurable retreat.

Maron transmitted the quarterly report to the colonial authority's oversight board. The report's summary line read: *Programme tracking to model. No anomalies detected. Projected target attainment: Month 18, plus or minus two months.*

The governor's office sent a reply within the hour: a commendation for the climate team and an advisory that the colonial authority had begun accepting applications for open-air agricultural leases in the primary cultivation zone, contingent on reaching the two-degree target.

Maron's deputy pulled up the lease applications on the shared display. Forty-seven families had applied. Three agricultural cooperatives. A livestock consortium.

"They're planning their farms," her deputy said.

"That's the point," Maron said. "That's why we're doing this."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Maron's quarters, month thirteen. She was reviewing the latest orbital survey data when she found the discrepancy.

The polar ice cap on the northern continent had retreated four kilometres since the previous survey, eight weeks earlier. The model had predicted zero retreat at the current temperature -- the ice-cap threshold was at three degrees of warming, and the planet was at 1.4 degrees above baseline.

She ran the albedo calculation. The retreating ice had exposed a band of dark volcanic regolith along the polar margin -- rock that had been buried under ice for millennia. The regolith's albedo was 0.08, far lower than the surrounding ice at 0.75. The dark surface was absorbing solar radiation that the ice had previously reflected.

She checked the model's regolith-albedo parameter. The model used a default value of 0.25 for sub-ice surfaces -- an average drawn from analogous bodies in the system's geological database. The actual regolith on this world was three times darker.

The one-degree buffer she had described to the governor was built on the assumption that the surface under the ice was moderately reflective. It was not. The dark regolith was accelerating local warming, which was accelerating ice retreat, which was exposing more dark regolith.

She calculated the revised feedback coefficient and stared at the number for a long time. Then she called the systems engineer.

"I need to talk to you about the albedo model," she said. "Tonight. Bring the drone fleet's abort protocols."

**E4 (inversion scene).** The operations centre, month fifteen. The seeding had been halted two weeks earlier -- Maron had grounded the drone fleet the night of her conversation with the systems engineer. The atmospheric sulfur-hexafluoride concentration was already declining. But the temperature was not.

Maron stood at the display wall with the governor, the systems engineer, and the colonial authority's oversight representative, who had arrived from the orbital station that morning.

The display showed the current global mean temperature: 4.1 degrees above baseline. The seeding had been halted at 1.6 degrees. In the two weeks since, the temperature had risen another 2.5 degrees -- driven entirely by the albedo feedback loop. The northern ice cap had retreated forty kilometres. The exposed regolith was absorbing heat at a rate the original model had not contemplated. The feedback was self-sustaining: the warming melted ice, which exposed dark rock, which absorbed heat, which melted more ice.

"Where does it stop?" the governor asked.

Maron pointed to the model's revised equilibrium projection. "The feedback stabilises when the ice cap reaches the continental highland, where the bedrock is lighter-coloured granite. That happens at approximately thirteen degrees above baseline."

The governor looked at the projection. "Thirteen degrees. The agricultural threshold is two. The primary cultivation zone is rated for a maximum of four."

"The cultivation zone will be uninhabitable," Maron said. "At thirteen degrees, the soil moisture drops below viable levels for any grain species in our seed stock. The zone becomes arid steppe."

The oversight representative spoke for the first time. "Can we reverse the albedo feedback? Reflective aerosols, surface coatings, anything?"

The systems engineer answered. "We modelled it last week. To offset the albedo change, we'd need to deploy reflective material across four thousand square kilometres of exposed regolith. We don't have the material, the drones, or the time. The feedback is outrunning any intervention we can mount."

The governor sat down. "The forty-seven families," she said. "The lease applications."

"The leases should not be issued," Maron said.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The observation deck of Kepler-442 Station, that evening. Maron stood at the viewport, looking out at the northern continent. From orbit the ice-cap retreat was visible to the naked eye -- a dark ring expanding along the polar margin, the regolith absorbing starlight while the remaining ice glowed white at its centre.

The systems engineer found her there.

"The oversight representative is drafting her report," he said. "She's recommending evacuation of the primary cultivation zone."

"She's right to," Maron said.

"The model was wrong."

"The model was right about everything except one parameter. The albedo of the surface under the ice. We used a default because we didn't have ground-truth data. The default was wrong by a factor of three. One number. Everything else held."

She pressed her hand against the viewport. Three years of modelling. A successful small-scale trial. The colonial authority's approval. Forty-seven families who had filed their applications and started planning what they would grow. The programme had done exactly what it was designed to do -- it had warmed the planet. It had warmed it past the target, past the buffer, past the threshold where the feedback became self-sustaining, because the ground beneath the ice was darker than anyone had measured.

"The trial in Sector Seven worked because Sector Seven is in the equatorial belt," she said. "No ice. No albedo feedback. The small-scale test was valid for the conditions it tested. It wasn't valid for the conditions at the poles. And the poles are where the feedback lives."

The systems engineer said nothing. Below them, the dark ring continued to widen.

## R-arc-Sf1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A deep-space relay station's communications officer is tasked with transmitting a priority diplomatic packet to a colony ship before it crosses the relay's signal horizon -- the point beyond which the ship will be out of range for seven years. The officer has confirmed the ship's trajectory, locked the transmission beam, and received the colony ship's ready signal. The relay's power grid fails during the transmission window due to a cascading fault in the solar array's voltage regulator, and the packet is never sent. The colony ship crosses the horizon carrying outdated navigation data that routes it through a debris field that the packet was meant to warn it about.

> A = communications officer (Ensign Dara Achebe); G = transmitting the diplomatic packet to the colony ship before it crosses the signal horizon; X = packet transmitted successfully (delivered); not-X = transmission fails, packet never sent (undelivered).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The communications bay of Relay Station Eleven, a Tuesday by the station's calendar. Achebe had been tracking the colony ship *Endurance* on the long-range scope for three weeks, watching it close on the relay's signal horizon at a steady 0.04c.

The diplomatic packet had arrived from Earth Command twelve days earlier, flagged priority-one. It contained updated navigation charts for the Oort corridor -- the section of deep space the *Endurance* would transit over the next seven years. The charts included a new debris-field survey that remapped a hazard zone directly on the *Endurance*'s filed flight plan. The ship needed the updated charts to adjust course before entering the corridor. Once it crossed the signal horizon -- the maximum range at which Relay Eleven's transmitter could maintain a coherent beam -- there would be no way to reach it until it arrived at the next relay, seven years and four months from now.

Achebe had computed the transmission window: forty-eight hours, beginning Thursday at 0600 station time, ending Saturday at 0600. The window was generous. The packet was 1.2 terabytes -- large for a diplomatic transmission, but well within the relay's bandwidth at the current range. A clean transmission would take four hours. She had forty-eight.

She logged the window, confirmed the beam alignment, and transmitted a handshake signal to the *Endurance*. The ship's communications officer responded within the expected light-delay: handshake acknowledged, receiver array aligned, ready to receive at window open.

Achebe filed her readiness report with the station commander and returned to her quarters. The packet was queued. The beam was locked. The ship was ready. Forty-eight hours was more than enough.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The communications bay, Thursday, 0550 station time. Achebe arrived ten minutes before the window opened and ran her pre-transmission checklist. Beam alignment: confirmed. Transmitter power: nominal. Signal-to-noise ratio at current range: well above the minimum threshold. Packet integrity: verified -- the 1.2-terabyte file checked against its hash without error.

At 0600, she initiated the transmission. The relay's main dish oriented toward the *Endurance*'s computed position, the beam locked, and the data stream began. The throughput indicator climbed to rated capacity within thirty seconds. Estimated completion: four hours twelve minutes.

Achebe watched the first hour pass without anomaly. The signal-strength indicator held steady. The *Endurance*'s automatic acknowledgement pulses arrived at the expected intervals, confirming receipt of each data block. Everything was nominal.

The station commander stopped by the bay at 0700, glanced at the throughput display, and nodded. "Clean run?" he said.

"Clean run," Achebe confirmed.

He left. Achebe settled into the watch. Three hours remaining.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The communications bay, 0847 station time. The throughput indicator dropped. Not to zero -- to sixty percent of rated capacity, then forty, then it oscillated between twenty and fifty.

Achebe checked the transmitter diagnostics. Power output was fluctuating. The relay's main power bus -- fed by the station's solar array -- was delivering intermittent voltage to the communications suite. She called the station's power engineer on the intercom.

"I'm seeing voltage instability on the main bus," she said. "My transmitter output is fluctuating."

The power engineer's voice came back tense. "I see it. The solar array's primary voltage regulator is showing a thermal fault. It's been cycling in and out of safe mode for the last ten minutes. When it drops to safe mode, the bus voltage falls and non-essential loads shed automatically."

"Communications is non-essential?"

"Communications is priority-two on the load-shedding hierarchy. Life support and station-keeping are priority-one. When the regulator faults, the system protects one and two. You're two."

Achebe looked at the throughput display. The packet was sixty-two percent transmitted. At the reduced and fluctuating rate, she calculated the remaining transmission time: somewhere between six and fourteen hours, depending on how often the regulator faulted. She had thirty-nine hours of window remaining. Even the worst case left margin.

"Can you fix the regulator?" she asked.

"I'm looking at it. The thermal fault usually clears on its own when the array cools during the station's shadow pass. That's in four hours. The voltage should stabilise then."

Achebe acknowledged and returned to the transmission. The data stream continued at reduced rate. The *Endurance*'s acknowledgement pulses were still arriving, though spaced farther apart as the throughput dropped. She noted the fault in her log and calculated the revised completion time: 1400 at current rate, earlier if the regulator cleared.

The margin was smaller. It was still sufficient.

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The communications bay, 0923 station time. The throughput indicator dropped to zero.

Achebe's console went dark. The overhead lights in the bay cut out. The emergency lighting -- battery-backed, independent of the main bus -- came on three seconds later, casting the bay in red.

The station's intercom crackled. The power engineer's voice: "Main bus failure. The voltage regulator didn't cycle to safe mode -- it failed outright. Cascading fault across the solar array's power conditioning unit. The entire main bus is down. I'm on backup power -- life support and station-keeping only. Everything else is offline."

"How long to restore?" Achebe said.

"The power conditioning unit needs a manual reset, and I can't do that until the array's in shadow and the thermal load drops. Four hours to shadow. Two hours to reset if the components aren't damaged. If they are, I need to swap the regulator module, and that's a twelve-hour EVA."

Achebe did the calculation. Six hours in the best case. Eighteen in the worst. She had thirty-nine hours of window remaining -- technically sufficient even in the worst case. But the packet was sixty-two percent transmitted, and the interruption meant the *Endurance*'s receiver would have timed out and dropped the partial transfer. She would need to retransmit from the beginning.

"If I retransmit from zero at full rate, I need four hours of clean power," she said. "When is the earliest I can get that?"

"Best case, ten hours from now. Worst case, twenty-two."

Twenty-two hours from now was 0723 Friday. The window closed at 0600 Saturday. That left less than twenty-three hours -- and she needed four clean hours within that span, assuming no further faults.

Achebe sat in the red-lit bay and looked at the blank transmission console. The packet was queued. The beam alignment was stored in memory and would restore when power returned. The *Endurance* was still within range and would be for another thirty-seven hours. Everything was recoverable if the power came back.

The power engineer called again at 1340 -- four hours later, during the shadow pass. The thermal fault had damaged the regulator module. The component was not repairable in place. He would need to EVA to the array and swap the module. Twelve hours minimum.

Achebe recalculated. Module swap complete at approximately 0140 Saturday. Two hours for system restart and bus stabilisation: 0340. Transmission start: 0400 at the earliest. Four hours to transmit the full packet: 0800. Window close: 0600.

The transmission would finish two hours after the *Endurance* crossed the signal horizon.

She filed the calculation in her log. She contacted the *Endurance* on the emergency low-bandwidth channel -- a narrow-beam system that could send text but not data files -- and transmitted a single message: *Priority packet transmission interrupted by station power failure. Unable to retransmit within window. Recommend you hold current course and await relay at next station. Advise extreme caution in Oort corridor transit.*

The *Endurance*'s acknowledgement came back at the expected light-delay: *Received. Holding filed flight plan. Will monitor for relay contact. Good luck with repairs.*

Achebe read the acknowledgement and closed the channel. The filed flight plan routed the *Endurance* through the debris field. The packet that would have told them to change course was sitting in her transmission queue at sixty-two percent, and the power to send the remaining thirty-eight percent was twelve hours away, and the ship was thirty-seven hours from the horizon, and the arithmetic did not close.

She sat in the red-lit bay for a long time. Then she opened her log and began writing the incident report.

## R-arc-Sf1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (expectation set) -> E3 (expectation reinforced) -> E4 (first crack) -> E5 (inversion) -> E6 (inversion acknowledged). Embedded-document voice.**

**Scenario.** An orbital station's lead engineer designs an automated docking system to guide a resupply freighter into the station's only cargo bay during a scheduled blackout of the station's positioning thrusters. The system passes every simulation, every hardware-in-the-loop test, and the station commander's formal inspection. During the live docking, the freighter's transponder broadcasts on a frequency that the docking system's antenna shares with the station's fire-suppression network -- a design conflict that was never tested because the two systems were built by different contractors and never integrated. The docking system loses lock, the freighter drifts past the bay, and the station goes without resupply for eleven weeks.

> A = lead engineer (Kira Vassilev); G = automated docking of the resupply freighter during the thruster blackout; X = successful dock (freighter captured); not-X = docking failure (freighter drifts past, resupply missed).

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E5 -- E6 -- E1. Depicted order opens with the aftermath.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The station commander's incident log, filed eleven hours after the failed docking attempt:

> *At 0347 station time, the resupply freighter Cygnus-IV initiated automated approach to Cargo Bay One under the guidance of the station's automated docking system (ADS), designed and certified by Lead Engineer K. Vassilev. The station's positioning thrusters were offline for scheduled maintenance, as planned. The ADS was the sole guidance system for the approach.*

> *At 0351, the ADS lost transponder lock on Cygnus-IV. The freighter's guidance system, receiving no correction signal, defaulted to its pre-programmed trajectory and passed the station at a distance of 220 metres without decelerating. The freighter could not be recalled -- its onboard fuel reserves were insufficient for a return approach, and no manual override was available from the station.*

> *Cygnus-IV has been rerouted to Waypoint Delta for recovery. The next available resupply window is eleven weeks from today. Current station provisions are sufficient for nine weeks at full ration and thirteen weeks at reduced ration. I have ordered reduced rationing effective immediately.*

> *Root cause, as determined by Engineer Vassilev's preliminary analysis: the ADS transponder antenna operates on the 2.4 GHz band. The station's fire-suppression network, installed by a separate contractor eighteen months ago, uses the same frequency band for its wireless sensor mesh. During the docking approach, the fire-suppression network's routine sensor polling created interference on the ADS antenna, degrading the transponder signal below the lock threshold. This interference was not present during any simulation or hardware-in-the-loop test because the fire-suppression network was installed after the ADS was designed and tested, and the two systems were never subjected to integrated electromagnetic compatibility testing.*

> *No personnel were harmed. Station operations continue at reduced capacity.*

**E2 (expectation-set scene).** The engineering bay of the orbital station, four months earlier. Vassilev presented the automated docking system to the station commander and the operations team.

The problem was straightforward: the station's cargo bay required precise alignment during docking, and the alignment was maintained by the station's positioning thrusters. But the thrusters were due for a maintenance overhaul that would take them offline for seventy-two hours. During that window, the station could not hold position for a manual docking approach. The resupply freighter's arrival fell inside the maintenance window and could not be rescheduled -- the freighter's orbital mechanics were fixed by its launch date, and its fuel budget allowed no delay.

Vassilev's solution was the ADS: an automated system that would track the freighter's transponder, compute the approach vector, and transmit correction signals to the freighter's guidance system, bringing it into the bay without the station needing to hold position. The system used a dedicated antenna on the station's hull, a transponder receiver, and a guidance algorithm that Vassilev had adapted from a proven deep-space rendezvous protocol.

"The system has been tested in simulation across four hundred approach scenarios," Vassilev said. "Including thruster-offline conditions, sensor noise, and freighter-guidance latency. Success rate: one hundred percent."

The station commander asked about hardware testing. Vassilev said the antenna and receiver had been bench-tested and would be installed for a full hardware-in-the-loop test next month, using a maintenance drone as a transponder surrogate.

"If the hardware test passes," the commander said, "I'll certify the system for live use."

"It will pass," Vassilev said.

**E3 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The cargo bay's observation gallery, six weeks later. The hardware-in-the-loop test. Vassilev stood at the monitoring console with the station commander and the operations officer.

A maintenance drone, fitted with a transponder identical to the resupply freighter's, approached the station from two kilometres out. The ADS acquired the transponder signal, computed the approach vector, and began transmitting correction signals. The drone adjusted its trajectory in real time, decelerating on the predicted curve, aligning with the cargo bay's docking collar.

The dock was clean. The drone entered the bay at 0.3 metres per second, within the tolerance envelope, and the magnetic clamps engaged without manual intervention.

Vassilev ran the test three more times over the following two days, varying the approach angle and the drone's initial velocity. All three docks were successful. The ADS maintained transponder lock throughout each approach. No signal degradation. No correction errors.

The station commander signed the certification: *ADS hardware-in-the-loop test complete. Four successful docking trials under thruster-offline conditions. System certified for live use during the Cygnus-IV resupply approach.*

Vassilev filed the certification and began pre-deployment preparations. The live docking was five weeks away.

**E4 (first-crack scene).** The engineering bay, ten days before the live docking. Vassilev was running a final diagnostic on the ADS antenna when the station's fire-safety officer stopped by to discuss an unrelated maintenance issue.

"We're doing a network check on the fire-suppression sensors next week," the fire-safety officer said. "Just a heads-up -- the wireless mesh does a full poll of all sensor nodes once every ninety seconds. You might see a blip on any RF-sensitive equipment near the sensor clusters."

Vassilev looked up from the diagnostic. "What frequency?"

"2.4 GHz. Standard ISM band. The contractor used off-the-shelf components."

Vassilev paused. The ADS antenna operated on the 2.4 GHz band. She had specified the frequency during the design phase, before the fire-suppression network had been installed.

"Where are the nearest sensor nodes to the ADS antenna?"

The fire-safety officer pulled up the network map on his tablet. "There's a cluster on the hull, fifteen metres from your antenna mount. Four sensors, one relay."

Vassilev looked at the map. Fifteen metres. The ADS antenna was directional, pointed outward toward the approach vector, but the fire-suppression sensors were omnidirectional -- they broadcast in all directions. A routine sensor poll from fifteen metres would put RF energy directly onto the ADS antenna's receive path.

"Has the fire-suppression mesh ever interfered with any other station system?" she asked.

"Not that I know of. But nobody's tested it against anything on the same band. The mesh was installed after most of the station's external systems were already in place."

Vassilev thanked him and returned to the diagnostic. She considered running an interference test -- activating the fire-suppression mesh while the ADS antenna was receiving a transponder signal. She checked the maintenance schedule. The fire-suppression mesh's next routine poll was in six hours. She could set up a test.

She looked at the clock. She had three open work orders, a pre-deployment review with the commander in the morning, and a shift change in two hours. The hardware-in-the-loop test had passed four times. The ADS had maintained lock through every trial. The probability that a sensor poll from fifteen metres would degrade a transponder signal that the system had locked at two kilometres seemed small.

She noted the potential conflict in her engineering journal. She did not run the test.

**E5 (inversion scene).** The cargo bay's observation gallery, the morning of the live docking. Vassilev stood at the monitoring console. The station commander stood behind her. The operations officer manned the communications panel.

The resupply freighter Cygnus-IV was inbound, six kilometres out, its transponder broadcasting on schedule. The ADS acquired the signal and established lock. Approach vector computed. Correction signals transmitting. The freighter's guidance system acknowledged and began adjusting.

At four kilometres, the approach was nominal. At three, the signal-strength indicator flickered -- a brief dip, then recovery. Vassilev noted it. At two kilometres, the indicator dipped again and stayed low for three seconds before recovering.

"Signal degradation," Vassilev said. She checked the ADS diagnostic panel. The transponder lock was holding, but the margin was narrowing. The signal-to-noise ratio was declining in periodic pulses -- every ninety seconds.

Every ninety seconds. The fire-suppression mesh's polling interval.

Vassilev felt the connection form in her mind. She reached for the intercom to call the fire-safety officer.

At 1.2 kilometres, the fire-suppression mesh executed its routine poll. Four sensors and a relay, fifteen metres from the ADS antenna, broadcasting omnidirectionally on 2.4 GHz. The RF energy saturated the ADS antenna's receive path. The transponder signal dropped below the lock threshold. The ADS lost lock.

The system attempted to reacquire. The mesh poll lasted four seconds. During those four seconds, the freighter's guidance system received no correction signal and defaulted to its pre-programmed trajectory -- a straight line that had been computed before the ADS was engaged, based on an approach vector that was no longer current. The freighter continued on the uncorrected path.

The mesh poll ended. The ADS reacquired the transponder. But the freighter was now forty metres off the approach vector and decelerating too late. The ADS computed a correction, but the freighter's guidance system had a five-second processing delay on new inputs. By the time the correction reached the freighter's thrusters, the geometry was unrecoverable. The freighter passed the station at 220 metres, still decelerating, too close to abort safely and too far to dock.

Vassilev watched the freighter's running lights slide past the observation gallery window and disappear behind the station's hull.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The engineering bay, two hours later. Vassilev sat at her workbench with the ADS diagnostic log and the fire-suppression network's polling record, printed side by side.

The station commander stood across from her.

"The antenna and the sensor mesh share a frequency band," Vassilev said. "The mesh was installed after I designed the ADS. I specified 2.4 GHz. The contractor specified 2.4 GHz. Neither system was tested against the other."

"The hardware-in-the-loop test passed four times," the commander said.

"The fire-suppression mesh was installed, but I didn't activate it during the tests. I tested the ADS in isolation. The interference wasn't present because I didn't introduce it."

"You knew about the frequency overlap?"

"I learned about it ten days ago. The fire-safety officer mentioned the mesh polling during a routine conversation. I noted it. I didn't test it. I had four successful hardware trials and a certification. I made a judgment that the risk was low."

The commander looked at the two printouts -- the ADS log showing the lock drop at 0351, and the mesh log showing the sensor poll at the same timestamp. Four seconds of interference. A forty-metre drift. An eleven-week delay.

"The system worked," Vassilev said. "The algorithm worked. The antenna worked. The transponder worked. The failure was an integration gap between two systems that were never tested together because they were built by different teams at different times and nobody asked whether they shared a frequency band. I should have asked. I was told, and I didn't follow through."

The commander took the printouts. "Write it up. Full root cause. I want it in the incident log before the end of the day. And start designing the fix -- the next resupply freighter arrives in eleven weeks, and the thrusters will be back online by then, but I want the ADS operational as a backup."

Vassilev nodded. The commander left. She sat at the bench and looked at the antenna diagnostic display, still showing the last clean lock at 1.2 kilometres before the signal died. Four hundred simulations. Four hardware tests. A commander's certification. A system that performed flawlessly in every condition except the one she had been warned about and chose not to test.

## R-arc-Sf1-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E1 (inversion) -> E2 (expectation set, three months earlier) -> E3 (expectation reinforced) -> E4 (first crack) -> E5 (inversion acknowledged). Present tense.**

**Scenario.** A colony's chief geologist is certain that the seismic stabilisation array she has built will prevent the colony's foundation plate from shifting during a predicted tectonic event. The array is calibrated to counteract a lateral-shear event. The tectonic event arrives as a vertical-displacement event instead -- a mode the planet's geological survey classified as impossible. The array fires its counterforce laterally into a foundation that is buckling vertically, and the colony's primary habitat dome cracks along its base ring.

> A = chief geologist (Dr. Nasreen Farouk); G = stabilising the colony's foundation plate during the predicted tectonic event; X = foundation holds (stability maintained); not-X = foundation fails (habitat dome cracked).

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E1 -- E5. Depicted order opens with the inversion.

**E1 (inversion scene).** The colony's seismic monitoring station, 0614 local time. The alarms are already sounding when Farouk reaches the console.

The seismograph array shows the event -- the one she has spent three months preparing for. The predicted magnitude is correct: 6.2 on the colony's adapted scale. The predicted timing is correct: within the forty-eight-hour window she identified. But the waveform is wrong.

Farouk stares at the display. The waveform shows a vertical displacement pulse -- the foundation plate is being pushed upward from below, not sheared laterally. The stabilisation array, which she designed and calibrated to counteract lateral shear, is already firing. She can see the counterforce on the engineering display: horizontal, powerful, precisely computed for a shear event that is not occurring.

The array's lateral counterforce meets a foundation plate that is buckling vertically. The force does not stabilise the plate. It loads the base ring of the primary habitat dome with a torsional stress it was never designed to bear -- the dome's structural engineers rated it for vertical loads and lateral loads independently, but not for the combination that the array is now creating.

The base-ring stress indicator on the structural monitor climbs past the yellow threshold, past the orange. Farouk reaches for the array's manual override. She enters the shutdown code. The system requires a five-second confirmation cycle.

During those five seconds, the base-ring stress indicator crosses into red. A fracture propagation alarm sounds on the structural monitor. The array shuts down.

Farouk watches the structural display. A crack has opened along the base ring of the primary habitat dome -- forty-two metres long, propagating from the western anchor point to the first structural rib. The dome's internal pressure begins to drop. The colony's emergency bulkheads seal automatically.

**E2 (expectation-set scene, three months earlier).** The colony's geological survey office. Farouk stands at the holographic terrain model with the colony governor and the structural engineering lead.

"The tectonic event is coming," Farouk says. She points to the fault line on the model -- a lateral shear boundary, twenty kilometres south of the colony, mapped during the original planetary survey. "The survey team classified this as a transform fault. Lateral motion only. The historical seismic data from the planet's monitoring satellites confirms it: fourteen events in the past century, all lateral shear, magnitudes between 4.0 and 7.1."

She pulls up the probability model. "The next event is predicted within three months. Magnitude estimate: 5.8 to 6.5. The colony's foundation plate sits on bedrock that will transmit the lateral shear force directly to the base ring of the habitat dome."

The structural engineering lead asks what the dome's lateral-shear tolerance is. Farouk pulls the number: the dome's base ring is rated for a 5.5 lateral event. Anything above that risks structural damage.

"So we need to absorb the difference," the governor says.

"That's why I'm proposing the stabilisation array," Farouk says. She brings up the design: a ring of twenty-four counterforce emitters, buried in the bedrock around the foundation plate, calibrated to fire a horizontal counterforce that opposes the lateral shear during a seismic event. The array would reduce the effective lateral load on the base ring to within tolerance.

"This only works for lateral shear?" the governor asks.

"That's all we need," Farouk says. "The fault is a transform boundary. Lateral motion. The survey classified vertical displacement as -- " she pulls up the geological survey report -- "here: 'Mode not supported by fault geometry. Probability assessed as negligible.' The planet's crust at this fault doesn't have the subduction structure that produces vertical events."

The governor signs the construction authorisation. Farouk begins assembly the following week.

**E3 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The seismic monitoring station, six weeks later. The stabilisation array is installed and online. Farouk runs the final calibration test.

She triggers a simulated lateral-shear event using the array's own counterforce emitters -- a controlled pulse that mimics the seismic signature of a 6.5-magnitude lateral event. The array detects the simulated shear, computes the counterforce, and fires.

The structural monitor shows the result: the foundation plate's lateral displacement, which peaked at 12 centimetres during the simulated event, is reduced to 1.8 centimetres after the counterforce is applied. The base ring experiences a peak load well within its rated tolerance.

Farouk runs the test twice more, at 5.8 and 7.0 magnitude. Both pass. The array reduces the effective lateral load to within tolerance at every tested magnitude.

She transmits the test results to the governor's office with a summary: *Stabilisation array operational. Calibration tests confirm lateral-shear attenuation to within dome base-ring tolerance at all predicted event magnitudes. The colony is protected.*

The governor replies: *Good work. Keep monitoring.*

Farouk saves the calibration data and returns to her seismic monitoring routine. The array is ready. The event is somewhere between six weeks and twelve weeks away. She is confident it will hold.

**E4 (first-crack scene).** The geological survey office, ten days before the event. Farouk is reviewing the latest seismograph data when she notices an anomaly in the micro-tremor record.

The micro-tremors are small -- below the threshold that would trigger the stabilisation array -- but their waveform profile is unusual. Standard lateral-shear precursors produce a characteristic horizontal oscillation. The micro-tremors she is seeing have a vertical component: a faint upward pulse embedded in the lateral signal.

She runs the waveform through the survey's classification algorithm. The algorithm flags the vertical component as noise -- an artefact of the sensor's mounting orientation, which can introduce apparent vertical motion from strong lateral signals. The classification is consistent with the geological survey's assessment: the fault does not produce vertical displacement events.

Farouk looks at the waveform again. The vertical component is periodic -- it appears in every micro-tremor, at the same phase offset, with the same relative amplitude. Noise artefacts are random. This is structured.

She considers re-running the classification with a manual override that would force the algorithm to treat the vertical component as a real signal. The override would take several hours of analysis and would require her to challenge the geological survey's fault classification -- a document that was produced by the planetary survey team using orbital data and a decade of monitoring records.

She has ten days. The stabilisation array is calibrated for lateral shear. If the vertical component is real, the array's calibration would need to be revised -- or supplemented with a vertical counterforce system that does not currently exist.

She writes a note in her analysis log: *Anomalous vertical component in precursor micro-tremors. Structured, not random. Inconsistent with noise-artefact explanation. Consistent with geological survey's fault classification only if the sensor mounting is introducing a systematic bias. Recommend further analysis. Flagging but not escalating pending confirmation.*

She does not re-run the classification. She does not escalate. The geological survey's assessment is clear, and fourteen historical events support it. She returns to monitoring.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The colony governor's emergency shelter, four hours after the event. Farouk sits across from the governor. The structural engineering lead is on a screen from the damaged habitat dome, where repair teams are working to seal the base-ring fracture.

"The event was vertical displacement," Farouk says. "Not lateral shear. The foundation plate moved upward by eight centimetres. The array fired laterally because it was calibrated for lateral shear and the event's magnitude matched the trigger threshold. The lateral counterforce hit a foundation that was buckling vertically, and the combined load cracked the base ring."

"The geological survey said vertical displacement was impossible at this fault," the governor says.

"The survey classified it as negligible. It was wrong. The fault has a subduction component that the orbital survey did not detect -- it's deep, below the resolution of the planetary monitoring satellites. The micro-tremors I recorded ten days ago showed a vertical signal. I flagged it in my analysis log. I did not escalate it or re-run the fault classification."

The governor looks at her. "Why not?"

"Because the survey said the fault was transform-only, and fourteen historical events supported that classification. I treated the vertical signal as an anomaly rather than as evidence that the classification was incomplete. I had ten days. It was enough time to investigate. I chose not to."

The structural engineering lead reports from the screen: the fracture is forty-two metres long but has been arrested by the emergency sealant. The dome's internal pressure is stabilised at seventy percent. Full repair will take three weeks. No casualties.

The governor closes the report. "The array made it worse," she says.

"The array made it worse," Farouk confirms. "If the array had not fired, the vertical displacement alone would have loaded the base ring within its vertical tolerance. The dome would have flexed. It would not have cracked. The crack happened because the array added a lateral force to a vertical event, creating a torsional load the ring was never rated for. The defence I built for the colony is the thing that damaged it."

She sets her analysis log on the table -- the entry from ten days earlier, the flagged vertical component, the decision not to escalate. The evidence had been in the data. The fault classification had been wrong. She had seen the signal, noted it, and trusted the survey over her own instruments.

The governor picks up the log, reads the entry, and sets it down.

"Fix the array," the governor says. "Vertical and lateral. Both modes. And the next time your instruments disagree with a survey report, I want to hear about it the same day."

Farouk nods. She leaves the shelter and walks toward the monitoring station. The base-ring fracture is visible from the path -- a dark line along the dome's foundation, sealed with emergency foam, the repair scaffolding already rising around it. The array's counterforce emitters are still embedded in the bedrock, twenty-four of them, pointing sideways at a fault that moves up.
