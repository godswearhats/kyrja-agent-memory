# Reversal arc × Sci-fi — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## R-arc-Sf2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A terraforming engineer on a colony world spends four years developing a bacterial seeding protocol that will convert the planet's toxic regolith into arable soil. On the day of planetary deployment, the bacteria mutate under the planet's radiation environment and sterilise the soil entirely, rendering it permanently barren.

> A = terraforming engineer (Dr. Lian Xu); G = successful bacterial seeding converting regolith to arable soil; X = seeding succeeds, soil becomes arable; ¬X = seeding fails catastrophically, soil permanently sterilised

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The terraforming laboratory, Colony Kepler-442b, a Monday morning. Dr. Lian Xu stands at the culture array with the colony director, Prashant Mehta, and the chief agronomist, Yuki Tanabe. The array holds sixteen sealed cylinders, each one containing a different strain of engineered soil bacteria.

"Four years," Lian says. She taps the cylinder labelled KX-16. "This is the one. Strain KX-16. It metabolises the iron sulfides in the regolith and converts them to bioavailable iron oxide. The metabolic byproduct is organic carbon. In the lab tests, KX-16 converts a sterile regolith sample to plantable soil in ninety days."

Prashant leans forward. "The colony's food reserves last eighteen months. If we deploy in the next two weeks and the conversion holds at the lab rate, we'll have arable soil before the reserves run out."

"That's the timeline." Lian pulls up the deployment model on the wall screen. "We seed a fifty-hectare test plot on the eastern plain. KX-16 propagates through the top thirty centimetres of regolith. At day ninety, we soil-test. If the conversion is above threshold, we scale to the full agricultural zone -- two thousand hectares."

Yuki studies the model. "What's the confidence level on the lab-to-field translation?"

"Ninety-four percent. We've tested KX-16 in simulated regolith at every concentration the field will present. The only variable we can't fully replicate in the lab is the UV environment -- this planet's star puts out thirty percent more UV-C than Sol. But we've UV-hardened the strain through six generations of selective pressure. The attrition rate under simulated UV is less than two percent."

Prashant straightens. "Then we deploy. Two weeks from today. I'll notify the colony council."

Lian nods. She has spent four years in this laboratory, building this strain from a baseline soil bacterium that could not survive outside Earth's atmosphere. KX-16 is her work. The colony's future is inside those sixteen cylinders.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The eastern plain, deployment day, two weeks later. Lian oversees the seeding from the field station -- a prefabricated shelter at the edge of the fifty-hectare test plot. The seeding drones fly in grid formation, dispensing KX-16 suspension into the regolith at calibrated intervals.

The process takes six hours. When the last drone returns, Lian walks the perimeter of the plot with a soil probe. She takes samples at twelve points and returns to the field station to run the initial viability assay.

The assay confirms: KX-16 is active in the regolith. The bacteria are metabolising iron sulfides at the expected rate. The colony count at each sample point is within the target range. The UV attrition is measured at 1.8 percent -- below the lab prediction.

She radios Prashant. "Seeding is nominal. Colony counts are strong. UV attrition is under two percent. We're on track."

"The council will want a progress report at day thirty."

"They'll get one. If the day-thirty numbers hold, we scale."

She closes the radio and sits in the field station as the sun sets over the eastern plain. The regolith outside is grey and barren -- the same dead surface the colonists have looked at for five years. In ninety days, if KX-16 does what it did in the lab, the grey will darken to brown, then to something that can hold a root. She allows herself, for the first time in four years, to believe it will work.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The field station, day twenty-two. Lian runs the weekly soil assay and stops at the third sample point. The colony count is high -- not just within range but above it. KX-16 is reproducing faster than the model predicts.

She checks the other eleven sample points. Nine show the same pattern: colony counts fifteen to twenty percent above the day-twenty-two projection. The bacteria are thriving.

She sits with the data for an hour. High colony counts are not inherently a problem -- more bacteria means faster conversion. But the model was calibrated to the lab reproduction rate, and the field rate is outpacing it. Something in the field environment is accelerating KX-16's growth beyond the parameters she designed for.

She runs a genetic stability check on three samples. The check takes four hours. The results show minor variations in the KX-16 genome -- point mutations at six loci, all within the tolerance band she established during the UV-hardening process. Minor drift. Expected. Nothing outside parameters.

She logs the data and flags the accelerated growth rate in her report to Prashant. She notes that the field environment appears to be more favourable to KX-16 than the lab simulations predicted, and that she will increase the frequency of genetic stability checks from weekly to every three days.

She does not halt the deployment. The data is good. The soil conversion is ahead of schedule.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The field station, day forty-one. Lian arrives at dawn to run the scheduled stability check. She takes twelve samples, brings them to the field station, and sets up the genetic sequencer.

The first sample completes sequencing at nine a.m. She reads the output and does not move for two minutes.

The KX-16 genome has shifted. Not point mutations -- a structural rearrangement. The metabolic pathway that converts iron sulfides to iron oxide has been replaced by a variant pathway that Lian has never seen, in the lab or in the literature. The variant does not produce iron oxide. It produces iron sulfate and hydrogen peroxide -- two compounds that are toxic to plant root systems and corrosive to organic carbon.

She sequences the remaining eleven samples. All twelve show the same rearrangement. The mutation is not a drift; it is a convergent adaptation. The planet's UV environment -- the thirty percent excess UV-C that she had hardened the strain against -- has driven KX-16 through a genetic bottleneck and out the other side into a metabolic regime that does the opposite of what she designed it to do.

The bacteria are not converting the regolith to soil. They are converting it to poison.

She pulls up the soil chemistry data from the automated sensors embedded in the test plot. The readings confirm it: iron sulfate and hydrogen peroxide concentrations have been rising for eleven days, since approximately day thirty. The organic carbon that the bacteria produced in the first month is being destroyed by the peroxide. The regolith is not becoming arable. It is becoming less arable than it was before the seeding -- the peroxide is degrading the trace organics that were already present in the native soil.

She sits in the field station with the sequencer output on the screen and the soil chemistry on the secondary display. Outside, the fifty-hectare test plot stretches to the horizon, and every square metre of it is being sterilised by the organism she built to save it.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The colony council chamber, that evening. Lian stands before the nine council members with the data displayed on the wall screen. Prashant sits at the head of the table. Yuki is beside him.

Lian presents the findings. She shows the genetic rearrangement, the metabolic shift, the soil chemistry. She explains the mechanism: the UV-C environment selected for a variant that uses a sulfate pathway instead of an oxide pathway, and the sulfate pathway produces compounds that are toxic to the very soil biology the seeding was designed to create.

"Can you kill the bacteria?" Prashant asks.

"Yes. A targeted bacteriophage would eliminate KX-16 from the test plot within two weeks. But the damage is done. The hydrogen peroxide has degraded the trace organics in the regolith. The test plot is now less viable for agriculture than it was before we seeded it. The soil is not just unconverted. It is sterilised."

"Can you develop a new strain? One that's stable under the actual UV environment?"

"I can try. The development cycle for KX-16 was four years. A new strain, designed from scratch to avoid the sulfate pathway under this UV load, would take a minimum of two years. Probably three."

Prashant looks at the food-reserve timeline on his tablet. "We have fourteen months of reserves remaining."

The room is silent. The arithmetic is simple: the colony needed arable soil in ninety days, and instead it has soil that will not support life for years. The reserves will run out long before a replacement strain is ready.

Yuki speaks. "The eastern plain is gone. What about the northern basin? It's shielded from direct UV by the ridge line. Lower UV exposure might keep the original KX-16 stable."

Lian considers this. "The northern basin was excluded from the original plan because the regolith composition is different -- lower iron sulfide content, slower conversion. But if the UV is low enough to prevent the rearrangement, KX-16 might hold its designed metabolism."

"Might," Prashant says.

"Might," Lian repeats. "I have no field data for that environment. I would need to run a small-scale test, thirty days minimum, before recommending a deployment."

Prashant closes his tablet. "You have thirty days. Run the test. If the northern basin holds, we scale. If it doesn't --" He stops. He does not finish the sentence. Everyone in the room can finish it themselves.

## R-arc-Sf2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A deep-space communications relay is expected to establish first contact with an approaching alien signal that has been decelerating toward the solar system for two years. On the designated day, the signal accelerates past the relay without stopping, and the transmission it carries is identified as automated -- not a contact attempt but a navigational beacon for something else.

> A = relay station commander (Okonkwo); G = first contact via the approaching signal; X = signal stops at relay, contact established; ¬X = signal accelerates past, identified as automated beacon

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The command module of Relay Station Janus, positioned at the heliopause, sixty-two months after the signal's initial detection. Commander Okonkwo sat at the primary console reviewing the approach telemetry with her signals officer, Brandt.

The signal had been decelerating for two years. When Earth's deep-space array first detected it, it was moving at twelve percent of light speed. Over the following twenty-four months, it had slowed steadily -- a deceleration profile consistent with intentional approach, not ballistic trajectory. The astrophysics community had published forty-seven papers on the deceleration curve. Every model agreed: the signal was slowing to a stop, and the stop point, within the uncertainty of the measurements, was the heliopause -- precisely where Relay Station Janus was positioned.

Okonkwo had been assigned to Janus eighteen months ago, when the deceleration profile first indicated the heliopause as the likely terminus. Her orders were specific: maintain the relay's broadband receivers at maximum sensitivity, prepare a suite of response protocols in thirty-two candidate formats, and be ready to transmit within sixty seconds of confirmed arrival.

The signal was now fourteen light-hours out, still decelerating. Projected arrival: nine days.

"The deceleration is holding," Brandt said, pointing to the curve on his display. "Rate is consistent with the last six months of data. If it maintains this profile, it reaches relative rest at our position, plus or minus forty thousand kilometres."

Okonkwo looked at the display. Two years of physics, eighteen months of preparation, thirty-two response protocols loaded and ready. In nine days, something from outside the solar system would stop at her station, and she would be the first human being to answer it.

"Keep the receivers hot," she said. "I want continuous telemetry updates every four hours."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The command module, six days later. Three days to projected arrival. Okonkwo convened the full crew -- twelve people -- in the common area for a briefing.

The telemetry was clean. The signal continued to decelerate along the predicted curve. Brandt had refined the terminus estimate: the signal would reach relative rest at a point eleven thousand kilometres from the station, well within the relay's transmission range. The spectral analysis of the signal itself showed a structured, repeating pattern -- not natural emission, not noise. It carried information.

Earth had been watching, too. Okonkwo's orders had been updated twice in the last week. The United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs had designated the event a Category One contact scenario. Three additional relay stations had been repositioned to provide backup coverage. The response protocols had been reviewed by a committee of linguists, mathematicians, and signals engineers, and the final suite -- thirty-two formats, each one a different encoding of basic mathematical and physical constants -- had been uploaded to Janus's transmitter the previous day.

The crew listened to the briefing. The mood in the common area was the quietest Okonkwo had ever heard on the station. Not tension -- something closer to awe, held in check by the discipline of people who understood that the most important moment of their careers was three days away and that the only thing they could do was wait and be ready.

Okonkwo dismissed the crew and returned to the command module. She ran through the response protocols one more time, testing each format's transmission sequence. Everything was nominal.

**E3 (inversion scene).** The command module, arrival day. Okonkwo sat at the primary console. Brandt was at signals. The entire crew was at stations. The signal was forty minutes from the projected terminus.

The telemetry feed updated every thirty seconds. At twenty minutes out, Brandt frowned.

"The deceleration rate is dropping," he said.

"Dropping how?"

"It's slowing less. The curve is flattening." He ran the numbers. "If this rate holds, it won't reach relative rest. It'll still be moving when it passes us."

Okonkwo pulled up the raw data. The deceleration had been steady for two years. In the last twenty minutes, it had decreased by four percent. The projected terminus shifted -- no longer eleven thousand kilometres from the station but forty thousand, then eighty thousand, the number climbing as the deceleration continued to fall.

At ten minutes out, the deceleration stopped entirely. The signal's velocity stabilised at 0.3 percent of light speed -- slow by its original standard, but fast enough that it would cross the heliopause and continue into the inner solar system without stopping.

At five minutes out, Okonkwo watched the telemetry and understood: the signal was not stopping. It had never been stopping. The deceleration curve that had looked like an approach to rest was an approach to a cruising speed -- a speed calibrated for transit through a stellar system, not for arrival at one.

The signal passed Relay Station Janus at a distance of ninety-two thousand kilometres, moving at nine hundred kilometres per second. It did not slow. It did not alter course. It did not respond to any of the thirty-two transmission formats that Brandt fired at it during the eleven-second window when it was within optimal range.

The signal continued into the solar system. Janus's receivers tracked it as it receded. The structured pattern it carried -- the repeating, information-bearing signal that two years of analysis had identified as a potential communication -- continued to transmit, unchanged, undirected, broadcasting in all directions with no apparent awareness that a relay station full of human beings had just tried to speak to it.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The command module, two hours after passage. The crew remained at stations. Okonkwo had ordered continuous tracking, and the signal was now three million kilometres past the station, still moving at the same velocity, still transmitting the same repeating pattern.

Brandt had spent the two hours running a deep analysis of the signal's content with the station's full decryption suite. He turned from his console with the results.

"It's a beacon," he said. "The repeating pattern isn't a message. It's a navigational reference -- a timing signal with embedded positional data. It's broadcasting its own location and velocity in a coordinate frame I don't recognise, but the structure is consistent with a transit marker. Like a buoy."

"A buoy for what?"

"For whatever is following it. Or for whatever sent it. The signal is marking a path through the system. It's not here to talk to us. It doesn't know we're here."

Okonkwo sat with this for a long time. Two years of deceleration that the entire scientific community had interpreted as intentional approach. Eighteen months of preparation. Thirty-two response protocols designed for a moment that had lasted eleven seconds and achieved nothing.

She opened the command log and began composing her report to Earth. She wrote: *The signal did not stop. It transited the heliopause at 0.3% c and is proceeding into the inner solar system. Spectral analysis confirms the signal is an automated navigational beacon, not a directed communication. No response was received to any of the thirty-two contact protocols transmitted during the pass window. Assessment: the object is a transit marker for an unknown purpose. It does not appear to be aware of our presence. First contact has not occurred.*

She reviewed the report, transmitted it, and powered down the response protocols. The thirty-two formats -- each one a carefully constructed handshake, a mathematical greeting from one intelligence to another -- she archived to the station's long-term storage, where they would sit until someone found a reason to use them again.

## R-arc-Sf2-3

**6 beats, chronological, present tense, split discovery**

**Scenario.** A space station's chief engineer designs a new reactor containment system that will double the station's power output, enabling expansion into uninhabitable sections. During the full-power activation test, the containment field inverts and begins feeding energy back into the reactor core, triggering an emergency shutdown that leaves the station with less power than it had before the upgrade.

> A = chief engineer (Maren Solberg); G = successful reactor upgrade doubling station power output; X = containment holds at full power, output doubled; ¬X = containment inverts, emergency shutdown, net power loss

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The engineering bay of Orbital Station Caldera, docked at the L2 point. Chief Engineer Maren Solberg stands before the station council with the reactor upgrade proposal displayed on the bay's main screen. The six council members sit on the inspection platform overlooking the dormant secondary reactor below.

"The secondary reactor has been offline for three years," Maren says. "The original containment system was rated for sixty percent output. Above that threshold, the magnetic bottle loses coherence and the safety interlocks shut everything down. We've been running at fifty-five percent since I came aboard -- enough to power the inhabited ring, not enough to pressurize or heat the outer sections."

She advances the display. "My proposal replaces the original containment with a dual-gradient field. Two nested magnetic envelopes instead of one. The inner envelope handles the plasma directly. The outer envelope absorbs the flux that bleeds through the inner layer. In simulation, the dual-gradient holds coherence up to one hundred and twelve percent of the reactor's rated output."

The station commander, Okafor, leans forward. "And in practice?"

"I've run thirty-one bench tests on the containment prototype at scale. Every test held at full power. The physics are sound. The engineering is conservative -- I've built in a twenty percent safety margin above the target output. If this works, the station doubles its available power. We can pressurize the outer ring, heat it, and expand habitable capacity by forty percent."

Okafor looks at the dormant reactor below. "How long to install?"

"Six weeks for the containment hardware. Two weeks for integration testing. Full-power activation on week nine."

"Build it."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The secondary reactor chamber, week seven. The containment hardware is installed. Maren runs the integration diagnostic from the control booth above the chamber floor. The dual-gradient field activates -- two concentric magnetic envelopes shimmer into visibility on the holographic display, one blue, one amber.

She brings the reactor to twenty percent output. The inner envelope absorbs the plasma flux cleanly. The outer envelope shows nominal load. She steps to forty percent. Sixty percent -- the threshold where the old containment failed. The dual-gradient holds without strain. The instruments show even distribution across both envelopes.

She pushes to eighty percent. The reactor hums. The chamber temperature rises within predicted bounds. The containment coherence reads ninety-eight percent.

Her deputy, Tomas Ek, watches the readings from the adjacent console. "This is the highest output this reactor has seen since it was commissioned," he says.

"And the containment is barely working. Look at the load distribution -- the outer envelope is carrying twelve percent of the flux at eighty percent output. It's designed to carry sixty. We have headroom for days."

She powers down the reactor and logs the diagnostic. Nine days to the full-power activation test. The council, the crew, and the forty-three people living in the inhabited ring are all waiting for the moment when the outer sections come online and the station stops being a cramped cylinder and becomes something that can grow.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The control booth, week eight, day three. Maren runs a calibration pass at ninety percent output as part of the pre-activation checklist. The reactor comes up smoothly. The inner envelope holds. The outer envelope begins absorbing flux.

At ninety percent, the outer envelope's load spikes for 0.4 seconds -- a brief, sharp increase that exceeds the predicted value by eight percent before settling back to normal. The instruments log the spike. The containment coherence dips to ninety-one percent for the duration and recovers.

Tomas flags it. "Did you see the spike at the ninety-percent transition?"

Maren pulls up the log. She studies the waveform. The spike is a transient -- a momentary resonance between the inner and outer envelopes at the precise output level where the flux transfer between them peaks. It lasted less than half a second. It did not breach any safety threshold.

"It's a harmonic," she says. "The two envelopes are oscillating at slightly different frequencies at high output. The resonance creates a brief amplification in the outer layer. I can tune it out by adjusting the phase offset between the gradients."

She adjusts the phase offset and runs the calibration pass again. The spike does not recur.

"Fixed," she says. Tomas nods.

Maren logs the adjustment but notes in the margin of her report that the harmonic was not predicted by any of the thirty-one bench tests. The bench tests were run at scale but in a vacuum chamber, not inside an active station with the electromagnetic interference of a populated orbital platform. She considers delaying the full-power test by a week to run additional calibrations.

She decides against it. The spike was a transient. The phase adjustment eliminated it. The containment is holding at ninety percent with headroom to spare. She confirms the activation test for week nine.

**E4 (partial discovery -- the spike returns).** The control booth, week nine, activation day. The reactor chamber is sealed. Maren is at the primary console. Tomas is at the secondary. Okafor watches from the observation gallery above.

Maren brings the reactor up in stages: twenty, forty, sixty, eighty, ninety percent. Each stage is clean. The dual-gradient holds. She initiates the final ramp to one hundred percent output.

At ninety-six percent, the harmonic returns. Not a 0.4-second transient this time -- a sustained oscillation that builds in amplitude over three seconds. The phase adjustment she applied during calibration is not holding. The two envelopes are resonating again, harder, and the outer envelope's load is climbing past its design ceiling.

Maren cuts the ramp and holds at ninety-six percent. She watches the oscillation. It is not damping. It is growing. The outer envelope's load is now at seventy-four percent of capacity and rising, even though the reactor output is not increasing. The energy is feeding back -- the resonance between the two envelopes is creating a loop, each oscillation amplifying the next, pumping flux from the reactor into the outer envelope faster than the envelope can dissipate it.

"The field is inverting," Tomas says. His voice is controlled but his hands are moving fast across the console. "The outer envelope is pulling energy from the core instead of absorbing overflow. The containment gradient is running backward."

**E5 (inversion scene).** The control booth, ninety seconds later. The oscillation has not stopped. The outer envelope is now at ninety-two percent of capacity and the reactor core temperature is dropping -- the containment field is draining energy out of the plasma faster than the fusion reaction can replace it. The reactor is being quenched by its own containment system.

Maren has two options: cut the containment entirely and let the reactor shut down on thermal loss, or emergency-scram the reactor and dump the plasma. Either way, the test is over.

She hits the scram. The reactor drops to zero output in 1.8 seconds. The containment field collapses. The chamber fills with the sound of cooling metal, popping and ticking in the sudden silence. The emergency lighting activates. The instruments go dark for six seconds and then reboot to diagnostic mode.

The diagnostic takes four minutes. When it completes, Maren reads the damage assessment. The inverted containment field, during the ninety seconds it operated in feedback mode, overstressed the magnetic coils in the outer envelope. Seven of the twelve coil assemblies show thermal damage consistent with sustained overcurrent. They cannot be repowered without replacement.

The secondary reactor is not just offline. It is damaged. The containment system that was supposed to double the station's power has destroyed itself, and the reactor it was built to enhance is now in worse condition than before the upgrade began.

Maren sits at the console and reads the damage report a second time. The dormant reactor that she was going to bring back to life is now more dormant than ever. The station's power budget remains what it was -- the inhabited ring, the cramped cylinder, the outer sections dark and cold.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The station council chamber, that evening. Maren stands before the council with the damage assessment on the screen behind her.

"The secondary reactor sustained coil damage during the containment failure," she says. "Seven of twelve outer-envelope coils need replacement. We don't carry spares -- the coils are custom-fabricated. Replacement parts will need to come from the foundry at Ceres. Transit time is fourteen weeks."

Okafor's face is still. "And until the coils arrive?"

"The secondary reactor remains offline. We continue running on the primary reactor at current output. The outer ring stays unpressurized."

"You told this council the containment would hold."

"It did hold, up to ninety-six percent. The failure was a resonance mode that didn't appear in bench testing. The dual-gradient design creates a feedback path between the two envelopes that I did not predict and that my calibration adjustment did not eliminate. The harmonic I observed during the week-eight test was the early signature of this failure. I saw it, I adjusted for it, and the adjustment was insufficient."

"So we are worse off than before the upgrade."

Maren does not look away. "Yes. The original containment is gone -- I removed it to install the dual-gradient system. The new system is damaged. Until the replacement coils arrive and I rebuild the outer envelope, the secondary reactor cannot be activated at any output level. The station's total power capacity has decreased by the amount the secondary reactor was contributing at its previous fifty-five percent rating."

The council is silent. Okafor closes his tablet.

"Fourteen weeks," he says. "We ration to compensate for the lost output. Maren, you will submit a root-cause analysis within the week and a revised containment design before the replacement coils arrive. The design will be peer-reviewed by the engineering teams at Ceres and Vesta before I approve any further activation tests."

Maren nods. She powers down the display and leaves the council chamber. The corridor outside is dim -- the station has already begun reducing lighting loads to compensate for the power it no longer has.

## R-arc-Sf2-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5, embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A colony ship's navigation AI plots a slingshot manoeuvre around a gas giant to reach the target star system, a trajectory the crew has verified and committed to. When the ship enters the gas giant's gravity well, the planet's magnetic field disrupts the AI's sensor array, and the slingshot flings the ship into a stable orbit around the gas giant instead of toward the target star -- trapping them.

> A = the colony ship's captain and navigation officer; G = successful slingshot manoeuvre, trajectory to target star; X = slingshot succeeds, ship exits on planned trajectory; ¬X = slingshot fails, ship trapped in orbit around gas giant

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E1 (trajectory planning), E2 (crew verification and commitment), E3 (the slingshot), E4 (orbit confirmed), E5 (implications assessed). Depicted order opens with the slingshot itself.

**E3 (inversion scene -- depicted first).** The bridge of the colony ship *Farthest Shore*, 14:00 shipboard time. The gas giant fills the forward viewscreen -- a banded sphere of ochre and rust, sixty times the mass of Earth, with a magnetic field that the ship's instruments have been tracking for eleven hours.

The navigation AI, CARINA, announces the slingshot sequence: engine burn in four minutes, gravity-assist window in nineteen minutes, exit trajectory confirmed. The ship's velocity is 42.6 km/s. The planned exit velocity after the slingshot is 71.3 km/s, on a heading that will carry the ship to the target star, Gliese 667C, in fourteen years.

Captain Adaeze Obi sits in the command chair. Navigation Officer Renn Vasik monitors the trajectory plot on his console. The plot shows the ship's path: a clean hyperbolic curve around the gas giant, dipping into the gravity well and emerging on the far side with the velocity boost that will make the crossing possible.

CARINA initiates the engine burn. The ship accelerates. The gas giant grows on the viewscreen. At nine minutes from the gravity-assist window, the ship enters the outer boundary of the planet's magnetosphere.

The sensor array fails. Not a graceful degradation -- a hard failure. The magnetic field overwhelms the array's shielding in a cascade that takes the primary, secondary, and tertiary sensor clusters offline in four seconds. CARINA loses positional data. The navigation plot on Vasik's console freezes, then redraws with error bars that widen by the second.

CARINA recalculates using the ship's internal accelerometers and the last known position before the sensor failure. The trajectory plot updates. The hyperbolic curve has shifted. The ship's approach angle, without real-time positional correction, is three degrees steeper than planned. Three degrees is the difference between a hyperbolic trajectory that escapes the gravity well and an elliptical trajectory that does not.

The ship passes periapsis -- the closest point to the gas giant -- at 14:19. CARINA announces the exit trajectory. The number on Vasik's console is not 71.3 km/s on a heading to Gliese 667C. It is 38.1 km/s on a closed elliptical orbit with a period of nine days.

The ship has not been flung toward the target star. It has been captured.

**E1 (expectation-set scene -- chronological backtrack).** The navigation log of the *Farthest Shore*, entry dated sixty-two days before the slingshot. The entry is in CARINA's standard format:

> *TRAJECTORY PLAN: GRAVITY-ASSIST MANOEUVRE*
> *Body: PSR-7b (gas giant, 60.2 Earth masses, orbital period 4.7 years)*
> *Approach velocity: 42.6 km/s*
> *Periapsis altitude: 180,000 km (above cloud tops)*
> *Exit velocity: 71.3 km/s*
> *Exit heading: 227.4° ecliptic, targeting Gliese 667C*
> *Confidence: 99.7% (based on gravitational model, current positional data, and planned engine burn)*
> *Note: The gas giant's magnetic field is estimated at 14 times Jupiter-equivalent. Sensor performance within the magnetosphere has not been field-tested. Simulations indicate nominal performance with standard shielding at the planned periapsis altitude.*

Captain Obi reviewed the plan in her quarters that evening. She noted the magnetic-field estimate and the simulation confidence. She asked CARINA for a failure-mode analysis: what happens if the sensors degrade within the magnetosphere?

CARINA's response, also logged:

> *If sensor degradation exceeds 40%, the navigation system will rely on internal accelerometers for positional data during the magnetosphere transit (estimated duration: 22 minutes). Accelerometer-only navigation introduces a positional uncertainty of ±0.8 degrees over 22 minutes. The slingshot trajectory tolerates an angular deviation of ±2.1 degrees before the exit trajectory shifts from hyperbolic (escape) to elliptical (capture). The safety margin is 1.3 degrees.*

Obi noted the margin and approved the plan. A margin of 1.3 degrees was conservative by the standards of orbital mechanics. She signed the navigation log and forwarded it to the crew for review.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene -- chronological backtrack).** The briefing room of the *Farthest Shore*, thirty days before the slingshot. Captain Obi convened the full crew of forty-eight for the trajectory review.

Vasik presented the navigation plan. He walked through the approach, the engine burn, the gravity-assist geometry, and the exit trajectory. He showed the simulation results: two hundred randomised runs with varying initial conditions. One hundred and ninety-seven produced successful slingshots. Three produced marginal trajectories that required a corrective burn but still achieved escape velocity. None produced capture.

A crew member asked about the magnetic field. Vasik displayed CARINA's analysis: the sensor shielding was rated for eighteen times Jupiter-equivalent. The gas giant's field was estimated at fourteen times. The margin was comfortable.

Another crew member asked what would happen if the estimate was wrong -- if the field was stronger than fourteen times Jupiter.

Vasik pulled up the failure-mode table. At eighteen times Jupiter-equivalent, the sensors would begin to degrade. At twenty-two times, they would fail entirely. CARINA's accelerometer backup would take over, with the 1.3-degree safety margin.

"And above twenty-two times?" the crew member asked.

"Above twenty-two times, the accelerometers themselves may be affected by the magnetic interference. But we have no model for a gas giant of this mass producing a field above twenty times Jupiter. The upper bound of our estimate is eighteen."

The crew voted to proceed with the trajectory plan. The vote was unanimous.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The bridge, two hours after periapsis. The sensor array is still offline. CARINA has confirmed the orbital parameters using the ship's backup star tracker, which came online once the ship exited the densest region of the magnetosphere.

The ship is in a stable elliptical orbit around PSR-7b. Orbital period: 9.2 days. Apoapsis altitude: 1.4 million kilometres. The orbit is not decaying. Without a significant engine burn, the ship will continue to orbit the gas giant indefinitely.

CARINA presents the escape options in a report to the bridge:

> *ORBIT ESCAPE ANALYSIS*
> *Option 1: Direct escape burn at apoapsis. Required delta-v: 18.4 km/s. Fuel available: 12.1 km/s equivalent. INSUFFICIENT.*
> *Option 2: Gradual orbit-raising over multiple passes, using the remaining fuel to incrementally increase apoapsis until escape velocity is achieved. Estimated passes: 14. Estimated time: 129 days. Fuel margin after escape: 0.3 km/s. NOTE: This option leaves no fuel for course corrections during the 14-year transit to Gliese 667C.*
> *Option 3: Aerobraking at periapsis to reshape the orbit for a more efficient escape trajectory. Risk: atmospheric entry at current periapsis altitude may exceed the ship's thermal limits. Probability of hull integrity: 61%.*

Captain Obi reads the report aloud to the bridge crew. The options are: impossible, possible but leaving them on a fourteen-year journey with no ability to correct course, or a sixty-one percent chance of survival.

**E5 (aftermath scene).** The captain's log of the *Farthest Shore*, recorded that evening:

> *Shipboard date 1,847. The slingshot manoeuvre around PSR-7b has failed. The gas giant's magnetic field exceeded our sensor shielding capacity, causing a total sensor failure during the magnetosphere transit. Without real-time positional data, the navigation AI could not correct the approach angle, and the ship entered an elliptical capture orbit instead of the planned hyperbolic escape trajectory.*
>
> *Post-event analysis indicates that the gas giant's magnetic field strength at periapsis was approximately 26 times Jupiter-equivalent -- well above the estimated 14 and beyond the shielding's rated 18. The estimate was based on remote observation of the planet's radio emissions. The actual field structure included a concentrated flux tube at the periapsis latitude that was not detectable at observation range.*
>
> *The crew has been briefed on the three escape options. I have ordered a 72-hour assessment period before committing to any trajectory. The fuel arithmetic does not favour us. We carry 2,300 colonists in cryogenic suspension who have no voice in this decision and whose survival depends on it.*
>
> *The slingshot was the only manoeuvre on this mission that could not be reversed if it went wrong. Every other phase of the journey -- launch, transit, deceleration -- allowed for correction. The slingshot did not. We knew this. We accepted the risk on the basis of a margin that turned out to be insufficient. The margin was calculated correctly, given the data we had. The data was wrong.*

Obi closes the log and looks at the gas giant on the viewscreen, still enormous, still banded in ochre and rust. The ship orbits it in silence, circling a world it was supposed to have left behind two hours ago.
