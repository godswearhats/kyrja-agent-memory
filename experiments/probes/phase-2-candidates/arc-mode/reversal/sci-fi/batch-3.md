## R-arc-Sf3-01

**Scenario.** A terraforming station's atmospheric engineer has spent three years calibrating a nitrogen-release protocol that models show will tip the station's target moon into a self-sustaining temperature band within six months. The protocol runs, the telemetry looks perfect — and then the deep-core sensor network reports a subsurface ice-melt cascade that the surface models had failed to predict, flooding the nitrogen with water vapour and destabilising the atmospheric mix. The temperature band collapses.

> A = atmospheric engineer Dara Keel; G = achieving stable temperature-band conditions on Mira-4; X = protocol executes and atmosphere enters self-sustaining temperature band; ¬X = protocol executes but atmosphere destabilises due to unforeseen subsurface cascade

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — protocol approval).** The station's review chamber, two months before activation. Dara had the model output on the main display: a probability distribution with the ninety-fifth percentile band well inside the target temperature range. Three years of simulation refinement, adjusted after each new telemetry drop from the orbital array.

The station director, Vasquez, asked the same question she always asked: "What's our worst-case tail?"

"Protocol failure at the three-month mark, temperature regression to baseline." Dara had the failure mode on a separate slide. "Tail probability is four point two percent."

"Cause?"

"The most likely failure mode in the tail is nitrogen stratification -- the gas settles into cold bands before the full atmospheric column can stabilise. We've designed the release sequencing specifically to prevent that."

Vasquez looked at the output for a long time. Then she said: "The colony authority has been waiting six years for this moon. Approve for activation in eight weeks." She noted her approval in the mission log and asked Dara to confirm the activation date by end of day.

Dara confirmed it that afternoon. She sent a message to her team: *We're on for Sixthmonth.*

**E2 (expectation reinforced — pre-activation checks).** The engineering bay, three days before activation. The deep-core sensor grid had returned its final pre-activation sweep. Dara had spent the morning reviewing the data. Subsurface temperature at every node was within expected parameters. No anomalous readings. The ice-table depth was consistent with the orbital survey from eighteen months prior.

Her deputy, Feran, had run the atmospheric column models against the sweep data independently. He came to her desk and said: "Numbers hold. I can't find anything that would change the projection."

"Neither can I." Dara looked at the sweep data one more time. "The model is as good as it's going to get with the data we have."

"Better than any Titan-class protocol in the last ten years," Feran said. "By a lot."

She sent the pre-activation clearance to Vasquez.

**E3 (first crack — anomalous sub-reading).** Activation plus fourteen days. The protocol was running at sixteen percent of total nitrogen release. Dara was in the monitoring station when a peripheral alert came in from three deep-core nodes in the equatorial array: temperature at the three-hundred-metre depth level was running two degrees warmer than the baseline established in the pre-activation sweep.

Two degrees. Within the error envelope of the sensor calibration. Feran looked at it and said it was probably sensor drift -- the deep-core nodes had been in place for eight months and the calibration cycles were on a quarterly schedule.

Dara flagged the readings in the monitoring log and initiated a remote recalibration of the three affected nodes. The recalibrated readings came back twelve hours later and showed one point eight degrees above baseline. The anomaly was real.

She pulled the geological survey from the orbital archive and looked at the ice-table mapping for the equatorial zone. The survey was eighteen months old.

She called Feran in and said: "I want to run the subsurface thermal model against this delta. Tonight."

**E4 (inversion — cascade event).** Activation plus twenty-two days. The thermal model had come back equivocal -- it showed a possible ice-table response to the surface warming that the atmospheric protocol was producing, but the magnitude was within the uncertainty range of the geological survey. Dara had sent the analysis to Vasquez with a note recommending that they consider pausing the protocol while they ran a fresh orbital ground-penetrating scan. Vasquez had the analysis under review.

At fourteen hundred hours station time, the deep-core sensor network tripped its critical threshold alert. Temperature at two hundred and fifty metres across the equatorial array had jumped eight degrees in forty minutes. The ice table was responding to the surface thermal gradient far faster than the model had predicted. Dara watched the telemetry come in and ran the cascade projection in real time.

The ice melt was releasing water vapour into the lower atmosphere. The vapour was interacting with the nitrogen release, elevating the greenhouse coefficient. The atmospheric column temperature, which should have been rising slowly toward the target band, was now rising faster and less stably. The humidity curve was diverging from the model. The stable band required low water vapour until the nitrogen density reached a threshold; the cascade was flooding water vapour in before that threshold was reached.

She ran the projection three times with different damping assumptions. All three showed the same thing: the temperature band would overshoot and then collapse back below baseline. The self-sustaining condition would not be reached.

She opened a channel to Vasquez. "The protocol needs to be suspended," she said. "I'm sorry. We have a subsurface cascade. The atmosphere is not going to stabilise."

There was a pause on the channel. Then Vasquez said: "Suspend and document. I'll brief the colony authority this afternoon."

Dara entered the suspension command. The nitrogen release stopped. She watched the telemetry continue to diverge in real time for another four hours before the cascade peaked.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — debrief).** The station director's office, three days after suspension. Dara had the post-event analysis: the geological survey's resolution had been insufficient to model the ice-table response accurately. The survey instrument's penetration depth had captured the primary ice table but had missed a secondary melt-susceptible layer at two hundred and thirty metres -- a layer that the surface warming had reached faster than expected because the conductivity of the intervening rock stratum was higher than the survey had assumed.

"The model was correct for the data we had," Dara said. "The data was insufficient for the model we needed."

Vasquez looked at the analysis. "Is this recoverable?"

"Yes. We need a new ground-penetrating orbital pass at twice the resolution, then a revised protocol that accounts for the secondary layer. I estimate eight months to the revised activation date." She paused. "The cascade will dissipate. It hasn't permanently altered the atmospheric baseline."

"Eight months." Vasquez set the analysis down. "The colony authority will want to know how we missed the secondary layer."

"We didn't miss it deliberately. The survey specification was written before we had the results of the Kepler-7 melt-cascade work from last year. If I had written the survey spec today, I would have required higher resolution. I didn't know to require it three years ago." She met Vasquez's eyes. "That's the honest answer."

Vasquez nodded slowly. "Write it that way in the authority report," she said. "Exactly that way."

## R-arc-Sf3-02

**Scenario.** An orbital salvage crew has tracked and calculated the intercept trajectory for a valuable derelict freighter, filed the legal claim with the Port Authority, and executed a precision burn to match velocity with the hulk. When the crew boards, they find the cargo hold has already been stripped — another crew got there first through an unregistered approach vector that the Port Authority's tracking grid had missed. The claim is valid but worthless.

> A = salvage captain Nomi Strand; G = recovering the cargo of the derelict freighter Kossuth's Margin; X = crew boards and recovers the cargo; ¬X = crew boards and finds the cargo already stripped

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — claim filed, dialogue-driven).** The Port Authority desk at Ceres Station, Nomi's face on the comm screen.

"Claim filed," the PA clerk said. "Derelict registry number 7-Kossuth-14. You're the first registered intercept on this hulk. Approach vector and burn schedule are in the claim record. Nobody else has a right of first access until your scheduled board window closes."

"When does the window close?"

"Sixty-two hours from your confirmed velocity match. If you haven't boarded by then, the claim opens to secondary filers."

"We'll be there in forty hours." Nomi had the trajectory already plotted; the burn was in three hours. "What's the cargo manifest from the last registry entry?"

"Manifested at seventeen months prior, last waypoint at Ganymede freight dock. Cargo: four hundred and twelve tonnes of high-grade cerium oxide, sealed industrial quantity." A pause. "At current market rate you're looking at somewhere north of two hundred and fifty thousand."

Nomi's second, Petra, was at the nav console running the burn numbers. Nomi looked over at her. Petra held up two fingers: two percent margin on the intercept burn. Clean.

"We'll be there at hour forty," Nomi said, and closed the comm.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — velocity match).** Forty-one hours later. The Kossuth's Margin was a kilometre off the bow, tumbling slowly, the solar panels dark. Nomi had the exterior scan on the main display. The hull was intact on the visible faces. No obvious breach, no scoring. The beacon was dead, which was expected -- the hulk had been cold for seventeen months.

Petra ran the approach scan. "Exterior hull reads clean. No thermal signatures. No active systems."

"Docking ring?"

"Standard type-seven. We can mate." Petra turned from the console. "Nomi. The hold seals look intact from here. If the cargo is what the manifest says, we're looking at a payday."

"We haven't boarded yet."

"I know." Petra was already getting her suit.

Nomi matched the hulk's rotation over the next twenty minutes and brought the bow in for docking. The ring locked. She registered the board entry in the claim record: exact timestamp, crew manifest, docking port. Everything by the book.

**E3 (inversion — cargo hold).** The cargo hold, forty minutes after boarding. The hold was pressurised -- that was the first thing. Someone had used the ship's reserve power to maintain pressure after the primary systems died. The reserve cell was drained to twelve percent.

The second thing was the cargo. Four hundred and twelve tonnes of cerium oxide, manifested in sealed drums, should have filled this hold from deck to overhead. The hold was empty. Not swept-clean empty -- there were strap marks on the deck rings where the drum pallets had been secured, shadow-lines on the deck plates where the drums had sat for seventeen months, one broken pallet edge left behind. The cargo had been removed carefully and systematically by someone who had done this before.

Nomi stood in the middle of the empty hold for a moment. Petra was running the ship's internal log from the main panel.

"Someone was aboard twelve days ago," Petra said. "Six-hour docking event. They used a cold approach -- no active transponder, came in from the sun-side. The PA grid doesn't scan sun-side at that resolution."

"Legal?"

"They had a claim filed. Filed three weeks ago, before ours. No registered approach vector -- that's the violation. But the cargo lift itself was legal. They had the prior claim."

"If the prior claim is valid, the cargo is theirs." Nomi walked the length of the hold. "Twelve days ago."

"Yes."

"We did everything right."

"Yes," Petra said. "We did."

**E4 (inversion acknowledged — debrief at Ceres Station).** The Port Authority review room, two days later. Nomi had filed the boarding report and requested a review of the prior claim. The PA adjudicator, a man named Okafor, had the file in front of him.

"The prior claim was registered," he said. "The approach vector violation is an operational infraction -- we can fine the crew, but it doesn't invalidate their cargo lift. The cargo was removed while their claim window was active."

"They used a blind approach specifically to avoid our grid."

"Probably. We can't prove that. The approach vector violation has a hundred-credit fine schedule. That's the regulatory ceiling for an unregistered approach on an uncrowded corridor."

Nomi looked at him. "We burned two thousand credits of propellant on that intercept."

"I'm sorry, Captain Strand. The prior claim is valid. The cargo is gone. Your claim is first on the empty hulk, which has some structural salvage value." He checked his console. "Frame and drive assemblies, maybe thirty-five thousand at scrap rates, depending on the drive condition."

Nomi stood up. "Who was the prior claim?"

Okafor turned his display so she could read the name. She looked at it. She had heard the crew's name before -- a salvage team out of Ceres Station, the same station she was sitting in. They had been in the dock bay that morning when she came in. She had walked past their ship.

She thanked Okafor and left. In the corridor she stood still for a moment, then went to find Petra to start running the numbers on what a drive-assembly scrap yield was worth after fuel costs.

## R-arc-Sf3-03

**Scenario.** A research scientist on a deep-space observatory platform has spent four years preparing a gravitational-wave detection event for publication, obtaining peer certification from two independent review panels and scheduling announcement through the Observatory Board. On the morning of the public release, the Board pulls the publication after a systems audit reveals that a calibration drift in the primary detector array had been misclassified as corrected in the data pipeline. The four-year finding cannot be confirmed.

> A = lead researcher Dr. Yuna Ashe; G = publishing the gravitational-wave detection in the Observatory's confirmed record; X = publication proceeds and the finding enters the confirmed record; ¬X = publication is pulled and the finding is withdrawn pending re-analysis

6 beats, non-linear: E5 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E6

**Note on beat order.** The depicted order opens at the withdrawal notice (E5), cuts back through the discovery and preparation arc (E1–E4), then closes with the immediate response (E6). Chronological order: E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E5 → E6.

**E5 (inversion — publication pulled, depicted first).** The observatory's main communications terminal, seven forty-two station time. The public release had been scheduled for eight hundred hours. Yuna was reviewing the final announcement text when the message arrived from the Board administrator: publication suspended pending audit review. She was to report to the Board chambers at nine hundred.

She read the message twice. She had the announcement text open on one screen and the detection dataset on another. She called her junior researcher, Poll, on the intracomm and told him to hold everything.

At nine hundred she sat across from three Board members and the observatory's systems director. The systems director showed her the audit report. During the annual pipeline validation run at the end of the previous month, a calibration flag in the primary array's gain-correction module had been logged as resolved when it had in fact been suppressed -- a software state that marked the flag inactive without applying the correction. The suppression had been present in the pipeline for approximately fourteen months. The affected data covered the detection window.

"The gain drift is small," Yuna said immediately. "Point zero three percent over the window. I can run the re-analysis."

"We know you can," the Board chair said. "The question is whether the original detection survives the corrected gain values. Until we know the answer, we cannot release."

She had no counter-argument to that. She sat in the chamber for another twenty minutes while the Board outlined the re-analysis protocol, and then she went back to her lab.

**E1 (expectation set — detection event).** Four years earlier. The alert had arrived at three forty in the morning station time, tripping the duty alarm in Yuna's quarters. She had reached the lab in six minutes.

The primary array had registered a strain signature lasting one point eight seconds: the characteristic chirp of a binary merger, the frequency sweep clean enough to read by eye on the raw output. Yuna had the duty-log timestamp, the array state, and the preliminary signal-to-noise ratio on her console within twenty minutes. The ratio was forty-seven, far above the detection threshold of eight.

She had stood in the lab at four in the morning looking at the output and had known, with as much certainty as the work permitted, that she was looking at the real thing. A gravitational-wave event from a source her preliminary triangulation placed at approximately one point four gigaparsecs.

She had woken Poll and the two of them had run the first validation checks until six.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — independent review panels).** Three years of preparation. The signal had been sent through the platform's internal analysis pipeline, then to the first independent review panel, then to the second. Both panels had returned confirmations: signal is consistent with a binary neutron star merger at the estimated distance, detection threshold exceeded, no identified instrumental artifact. The second panel had taken eleven months; they had requested three additional data cuts and a supplementary noise characterisation. Yuna had provided everything. They had confirmed.

She had submitted the publication package to the Observatory Board eighteen months after the detection. The Board's standard review had taken four months. The Board had approved the package for publication, with the announcement date set following the annual audit cycle.

She had told Poll the night before the announcement that it had been the most careful four years of work she had ever done.

**E3 (first crack — systems audit schedule).** Two months before the release date. The observatory's systems director had sent a routine notice: the annual pipeline validation run was scheduled for the following month, with results expected three weeks before the publication date. Yuna had noted the schedule. The pipeline had been audited annually for the entire four years since the detection. Each prior audit had come back clean. She had not worried about the timing.

**E4 (expectation reinforced — pre-release final check).** Three days before release. Yuna had run a final self-check on the detection dataset: re-read all the panel confirmation reports, verified the publication package was complete, confirmed the announcement text with the Board administrator. The pipeline audit results had not yet been distributed -- they were due the day before release, and she had not received them.

She had assumed the audit was clean. All four prior audits had been clean.

**E6 (aftermath — re-analysis begins).** Yuna's lab, six hours after the Board session. The corrected gain values were running through the pipeline. Poll was at his console managing the re-analysis queue; the compute cluster had been allocated priority access.

"Best case?" she asked him.

"If the gain drift was symmetric, the correction is small and the signal-to-noise stays above threshold. Detection confirmed, publication resumes in a week." He didn't look up from the console. "Worst case, the drift was asymmetric in a way that inflated the strain estimate and the corrected SNR drops below threshold."

"Below eight?"

"Unlikely. But possible." He ran a cursor over the gain correction log. "The suppression was in the module for fourteen months. I'm trying to reconstruct what the corrected values should look like."

Yuna sat down at her own console and looked at the raw detection output -- the chirp she had seen for the first time four years ago at four in the morning. The signal was still there, in the archived data, unchanged. Whether it was what she thought it was depended now on a fourteen-month gain drift and whether the correction made the difference.

"Tell me when the first corrected pass is done," she said.

"Three hours," he said. "Maybe four."

She opened the supplementary dataset from the second review panel and started reading it again from the beginning, looking for anything that touched the gain correction.

## R-arc-Sf3-04

**Scenario.** A colony administrator on a resource-extraction planet has lobbied the Colonial Governance Commission for three years to have her settlement reclassified from extraction-tier to residential-tier, a change that would unlock infrastructure funding and expanded autonomy. The Commission's reclassification board votes to approve. The approval is reversed three weeks later by the Commission's oversight council, which finds that the board did not have the authority to reclassify settlements in active extraction zones without a full Commission vote.

> A = colony administrator Marisol Veiga; G = reclassification of Settlement Verity-7 from extraction-tier to residential-tier; X = reclassification is approved and takes effect; ¬X = reclassification is reversed and does not take effect

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — petition submitted, present tense).** The Colony Administrator's office, Verity-7, at 1400 hours local. Marisol enters the final version of the reclassification petition into the Commission's submission system and reads the confirmation receipt. Three years of documentation: census records showing Verity-7's population has held stable above the residential threshold for thirty consecutive months, infrastructure assessment reports showing the settlement's services exceed the extraction-tier baseline on all nine Commission metrics, and two independent ecological surveys confirming that the primary extraction zone is operating within its licensed boundary and does not encroach on the settled residential area.

She sends the confirmation receipt to her deputy, Fen, with a single line: *Filed. Board review in eight weeks.*

Fen replies: *About time.*

She closes the submission window. The petition is formally in. The board has never reversed a petition that cleared all nine metrics simultaneously. She knows this because she checked.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — board hearing).** The Commission's reclassification board hearing, conducted via relay from the Commission's orbital office. Marisol presents for thirty-five minutes. The board chair asks four questions, all of them focused on the ecological boundary data -- the board has evidently done its own review of the extraction zone boundary and wants to verify the survey methodology. Marisol answers each question with the specific survey coordinates and the name of the certifying ecologist.

After the questions, the board chair thanks her and says the board will issue its decision within ten standard days.

Nine days later, the decision arrives: approved. Verity-7 is reclassified to residential-tier, effective the first day of the next administrative quarter, six weeks out. The notification lists the infrastructure funding schedule and the expanded governance powers that come with the reclassification.

Marisol reads the notification standing at her desk. She calls Fen in and shows him the screen. He reads it and says: "We did it."

She tells him to schedule a settlement-wide announcement for the following morning.

**E3 (first crack — a relay message from another administrator).** Five days after the board approval. A relay message from another settlement administrator, a man named Cours on a neighbouring extraction-tier planet: he congratulates Marisol on the approval and notes, in passing, that he has heard from a contact at the Commission that the oversight council has opened a procedural review of the reclassification board's recent decisions. He says he doesn't know if it affects Verity-7 specifically and that he may have incomplete information.

Marisol forwards the message to the Commission's public information portal and requests clarification on the oversight review's scope. The portal confirms that the oversight council has opened a review but says no further information is available until the review is concluded.

She tells Fen to hold the infrastructure procurement process until she knows more. She does not cancel the settlement announcement; the board decision is official and she does not know of any grounds to reverse it.

**E4 (inversion — oversight council ruling).** Twenty-two days after the board approval. The notice arrives at 0300 hours station time -- Marisol is asleep when it comes in. She sees it at 0600.

The oversight council's ruling: the reclassification board had authority to approve residential-tier upgrades for settlements in non-extraction zones, but Verity-7 is located within an active extraction zone as defined in the Commission's Zoning Register -- the registered zone boundary, which differs from the operational boundary Marisol's surveys had measured. The board did not have the authority to reclassify settlements in active registered extraction zones without a full Commission vote. The board's approval is therefore void. The reclassification does not take effect.

Marisol reads the ruling three times. The operational boundary is within the registered boundary: the extraction licence covers more land than the extraction operation actually uses, which is common practice in extraction licensing. She had documented the operational boundary in the petition. The board had reviewed it. The board had not noticed that the registered zone boundary was the operative definition under the Zoning Register, not the operational one.

She calls Fen at 0620. She tells him to pause everything. Then she reads the ruling again and starts drafting the appeal.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — confrontation with Commission relay officer).** The Commission relay hearing, two days after the ruling. Marisol has filed for an expedited appeal on the grounds that the oversight council applied the registered zone boundary without prior notification to the settlement, denying her the opportunity to address the boundary discrepancy before the board vote.

The relay officer assigned to her appeal is a Commission administrator named Prescott. He reviews the appeal and confirms that the oversight council's authority to reverse board decisions is established in the Commission's founding charter and is not itself appealable. The procedural objection -- lack of prior notice about the boundary definition -- is valid as a procedural complaint, but the procedural complaint does not restore the reclassification; it opens a review of the oversight council's process.

"What does the process review produce?" Marisol asks.

"If the council is found to have erred procedurally, the matter is remanded to a full Commission vote," Prescott says. "The Commission votes quarterly. Next session is in five months."

"Is there any mechanism to maintain the board approval while the review is pending?"

"No. The oversight council's reversal is operative immediately. The reclassification does not take effect unless and until a full Commission vote confirms it."

Marisol tells him she wants to file the procedural complaint in full and requests the relevant forms. Prescott sends them. She thanks him and closes the relay connection.

She sits in the quiet of the office. The settlement announcement had gone out seventeen days ago. She had told three thousand residents that Verity-7 was becoming residential-tier. She had told them about the infrastructure funding. She is going to have to make another announcement. She opens the settlement broadcast system and starts writing.
