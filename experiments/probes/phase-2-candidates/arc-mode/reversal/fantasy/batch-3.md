## R-arc-Fa3-01

**Scenario.** A master herbalist at a renowned healing order has spent two years compounding a restorative draught intended to cure the wasting sickness that has taken hold in the lower city. She presents the draught before the Order's Council of Remedies expecting formal sanction to distribute it. The Council instead condemns the formula as drawing on prohibited sympathetic principles and confiscates her work.

> A = master herbalist (Sera Voss); G = Council sanction to distribute the wasting draught; X = Council sanctions the formula and authorises distribution; ¬X = Council condemns the formula and confiscates it

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — petition filed).** The Order's hall of petitions, the morning of the autumn census. Sera set her written petition on the registrar's desk and laid the sealed sample vial beside it. She had prepared the petition in the Order's formal mode: thirty pages, with ingredient sources cited against the Order's own pharmacopoeia, a summary of the fourteen cases in which she had administered trial doses under supervised conditions, and three letters of attestation from the district healers who had witnessed the recoveries.

The registrar reviewed the cover page, noted the petition number, and told Sera that the Council of Remedies would convene within ten days to review the submission. He noted that she had filed the required number of attestation letters, the correct vial volume, and the petition in the correct mode. He stamped the cover page and handed her the receipt.

"Is there anything irregular about this submission?" Sera asked.

He looked at it again. "Nothing I can see," he said.

She thanked him and walked back to her workroom. The Council had never refused to sanction a petition that arrived in the correct mode with three attesting healers. She had been told this by three different masters.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — pre-council consultation).** The library of the Order's upper house, five days before the Council meeting. The senior archivist, who had served the Order for thirty-one years, had agreed to review Sera's formula privately before the hearing. He had done this for her twice before on less significant preparations.

He read the formula page by page. He asked questions about two of the botanical sourcing choices and about the infusion temperature she used for the third compound. She answered both. He set the pages down.

"The sympathetic element is minor," he said. "The compound draws a correspondence between the patient's blood temperature and the herb's activation threshold. That is a structural sympathy, not a ritual one. The Council's prohibition on sympathetic principles was written for ritual workings. This is not that."

"I know," Sera said.

"The distinction should be clear to anyone on the Council who has read the prohibition's founding charter. I expect they have." He handed the pages back. "Present the distinction plainly and you'll be fine."

Sera thanked him and left. She had already prepared the distinction as a section of her spoken presentation.

**E3 (first crack — rumour in the hall).** The Order's lower corridor, two days before the Council meeting. Sera was crossing the hall when she heard two junior healers speaking in the alcove near the supply room. She would not have stopped except that she heard her name.

"-- Voss's draught before the Council on Seventhday," one was saying. "But Councillor Braith has been asking questions. Apparently he pulled the petition two days ago and took it to the botanical masters."

"What kind of questions?"

"Nobody knows. But Braith hasn't approved a sympathetic formula in eight years. There was that case with the fever-drawing poultice. He wrote a dissent so long it went to the Grand Lodge."

Sera walked on. Braith was one of seven Council members, and she knew his reputation. But the archivist had read her formula and found nothing prohibited. One councillor's scepticism was not a condemnation. She went back to her workroom and reviewed the founding charter for the prohibition one more time, then set it aside.

**E4 (inversion — Council hearing).** The Council chamber, Seventhday morning. Sera stood at the petition lectern with her prepared notes. Councillor Braith was seated at the far left of the bench -- an older man, heavy-browed, with a copy of her petition open in front of him, annotated in red.

She gave her presentation. She cited the fourteen cases. She read the attestation letters into the record. She presented the distinction between structural sympathy and ritual sympathy clearly and with the charter language.

When she finished, Braith spoke without waiting for the presiding councillor to invite questions.

"The activation correspondence you describe -- between blood heat and botanical threshold -- is a sympathetic binding," he said. "You have framed it as structural, but the binding is operative. It reaches across the boundary between patient and compound. That is the mechanism the charter prohibits."

"The charter prohibits ritual bindings," Sera said. "This compound has no ritual form. There is no invocation, no drawn figure, no practitioner's intention at work. The correspondence is chemical."

"The correspondence is not chemical. You cannot reproduce it by mixing the compounds without the sympathetic attunement the formula describes. I have had it tested this morning." He held up a page. "The formula fails without the attunement step. That step is the operative binding. The prohibition applies."

Sera looked at the page. She had not submitted to a reproduction test because no reproduction test had ever been required for a petition in the correct mode. She had not known to contest one.

The presiding councillor asked for a vote. The vote was five to two in favour of Braith's finding. The Council condemned the formula as drawing on prohibited sympathetic principles. The sample vial was transferred to the Order's sealed archive. Sera's petition was marked refused and returned to her with the stamp of the Council.

She stood at the lectern while the Council moved to the next matter.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — corridor after the hearing).** The corridor outside the Council chamber. The archivist was waiting. He had attended as an observer and had watched from the rear bench.

"The reproduction test," he said. "I didn't know they would run one."

"Nor did I," Sera said.

"It changes the analysis. If the compound fails without the attunement step, Braith's reading of the charter is defensible. Not the reading I would have given, but defensible." He walked beside her toward the stair. "You could appeal to the Grand Lodge. The charter distinction is genuinely ambiguous."

"How long does a Grand Lodge appeal take?"

"A year. Perhaps two."

Sera thought of the fourteen cases. The three who were still in the lower city, still wasting. "Then the appeal doesn't help the people who need it now," she said.

"No," he said. "It doesn't." He stopped at the foot of the stair. "I'm sorry I told you the hearing would go well. I believed it."

She said it wasn't his fault and went down the stair.

## R-arc-Fa3-02

**Scenario.** A young cartographer's apprentice at the Surveyors' Guild has spent three years mapping an unmapped river delta, returning with charts precise enough to qualify for his master's certification. His examination before the Guild's certification board ends with rejection when the board determines that one of his primary measurements was made using a borrowed instrument of disputed calibration.

> A = apprentice cartographer (Davan Orell); G = earning master's certification from the Surveyors' Guild; X = board certifies him as a master cartographer; ¬X = board rejects his certification

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — return and submission).** The Surveyors' Guild hall, seventeen days after Davan's return from the Kestren Delta. He had spent those seventeen days inking the final charts and writing the accompanying survey notes in the Guild's required hand. The charts were good. He knew they were good -- not from pride but from having spent three years correcting errors and re-checking measurements against landmarks that did not move. The delta had been impossible to map because the channels shifted with the spring floods, but he had solved that by mapping the bedrock elevations first and working up from there. It was not a method that had been taught to him. He had designed it himself.

He submitted the charts and the survey notes to the certification board's registrar, who counted the pages, checked that the required measurements were present in the notes, and told Davan his examination was scheduled for the following Sixthday. He had three days.

**E2 (expectation reinforced -- peer review).** The Guild's apprentice workroom, two days before the examination. Davan had spread the charts across the largest table and invited the two other senior apprentices in his cohort to review them. Neither was competitive with him for the current certification cycle -- one had another year of fieldwork remaining -- and both knew the Guild's standards.

Mira, the more experienced of the two, spent an hour with the charts. She checked the scale notations, the landmark callouts, and the flood-line projections.

"The bedrock method is going to interest them," she said. "I've never seen it applied at this scale."

"It works," Davan said.

"I know it works. That's my point." She stepped back from the table. "These are master-level charts. I don't know what else they'd want."

The second apprentice agreed. He said the survey notes were clear and that the measurement methodology was documented thoroughly. He said he expected the board to certify by the end of the first session.

Davan rolled the charts carefully and went to sleep early.

**E3 (inversion -- examination board).** The certification chamber, Sixthday morning. The board was four senior masters, seated at a half-circle table. Davan had unrolled the charts and was answering questions about the bedrock elevation method when the board's senior examiner, Master Hollis, set down his reading glass and asked about the primary measuring instrument Davan had used for the lower delta reaches.

Davan explained: his own standard rod had broken in a flood in the second year. He had borrowed a rod from a river-trading captain who had carried it as navigation equipment. He had verified the borrowing in his survey notes and used the borrowed rod for the lower eight miles of the delta until he could replace his own at the next guild post.

Master Hollis opened a ledger. The trading captain's name was there. The instrument had been registered with the Guild several years earlier and had since failed its recalibration test at the Portside Guild post. The failure had been noted in the instrument ledger but the instrument had not been recalled -- it was private property. The captain had not been informed.

"If the borrowed instrument was out of calibration," Master Hollis said, "then the measurements taken with it are of indeterminate accuracy. The lower delta charts cannot be certified."

"The measurements are consistent with the surrounding sections," Davan said. "The error, if any, would show as discontinuity in the elevation model. There is no discontinuity."

"Consistency is not certification. The Guild requires traceable instrument calibration for all measurements in a certified chart. The borrowed instrument's calibration is not traceable." He looked at the other board members. "The lower delta accounts for thirty-one percent of the submitted survey. We cannot certify a chart with a section of indeterminate accuracy."

The board voted. The certification was refused. Davan could resubmit when he had re-measured the lower delta with a certified instrument.

**E4 (inversion acknowledged -- outside the chamber).** The Guild courtyard, an hour after the examination. Mira was waiting for him.

He told her what had happened. She was quiet for a long time.

"The instrument ledger," she said. "That ledger should have been checked when you filed the survey notes. If they knew the rod was out of calibration, they should have flagged it before the examination."

"It doesn't change the decision."

"No, but it changes who made the error." She looked at the Guild hall. "The board is correct that they can't certify on an untraced instrument. I'm not arguing with the rule. I'm saying they should have caught it earlier and saved you the examination day."

Davan had thought about this in the chamber and had come to the same conclusion. It did not help the lower delta, which was still unmapped to Guild standards. "How long to re-survey the section?" he asked.

"Summer expedition season is over. You'd be looking at next spring." She did the calculation. "Nine months."

He nodded. He picked up his rolled charts. They were still good charts. They were still the best survey of the Kestren Delta that existed. The Guild just could not say so yet. He thanked Mira and walked back to the apprentice workroom to find a storage case.

## R-arc-Fa3-03

**Scenario.** The champion of a border lord has fought through a five-day tournament and stands in the final bout, expected by every observer and by the border lord himself to win the lordship's disputed succession. He wins the bout. The succession adjudicator then rules that the challenge was improperly lodged and that the bout's result is void.

> A = border lord Cavan Tresh; G = securing the succession of the Tresh holding through trial by champion; X = his champion wins the final bout and the succession is confirmed; ¬X = the bout's result is voided and the succession is not confirmed

6 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6

**Note on beat order.** The depicted order opens at the moment the result is voided (E4), then cuts back to the challenge lodging and tournament (E1, E2, E3) before returning to the confrontation and aftermath (E5, E6). Chronological order: E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E5 → E6.

**E4 (inversion — ruling).** The adjudicator's platform, midday of the fifth day. Cavan's champion, a woman named Aldis, had just accepted the surrender of the contesting heir's second champion. The crowd on the tournament ground was already moving toward the celebration that everyone had assumed would follow. Cavan had already risen from his chair.

The adjudicator, Lord-Clerk Fennis, did not move from the platform.

"Before this proceeding closes," Fennis said, "I am required to examine the challenge instrument by which this trial was convened."

The noise on the ground quieted. Cavan's steward leaned toward him and said something. Cavan did not sit back down.

Fennis read from the challenge instrument: "The challenge was lodged in the name of Cavan Tresh, claiming right of succession to the Tresh holding through the direct line of Maret Tresh, mother. The charter of the Tresh holding establishes that succession challenges must be lodged by the claimant personally, or by a sworn heir of the body, within twelve months of the prior lord's death." He set the instrument down. "The prior lord, Edric Tresh, died fourteen months ago. The challenge was lodged eleven months ago, which appears within the window. However, Cavan Tresh was not yet twenty-three years of age at the time of lodging. The charter requires the claimant to have reached majority as defined in the Eastern Charter Compact, which is twenty-three years. He had not. The challenge was lodged by a minor, which the holding's charter does not permit. The instrument is invalid. This trial has no standing to confirm succession."

The ground was very quiet.

"The bout's result," Fennis said, "is noted in the record. It does not transfer the holding."

**E1 (expectation set — challenge lodged).** Eleven months earlier. Cavan had stood in the border magistrate's hall with his steward and his lawyer and placed his right hand on the succession register. He was twenty-two years and seven months old, though he did not think of it in those terms. He had been thinking of his mother's line and of the fourteen months he had waited, grieving and gathering allies, before he was ready to lodge.

The magistrate had reviewed the challenge instrument. He had counted the months since Edric's death and confirmed the challenge fell within the twelve-month window. He had not asked Cavan's age. The charter requirement on majority was not in the standard checklist the magistrate used. It was in the charter itself, which the magistrate had not opened.

Cavan had signed the register. His steward had witnessed. They had walked out into the autumn afternoon and his steward had said: now we find Aldis.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — Aldis in camp).** The tournament ground, the second day. Aldis had won her first two bouts in the morning session and her third in the afternoon. She was the only champion on the field who had not taken a wound in three days of competition.

Cavan watched the third bout from the rail. He had found Aldis through his mother's old household connections -- she had served as a guard captain for a merchant house in the southern provinces and had an unblemished record over eleven years. She was not the most celebrated champion on the field. She was the most consistent.

After the bout, she came to the rail and drank water.

"The Morven champion is good," she said. "I've watched him. He opens low and covers high. It's a trained pattern."

"Can you beat it?"

"Yes. I've beaten it before." She handed the cup back to the page. "Two more days."

Cavan looked at the field. Two more days. Everyone in the camp that evening was planning what they would do after.

**E3 (expectation reinforced — final bout).** The fifth day. Aldis and the Morven champion had been on the field for twenty minutes. The contest was close -- closer than the previous four days had suggested it would be -- but Aldis was composed. She was waiting. Cavan had seen her do it in the second bout: let the opponent commit, then answer.

The Morven champion opened low. Aldis stepped inside it and turned him. He went to one knee and did not get up. His second came onto the field with the white cloth.

The crowd made the sound crowds make when a long contest ends cleanly. Cavan had heard it four times over five days and each time it had meant Aldis, and now it meant the holding, and he had stood before Fennis called for quiet.

**E5 (confrontation — Cavan before Fennis).** The adjudicator's platform, twenty minutes after the ruling. The crowd had dispersed to clusters of argument and the Morven faction had withdrawn to their tent. Cavan had sent his steward to fetch his lawyer and had waited.

He stood before Fennis now with his lawyer at his right hand.

"The magistrate reviewed the instrument," Cavan said. "He confirmed the window. He did not raise the majority question."

"The magistrate's review does not bind the charter," Fennis said. "The charter requirement exists independently. The magistrate's error does not cure the instrument."

"I am asking you to consider that the spirit of the majority clause -- ensuring a claimant is not a child being used by a guardian -- is not implicated here. I lodged this challenge myself, with full understanding, at twenty-two years and seven months."

"I am not empowered to consider the spirit," Fennis said. "I administer the charter as written. The charter says twenty-three years. You were not twenty-three years."

Cavan's lawyer spoke: "We intend to petition the Grand Assembly for a declaratory ruling on the majority clause's application. We ask that the holding remain in interim administration pending that ruling."

Fennis said that was within the Assembly's jurisdiction and that he would note the petition in his record. The holding would go to interim administration under the Eastern Charter Compact's default provision. Neither Cavan nor the Morven claimant would hold it until the Assembly ruled.

The Assembly met three times a year. The next session was in four months.

"That is the process," Fennis said.

Cavan looked at him for a moment. "My champion won," he said.

"Yes," Fennis said. "She did." He gathered his papers. "I'm sorry."

**E6 (aftermath — camp that evening).** Aldis found Cavan at the edge of the tournament ground as the tents were coming down. She had her sword at her hip and her travel pack already on her shoulder.

"I heard what happened," she said.

"You did what I asked you to do."

"I know." She looked at the field. "What will you do?"

"Wait for the Assembly. My lawyer thinks the petition is strong."

"Four months."

"Four months." He looked at the ground where the final bout had been. The chalk marks were still visible. "I'll be twenty-three in six weeks."

Aldis was quiet for a moment. "For what it's worth," she said, "I've never lost a bout at a succession trial."

"You didn't lose this one," he said.

She nodded and walked toward the road. He stood on the tournament ground until the last tent was down and the field was empty.

## R-arc-Fa3-04

**Scenario.** A journeyman glassmaker at the city's Glassmakers' Guild has spent two years in secret developing a formula for glass that resists the corruption of shadowfire — a destructive magical phenomenon that has been shattering windows and lantern-panes across the city. He brings his formula before the Guild's masters expecting to be elevated to full master rank and to win the commission for the city's new watchtower lanterns. The Guild masters accept the formula and award the commission — to the Guild itself, removing his name from the work.

> A = journeyman glassmaker (Pell Arwick); G = elevation to master rank and the watchtower commission; X = Guild elevates Pell and awards him the commission; ¬X = Guild accepts the formula but awards neither elevation nor commission to Pell

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — formula completed).** Pell's workshop, late on a winter night. He had run the final test three times. The pane sat in the lantern housing with a shadowfire sample burning against its outer face — a small, controlled burn, sourced legally from the Guild's research stores. The glass did not pit. It did not fracture. The flame moved across the surface and left nothing behind it.

He had been working toward this for two years, ever since the shadowfire had begun spreading from the Unmade Quarter and the city had started losing windows at the rate of forty or fifty a week. The existing formula had no resistance. Nobody at the Guild had solved it. Three masters had tried officially and all three had produced panes that lasted less than a season.

Pell wrote the formula into his workbook in full, sealed it with wax, and set it on his bench. In the morning he would bring it to the Guild hall. He was a journeyman, which meant he could not hold a city commission under Guild rules. But bringing a formula of this significance to the masters was the traditional path to elevation by merit -- the Guild charter cited it explicitly. He had looked up the charter language three weeks ago.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — master's reception).** The Guild hall, two mornings later. The senior master, Harwick, had examined the test pane for forty minutes before saying anything. He had brought in two other masters. One of them had run a second shadowfire test in the Guild's testing room. The pane held.

Harwick had shaken Pell's hand in the hall afterward.

"Where did you source the binding agent?" he asked. "The third compound in the formula."

"Calcined boneash from the northern kilns. The particle size is the key -- finer than standard. I worked out the ratio by testing against the shadowfire's frequency pattern."

Harwick had nodded slowly. "This is significant work," he said. "Very significant."

"The watchtower commission," Pell said. "The city's been holding it open until someone solved the glass problem."

"They have," Harwick agreed. He had said nothing else about the commission but he had looked at the pane again and said: "Come back on Fourthday for the masters' review."

Pell had gone back to his workshop certain.

**E3 (first crack — a word from a fellow journeyman).** The Guild's common hall, the evening before Fourthday. Another journeyman, an older man named Torv who had worked under Harwick for eleven years, was eating at the long table when Pell came in.

"I heard you showed the masters something interesting," Torv said.

"A shadowfire-resistant formula."

Torv was quiet for a moment. "Did you bring a copy for yourself?"

Pell said he had brought the original workbook. Harwick had taken it for the review.

Torv set down his cup. "You should have kept the original," he said. "That's all." He went back to his meal and did not say more.

Pell had not slept well. But he had thought: Harwick had shaken his hand. Harwick had said the work was significant. He was a journeyman who had solved a problem three masters could not. The charter was clear.

**E4 (inversion — masters' review).** The Guild hall, Fourthday afternoon. Harwick was at the review table with four other masters. The workbook was open in front of them. The watchtower commission notice was also on the table.

Harwick thanked Pell for his contribution to Guild knowledge. He said the formula had been accepted into the Guild's proprietary record under the standard development clause, which Pell had agreed to when he accepted his journeyman's position. The clause held that any formula developed using Guild materials, Guild premises, or Guild research resources became Guild property upon acceptance. Pell had used Guild-sourced shadowfire samples for his tests.

The watchtower commission, Harwick said, would be awarded to the Guild as an institution. The formula would carry the Guild's name. Pell's contribution would be noted in the development record.

Pell looked at the workbook, then at Harwick. "The development clause requires the use of Guild resources to be material to the discovery," he said. "The shadowfire samples were six tests out of two years of work. The formula existed before I requested them."

"The clause does not distinguish between material and incidental use," Harwick said. "Any use of Guild resources is sufficient."

"And elevation?"

"The charter's merit elevation clause applies to discoveries made outside Guild resources. This discovery was made using Guild resources. The clause does not apply."

Pell stood in front of the table. Four of the five masters were not looking at him.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — outside the hall).** The Guild courtyard, half an hour later. Torv was there, as if he had expected Pell to come out at that time.

"You knew," Pell said.

"I suspected. It has happened before." Torv looked at the door to the hall. "The clause was revised twelve years ago. The old version required material contribution. The revision was Harwick's proposal."

"Is there a council to appeal to?"

"The Merchants' Charter Court can review Guild decisions on commission allocation. But you would need to show that the clause was applied in bad faith -- that Harwick knew the samples were incidental and accepted them specifically to trigger the clause." Torv paused. "Do you have records of when you first developed the base formula? A date before the shadowfire test requests?"

Pell thought of his workbook, now in the Guild's proprietary record. He had a second notebook at home -- not a clean record, but drafts. Pages with crossed-out ratios and dates written in the margins. He thought carefully about what dates were on those pages.

"I might," he said.

Torv nodded. "Then you have something to work with." He turned toward the gate. "Come find me when you've looked at the drafts. I know the clerk at the Charter Court."
