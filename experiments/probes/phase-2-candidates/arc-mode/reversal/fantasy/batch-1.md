# Reversal arc x Fantasy -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## R-arc-Fa1-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A guild alchemist petitions the High Conclave for the right to demonstrate a new alloy that would replace the kingdom's failing bridge-iron. After two years of private trials, a successful proof before the guild masters, and the Conclave's own assayer confirming the alloy's superiority, the Conclave votes unanimously to reject the alloy -- because the spell-binding required to temper it at scale would draw on a ley-source that the Temple has claimed as sacred ground, and the Temple's veto overrides the Conclave's technical authority.

> A = guild alchemist (Seren Valk); G = Conclave approval of the new alloy for public commission; X = approval (approved); not-X = rejection (blocked by Temple veto).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The guild hall's lower laboratory, a winter morning. Seren had been working on the alloy for two years -- longer, if she counted the failed iterations that had consumed most of her journeyman stipend and all of her patience. The crucible on the bench in front of her held the final proof: a bar of bridge-grade metal, cooled and scored and ready for the fracture test.

The guild's chief examiner stood beside her with his testing frame. He set the bar between the jaws, applied the standard load, and increased it in measured increments. The bar held past the threshold for common bridge-iron. It held past the threshold for spell-tempered steel. It held past the highest mark on the examiner's frame, which had been calibrated for the strongest alloy in the guild's registry.

The examiner removed the bar and turned it in his hands. No fracture line. No deformation. He set it on the bench and made his note in the guild's trial log: tested, passed, performance exceeding all registered alloys by a factor the examiner would need to calculate but estimated at no less than three.

Seren asked him what came next. He said the guild masters would review the trial log, and if they were satisfied, they would sponsor her petition to the High Conclave for a public commission. The Conclave controlled all infrastructure contracts in the kingdom. No alloy entered public use without their seal.

She had known this. She had been building toward it since the first successful pour. The kingdom's bridges were failing -- three had been condemned in the past year alone, and the iron that held them was a century-old formula that no amount of spell-tempering could strengthen further. Her alloy was the replacement. She believed that, and now the examiner's log said it too.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The High Conclave's assay chamber, three months later. The Conclave had appointed its own assayer -- a man named Aldric, independent of the guild, who served as the Conclave's technical authority on all material petitions. Seren had prepared three sample bars and delivered them to his workshop a month earlier. Today was the formal reading of his report.

Aldric stood before the seven members of the Conclave and read his findings without inflection. The alloy exceeded every performance metric in the current registry. Its fatigue resistance was four times that of standard bridge-iron. Its corrosion profile was superior to anything he had tested in his fourteen years as assayer. He had attempted to reproduce the alloy using Seren's documented process and had succeeded on the first attempt, confirming reproducibility.

He concluded with a single recommendation: the alloy was fit for public commission and should be approved for bridge construction without reservation.

The Conclave chair thanked Aldric and dismissed him. She turned to Seren.

"Your petition will be heard at the full session next month," the chair said. "The technical case is as strong as any I've seen. Prepare your scaling plan -- the Conclave will want to know how you intend to produce the alloy in quantity."

Seren bowed and left the chamber. In the corridor outside, her master -- the guild's senior alchemist, who had sponsored her petition -- was waiting.

"Well?" he said.

"Aldric recommended approval without reservation."

Her master nodded slowly. "Then you had better have a very good answer for the scaling question. That's where they'll press you."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Seren's workshop, a week before the full Conclave session. She was finalising the scaling plan when her master arrived unannounced and sat on the bench by the door.

"The tempering process," he said. "Walk me through the ley-source requirement again."

Seren explained. The alloy required a spell-binding at a specific temperature during the final temper. At workshop scale -- single bars, small batches -- any licensed spell-binder could draw sufficient power from the ambient ley-field. At bridge-commission scale -- hundreds of bars per month -- the draw would exceed what the ambient field could supply. She would need direct access to a ley-source: a node where the field was concentrated enough to sustain continuous high-draw binding.

"Which node?" her master asked.

"Thornmere," Seren said. "It's the only source within practical distance of the foundry district. The draw calculations are in the scaling plan."

Her master was quiet for a long moment. "Thornmere is Temple ground," he said. "Has been since the Consecration. The Temple claimed it as a site of sacred resonance forty years ago. No secular draw has been permitted since."

"The Consecration was a political act, not a technical one," Seren said. "The ley-source at Thornmere isn't different from any other node. The Temple claimed it because it was convenient, not because it was holy."

"That may be true," her master said. "But the Temple's charter gives them veto authority over any Conclave action that requires access to consecrated ground. It's Section Fourteen of the founding compact. The Conclave can approve your alloy on technical merit, but they cannot authorise the draw from Thornmere without the Temple's consent."

Seren set down her pen. "Has the Temple ever granted consent for a secular draw?"

"No," her master said. He stood and left the workshop. Seren looked at the scaling plan on her desk -- forty pages of production schedules, binding calculations, and supply chains, all of them built on access to a ley-source that was controlled by an institution she had never consulted.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The High Conclave's great chamber, the day of the full session. The seven Conclave members sat on the raised bench. Seren stood at the petitioner's lectern. The gallery was full -- guild members, merchant factors, two representatives from the crown's bridge commission.

The chair called the petition. Seren presented the scaling plan. She described the production process, the quality controls, the timeline for first delivery. She presented the cost comparison against current bridge-iron. She did not mention the ley-source requirement until the chair asked.

"Your plan specifies a direct draw from the Thornmere node," the chair said. "Are you aware that Thornmere is consecrated ground under Temple authority?"

"I am," Seren said. "The plan includes an application for secular access. I am requesting that the Conclave exercise its co-authority under Section Fourteen to petition the Temple for a limited-use exemption."

The chair looked down the bench. The other six members did not speak. The chair turned to the Temple's observer -- a robed figure seated at the far end of the gallery, present as was customary for any petition that might touch Temple interests.

"Has the Temple been consulted?" the chair asked.

The observer rose. "The Temple received the petitioner's scaling plan three days ago. The High Keeper reviewed the ley-draw requirement and has instructed me to communicate the following: the Temple will not grant access to the Thornmere source for secular industrial use. The Consecration is absolute. This position is not subject to negotiation, exemption, or Conclave petition under Section Fourteen, subsection three, which reserves to the Temple sole and final authority over the disposition of consecrated sites."

The chamber was still. The chair asked whether there was an alternative ley-source that could support the required draw. Seren said there was not -- Thornmere was the only node within range. A source in the northern province could theoretically serve, but it was two hundred miles from the foundry district, and the draw losses over that distance would reduce the binding efficiency below the minimum threshold.

The chair called the vote. The question, as she framed it, was whether the Conclave could approve a commission that it had no authority to execute. The vote was seven to zero: petition denied.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The corridor outside the great chamber, immediately after the session. Seren's master was leaning against the wall. The gallery crowd filed past them.

"Seven to zero," Seren said. "Aldric's report said approval without reservation. Seven to zero."

"The technical case was never the problem," her master said. "The technical case was perfect. The Conclave agreed with every word of it. They denied you because the thing you built cannot exist at the scale the kingdom needs without the Temple's cooperation, and the Temple will not cooperate."

Seren leaned against the opposite wall. Two years of work. A proven alloy. A unanimous technical endorsement. And the answer was no -- not because the metal was flawed, not because the science was wrong, not because a single member of the Conclave doubted the result, but because the power source required to produce it at scale stood on ground that a religious authority had claimed two decades before she was born.

"Is there an appeal?" she said.

"To whom?" her master said. "The crown? The crown defers to the Temple on consecrated sites. It's been that way since the founding compact." He pushed off the wall. "Come back to the guild. Bring your notes. There may be another path -- a different binding method, a lower-draw process, something that doesn't need Thornmere. But that's a new project, Seren. Not this one."

She followed him down the corridor. Behind them, a clerk was already clearing the petitioner's lectern for the next case.

## R-arc-Fa1-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A war-captain besieges a fortified monastery held by rebels, confident that starvation will force surrender within a month. The monks inside have been secretly channelling water from an underground spring through a consecrated well, sustaining both garrison and grain stores. When the war-captain's sappers breach the outer wall, they find the defenders healthy, provisioned, and formed for battle -- and the besieging army, weakened by a month of encampment in marshland, breaks on the monastery's counter-charge.

> A = war-captain (Edrath); G = forcing the monastery's surrender by siege; X = surrender (the garrison yields); not-X = defeat (the besieging army breaks and retreats).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The command tent on the ridge above the monastery, the first day of the siege. The war-captain stood at the map table with his three company commanders and the army's quartermaster.

"How long?" the first commander asked.

Edrath pointed to the map. "The monastery sits on limestone. No river access. No visible wells outside the outer wall. The only water source we've identified is a cistern on the eastern face, and my scouts watched them filling it from rain barrels last week." He turned to the quartermaster. "What's the standard calculus?"

The quartermaster ran his thumb along the monastery's outline on the map. "Two hundred defenders, based on the smoke count. The grain stores they could have stockpiled before we arrived would last them -- at full rationing -- six weeks. But without a reliable water source, grain doesn't matter. A man can ration food. He can't ration thirst. I give them three weeks before they're drinking from the cistern's mud. Four at the outside."

"So we wait," Edrath said.

"We wait," the quartermaster confirmed. "The walls are forty feet of dressed stone. Assault would cost us a third of the army. Siege engines would take two months to build from local timber, and by then it's over anyway. This is a starvation problem, and starvation is arithmetic."

Edrath placed a marker on the map at the monastery's gate. "One month. I want picket lines on every approach, double watch on the eastern cistern, and no one in or out. We starve them and we accept their surrender. That's the plan."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The command tent, eighteen days into the siege. Edrath sat with the quartermaster and a scout who had returned from a night observation post on the monastery's eastern face.

"Anything?" Edrath asked.

"Movement on the walls is down," the scout said. "Fewer patrols at night. Two sentries where there used to be four. I could see torchlight in the refectory wing -- dim, like they're conserving fuel."

"Water discipline," the quartermaster said. "When they start cutting patrols, it means they're rationing strength. Less movement, less sweat, less water consumption. It's the first sign."

"How long?" Edrath asked.

"A week. Maybe ten days. Once patrol strength drops below defensive minimum, they'll know they can't man the walls. That's when they send a delegation."

Edrath nodded. The siege was proceeding as modelled. His army was encamped on the ridge and the meadow below it, with the marshland to the south kept at their backs. Morale was adequate. Supplies were holding. The only cost so far was boredom and the damp that came with sleeping close to wetland in autumn.

"Ten days," Edrath said. "Then we accept their colours and go home."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The quartermaster's tent, twenty-five days into the siege. Edrath arrived at dawn to find the quartermaster standing over a report from the night watch.

"Something's wrong," the quartermaster said.

"Wrong how?"

"The reduced patrols -- they've reversed. Four sentries on the eastern wall last night. Full torch on the refectory. And my observer on the south ridge saw them carrying barrels across the courtyard -- not empty barrels. Full ones. Heavy. It took two men per barrel."

Edrath took the report. "They could be redistributing what's left. Consolidating stores before they send terms."

"They could," the quartermaster said. He did not sound convinced. "But there's something else. The marsh drainage on our southern perimeter -- the one we've been using as a latrine trench -- it's backing up. My engineers looked at it this morning. The water table has risen. There's a subsurface flow that wasn't there when we encamped."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning there's water moving underground in ways I didn't account for. If there's a spring source under that limestone that feeds the monastery from below, they could have water we never saw and never will see from the outside."

Edrath set the report down. "You told me the calculus was arithmetic."

"It is," the quartermaster said. "If you have all the variables. I may be missing one."

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The outer wall's western breach, thirty-one days into the siege. Edrath's sappers had spent three days digging under the foundations, and the section of wall came down just after dawn in a cloud of limestone dust.

Edrath stood on the ridge with his commanders, watching the breach open. The plan was simple: the breach would reveal the state of the garrison. If the defenders were weakened -- thin, disorganised, unable to form a line -- the army would advance through the gap and accept surrender on the field. If the defenders were still capable, Edrath would pull back and reassess.

The dust cleared. The defenders were standing in the breach.

Not standing -- formed. Two ranks deep, shields locked, weapons drawn. The front rank wore full armour. Their faces were lean but not hollow. Their formation was disciplined. Behind them, in the courtyard visible through the gap in the wall, Edrath could see supply carts -- not empty ones. Barrels stacked against the refectory wall. A well-housing in the centre of the yard, its winch in active use, a bucket rising as he watched.

"That's not a starving garrison," the first commander said.

It was not. The defenders had water. They had had it the entire time -- drawn from a source beneath the monastery that Edrath's scouts had never found because it had no surface expression. The cistern on the eastern face had been a decoy, or at best a supplement. The true supply was underground, invisible, and inexhaustible.

The defenders did not wait for Edrath to decide. A horn sounded inside the monastery, and the formed ranks advanced through the breach. Not a sortie -- a full counter-charge, two hundred men moving at speed into the gap between Edrath's forward positions.

Edrath's army had been encamped in marshland for a month. The damp had rotted boot leather, rusted mail, and settled into joints and lungs. The forward company that should have held the breach line buckled under the charge within minutes. The second company, drawn up on the meadow slope, tried to wheel into position but lost footing on the wet grass. The third was still forming when the monastery's garrison punched through the second line and kept coming.

Edrath called the retreat from the ridge. The army withdrew north along the road it had arrived on, leaving the camp, the siege works, and the supply train behind. The monastery's garrison did not pursue beyond the meadow's edge. They held the breach, reformed, and watched Edrath's column disappear into the tree line.

On the ridge, the quartermaster caught up to Edrath at the column's rear.

"A spring," he said. "Under the monastery. Consecrated well-housing. They were drawing from it the entire time."

Edrath did not answer. He had planned a siege on the assumption that he knew every source of water the defenders could access. He had been wrong. The arithmetic had been correct -- but one of the variables had been hidden under forty feet of limestone, and the garrison that was supposed to be dying of thirst had spent a month watching his army rot in a marsh.

## R-arc-Fa1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (expectation set) -> E3 (expectation reinforced) -> E4 (first crack) -> E5 (inversion) -> E6 (inversion acknowledged), embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A young mage-scholar submits her thesis to the Arcane Collegium for elevation to the rank of Adept, the threshold that would grant her access to the Collegium's restricted archive and a seat on the research council. Her thesis proposes a new classification of ward-geometry that unifies three previously incompatible schools of protective magic. Every member of her examination panel has praised the work in preliminary correspondence. On the day of the formal defence, the panel denies elevation -- because the Collegium's charter requires that any thesis touching protective magic receive a concurrent military review, and the military review board has classified her work as a strategic asset, barring publication and therefore barring the public defence that elevation requires.

> A = mage-scholar (Elara Kess); G = elevation to Adept rank via successful thesis defence; X = elevation granted (pass); not-X = elevation denied (fail, by procedural block).

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E5 -- E6 -- E1. Depicted order opens with the aftermath and then returns to the beginning.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The following morning. Elara found the formal notice pinned to the door of her study chamber in the scholars' wing. The document bore the Collegium's seal and the counter-seal of the military review board:

> *To Elara Kess, Scholar Third Rank, Arcane Collegium. The examination panel convened on the fourteenth day of Harvestmoon to consider your thesis, "On the Unification of Ward-Geometry Across the Carden, Elinath, and Torren Schools." The panel was unable to proceed to a defence. Pursuant to Collegium Charter, Article Twelve, Section Three, any work classified as a strategic asset by the Crown's military review board may not be presented in public session. As elevation to the rank of Adept requires a public defence, the panel has no procedural mechanism by which to grant elevation at this time. Your thesis remains under classification seal until such time as the military review board lifts the restriction. The panel commends the quality of the work and notes that this outcome reflects no judgment on its scholarly merit. You retain your current rank and privileges.*

Below the notice, in a separate hand -- the hand of her examination chair, Master Aldren -- a single line: *I am sorry. Come and see me when you are ready.*

Elara read the notice twice. She removed it from the door, folded it, and placed it inside her journal.

**E2 (expectation-set scene).** Eight months earlier. The Collegium's submission hall, a morning in early spring. Elara stood before the registrar's desk with three bound copies of her thesis and the application for examination.

The registrar reviewed the application, confirmed the title, and asked her to name her requested examination panel. Elara named three: Master Aldren, who had supervised her research; Warden Thessa, who held the chair in protective magic; and Scholar-General Brynn, a military-appointed member of the Collegium faculty who served as the standing liaison between the Collegium and the Crown's defence establishment.

The registrar noted the names, stamped the application, and placed one copy of the thesis in the examination file. The other two would be distributed to the panel.

"The panel will convene within six months," the registrar said. "You will be notified of the date. If you wish to amend the thesis before the defence, submit the amended text no later than thirty days before the scheduled date."

Elara thanked her and left the hall. In the corridor, she allowed herself to feel the weight of what she had done. Two years of work. Three schools of ward-geometry that had been treated as separate disciplines for a century, unified under a single geometric framework that predicted ward behaviour across all three. If the defence succeeded, she would be the youngest Adept in the Collegium's records. If it failed, she would have three more years of revision ahead of her before she could resubmit.

She did not think it would fail.

**E3 (expectation-reinforced scene).** Elara's study chamber, a summer evening five months later. Three letters on her desk, received over the preceding weeks, each from a member of her examination panel.

Master Aldren's letter was brief: he had read the thesis in full and found no structural weakness in the argument. He noted two passages that would draw questions at the defence and advised her to prepare responses. His final line read: *This is the best work to come out of my research group in a decade. Defend it well.*

Warden Thessa's letter was longer and more technical. She had tested Elara's geometric framework against seven historical ward-failures in the Collegium's archive and found that the framework predicted all seven. She wrote: *I have spent thirty years believing the Carden and Torren schools were fundamentally incompatible. Your thesis has changed my position. I intend to say so at the defence.*

Scholar-General Brynn's letter was the shortest. He complimented the work, noted its potential military applications, and confirmed his availability for the defence date. His final line read: *I look forward to recommending your elevation.*

Elara placed the three letters in her thesis file. Every member of her panel had endorsed the work in writing. The defence was two months away. She began preparing her responses to Aldren's two flagged passages.

**E4 (first-crack scene).** The Collegium's administrative wing, six weeks before the defence. Elara had been summoned by the registrar to review a procedural matter related to her examination.

The registrar's office was small and plain. The registrar sat behind her desk with a document Elara did not recognise.

"Article Twelve," the registrar said. "Are you familiar with it?"

Elara was not. Article Twelve governed the intersection of Collegium research and Crown military interests. She had never had reason to read it.

The registrar explained. Any thesis that touched protective magic -- wards, shields, defensive enchantments -- was subject to a concurrent military review under Article Twelve. The review was conducted by the Crown's military review board, which had the authority to classify any work it deemed of strategic value. Classification meant the work could not be presented in public session, shared with foreign scholars, or published in the Collegium's archive.

"Scholar-General Brynn sits on the military review board," the registrar said. "As the military liaison on your panel, he is required to submit your thesis for review. He did so last month."

"And?" Elara said.

"The review is ongoing. I'm told it's routine -- they review all protective-magic theses, and most are cleared without restriction. But I wanted you to be aware. If the board classifies your work before the defence date, the defence cannot proceed as a public session, and elevation requires a public session under the charter."

Elara asked how often the board classified a Collegium thesis. The registrar said it had happened twice in the past twenty years, both times involving work with direct battlefield applications. Elara's thesis was theoretical -- a geometric framework, not a weapon design. The registrar said she expected clearance.

Elara thanked her and returned to her study. She opened Article Twelve and read it in full. The language was clear: classification was at the board's sole discretion, not subject to appeal within the Collegium, and effective immediately upon issuance. The Collegium could petition the board for declassification, but the petition process took a minimum of two years.

She closed the charter and returned to her defence preparation. The registrar expected clearance. So did she.

**E5 (inversion scene).** The examination hall, the morning of the defence. The three panel members sat at the raised bench. Elara stood at the scholar's lectern with her notes. The gallery held perhaps forty people -- faculty, fellow scholars, two visiting researchers from a neighbouring Collegium.

Master Aldren called the session to order. He turned to Scholar-General Brynn and asked for the military review board's determination, as required by Article Twelve before any public presentation could begin.

Brynn stood. He held a sealed document bearing the review board's crest. He broke the seal and read the determination aloud.

The military review board had classified Elara's thesis as a strategic asset of the Crown. The board's finding was that the unified ward-geometry framework, if disseminated, would provide foreign powers with the theoretical basis to predict and defeat the kingdom's current ward deployments. The classification was immediate, absolute, and not subject to partial redaction. No portion of the thesis could be presented in public session.

The gallery was silent. Brynn set the document on the bench. He did not look at Elara.

Aldren asked whether the panel could proceed with a closed defence -- panel members only, no gallery, no publication. The registrar, seated at the clerk's table, consulted the charter and read the relevant passage: elevation to Adept required a public defence, defined as a session open to all Collegium members of Scholar rank or above. A closed session did not satisfy the requirement. The charter offered no alternative pathway.

Aldren looked at the other two panel members. Thessa's face was rigid. Brynn's was composed. Aldren turned to Elara.

"The panel cannot proceed," he said. "The defence is suspended. Your thesis remains under classification seal."

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Master Aldren's study, that afternoon. Elara sat across from him. The classified thesis -- her own work, now sealed in a document case she was permitted to hold but not to open in the presence of anyone outside the review board's clearance list -- rested on her knees.

"Brynn recommended my elevation in his letter," she said. "Six weeks ago."

"He did," Aldren said. "And I believe he meant it. The classification decision was made by the full board, not by Brynn alone. He may have argued against it. I don't know. The board's deliberations are sealed."

"Thessa tested my framework against seven historical failures and it predicted all of them. That's the problem, isn't it? It works too well. If it can predict our own ward-failures, it can predict our current wards."

Aldren said nothing for a moment. Then: "Yes. That's the board's reasoning, as far as I can interpret it."

Elara looked at the sealed case. Two years of work. Three letters of endorsement. A framework that unified a century of fragmented theory. The examination panel had been ready to elevate her, and every one of them had said so in writing. The answer was no -- not because the work was insufficient, not because the argument was flawed, not because a single member of the panel found a weakness, but because the work was too strong in exactly the way that made it dangerous, and a military body she had never appeared before had sealed it on grounds she could not challenge.

"Two years for the declassification petition," she said.

"At minimum," Aldren said. "And there's no guarantee. The board renews classifications annually. If the strategic assessment holds, it could remain sealed indefinitely."

Elara stood. She held the sealed case against her chest. The thesis was hers but it was no longer hers to speak aloud, and the rank it should have earned her was locked behind a procedural gate that the Collegium's own charter had placed beyond its power to open.

"I'll begin the petition tomorrow," she said. "And I'll start the next thesis the day after."

## R-arc-Fa1-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A clan chieftain challenges a rival for control of the sacred hunting grounds at the annual Gathering, confident of victory because his champion is the finest blade in three generations and the trial-by-combat rules have always favoured strength and speed. The elders, however, invoke a seldom-used provision of the ancient compact that replaces combat with a trial of stewardship -- a test of land-knowledge, animal husbandry, and seasonal reading. The chieftain's champion, trained only for war, fails every measure. The rival's daughter, a shepherdess who has worked the grounds since childhood, passes without error.

> A = clan chieftain (Brennach); G = winning control of the sacred hunting grounds through the trial; X = victory (his champion wins the trial); not-X = defeat (his champion fails the trial, rival retains the grounds).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The chieftain's longhouse, the night before the Gathering. Brennach sits at the head of the fire-circle with his champion, a man named Cael, and the clan's three war-elders.

Cael is sharpening his blade. He has won eleven single combats in formal challenge and has never been defeated. He is the reason Brennach issued the challenge in the first place -- without a champion of Cael's calibre, the claim would be empty, because the Gathering's trial has been settled by combat for as long as any living elder remembers.

Brennach turns to the eldest of his war-elders. "The rival will name his own champion. Do we know who?"

"His son," the war-elder says. "Young. Strong enough. But he's never fought a formal trial. Cael will finish him before the second pass."

Cael does not look up from the blade. "I've seen the son train," he says. "He leads with his shoulder and drops his guard on the return. I won't need a second pass."

Brennach nods. The hunting grounds have been in the rival clan's hands for three generations. Brennach's grandfather lost them in a trial that his clan still considers unfair -- a combat fought on muddy ground after a week of rain, where the heavier fighter had the advantage. Brennach has spent two years preparing this challenge. Cael is the instrument. The outcome, in Brennach's estimation, is already decided.

"Tomorrow," Brennach says. "We take back what's ours."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The Gathering field, the following morning. The twelve clans are assembled in the great circle. The two chieftains stand before the elder council -- seven elders drawn from neutral clans, who preside over all Gathering disputes.

Brennach states his challenge: his clan claims the sacred hunting grounds by right of ancestral use, and he names Cael as his champion to settle the matter by trial. The rival chieftain, a woman named Sorcha, accepts the challenge and names her champion.

She does not name her son. She names her daughter, Fenn.

The crowd murmurs. Fenn is not a warrior. She is a shepherdess -- lean, weather-darkened, dressed in working leathers, with no blade at her hip. She stands beside her mother and says nothing.

Brennach looks at Cael. Cael looks at Fenn. The calculation is immediate and obvious: if the trial is combat, this is no contest. A seasoned fighter against a shepherdess. Brennach feels the outcome settle into certainty. Sorcha has made an error, and the grounds are his.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The elder council's platform, moments later. The chief elder rises to announce the form of the trial. Brennach waits for the word *combat*.

The chief elder does not say it. He says: "The challenge concerns stewardship of the sacred hunting grounds. Under the compact, Article Nine, the elder council reserves the right to set the form of trial when the subject of the dispute is land held in sacred trust. The council has deliberated and determined that this dispute will be settled by trial of stewardship."

Brennach steps forward. "The trial has been combat for generations. My champion was named for combat."

"The trial has been combat by custom," the chief elder says. "Not by law. Article Nine gives the council authority to set the form when sacred land is at issue. The trial of stewardship tests the champion's knowledge of the land itself -- its water sources, its animal populations, its seasonal patterns, its boundaries, its carrying capacity. The champion who demonstrates greater stewardship of the grounds will prevail."

Brennach turns to his war-elders. They are silent. The eldest of them looks at the ground. He knows Article Nine. He has known it since before Brennach was born. He did not think the council would invoke it.

Brennach looks at Cael. Cael is a swordsman. He has never worked land, counted herds, or read a season's turning in the colour of the grass. He has never set foot on the sacred hunting grounds except to hunt.

He looks at Fenn. Fenn has lived on the hunting grounds since she was old enough to walk. She has herded sheep across every ridge, drawn water from every spring, counted every deer migration for a decade. The trial of stewardship is a test of everything she knows and nothing Cael does.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The trial ground, that afternoon. The elder council administers the stewardship trial in the presence of all twelve clans. The two champions stand on opposite sides of the great circle. The council asks seven questions -- one for each elder -- and the champions answer in alternation.

First question: name the three principal water sources within the grounds' boundaries and describe their seasonal flow. Fenn names them without hesitation -- the north spring, the Carden brook, the rain-pool above the eastern ridge -- and describes the seasonal variation in each. Cael names one, guesses at a second, and does not attempt the third.

Second question: estimate the current deer population on the grounds and describe the herd's migration pattern across the four seasons. Fenn gives a number -- one hundred and forty, plus or minus twelve, based on last autumn's count -- and walks through the migration route from winter shelter to summer grazing. Cael says he does not know the number. He has hunted deer on the grounds. He has not counted them.

The pattern holds through all seven questions. Fenn answers each with the fluency of a person describing her own home. Cael answers two partially, declines three, and gives incorrect responses to two. By the fifth question, the crowd has stopped murmuring. By the seventh, the result is beyond dispute.

The chief elder calls the verdict. The trial of stewardship is awarded to Fenn, champion of Sorcha's clan. The sacred hunting grounds remain under Sorcha's stewardship. Brennach's challenge fails.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The chieftain's camp at the edge of the Gathering field, that evening. Brennach sits by the fire. Cael stands at the tent's entrance with his blade -- still sharp, never drawn, useless for the contest that was actually fought.

"She didn't beat me," Cael says. "The elders changed the rules."

"The elders invoked a rule that already existed," Brennach says. "We prepared for a fight. They tested for something else. And the rival knew -- Sorcha knew. She named her daughter because she knew the elders would invoke stewardship. The shepherdess was the right champion for the trial that was actually called. Cael was the right champion for a trial that never happened."

Cael sets the blade against the tent pole and sits. "What now?"

Brennach looks at the fire. Two years of preparation. The finest swordsman in three generations. A challenge he had considered settled before it began. And the grounds remain with Sorcha's clan -- not because Cael was defeated in combat, but because the contest was never the one Brennach had prepared for, and the champion he had chosen had nothing to offer the one that was.

"Now we learn the land," Brennach says. "If the trial is stewardship, then stewardship is what we bring next time. And next time we don't send a swordsman. We send someone who can name every spring on the grounds and count every deer on the ridge."

He looks at the fire for a long time. The Gathering drums are still beating in the distance. The grounds are not his. They were never going to be his -- not this way, not with this champion, not against a rival who understood the rules better than he did.
