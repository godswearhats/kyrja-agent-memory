# Reversal arc × Fantasy — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## R-arc-Fa2-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A guild alchemist spends two years preparing to transmute heartstone -- the guild's highest trial -- under the confident expectation that her mastery will elevate her to Grandmaster. When she performs the transmutation before the full guild assembly, the heartstone shatters, and the guild strips her existing rank for the failed attempt.

> A = guild alchemist (Sable Voss); G = successful heartstone transmutation and elevation to Grandmaster; X = transmutation succeeds, rank elevated; ¬X = transmutation fails, existing rank stripped

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The guild library, a winter evening. Sable sat at the long table with the Grandmaster trial protocols open before her and a letter from the guild chancellor beside them.

The letter was brief. The chancellor had reviewed her application and her record -- fourteen years in the guild, nine successful transmutations at Masterwork grade, no failed trials on her ledger. He had approved her candidacy for the heartstone trial and scheduled the assembly for the first day of the autumn equinox, nine months hence. He noted, in the final line, that no alchemist had attempted the heartstone trial in eleven years, and that the guild would observe with particular attention.

Sable read the protocols again. The heartstone transmutation required a sequence of seven reagent infusions over four hours, each one calibrated to the stone's resonance frequency. The tolerances were extreme -- a deviation of more than two degrees in any infusion would fracture the crystalline lattice and destroy the stone. The last three alchemists to attempt it had all failed at the fifth infusion, where the resonance shifted unpredictably.

She had spent two years studying those failures. She had identified the resonance shift as a thermal artefact -- the stone's own heat output at the fifth stage altered the ambient temperature enough to throw the sixth infusion out of tolerance. Her solution was an insulating matrix of her own design, tested on lesser stones, which stabilised the thermal environment through the critical window.

She closed the protocols and placed the chancellor's letter inside the front cover. Nine months. She had already waited two years. She could feel the shape of what she would become on the other side of this.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The guild workshop, a summer afternoon, three months before the trial. The guild's examiner, Master Aldric, had come to observe her rehearsal -- a required checkpoint before any Grandmaster trial.

Sable performed the transmutation on a practice stone: a lesser grade, but one that exhibited the same resonance behaviour as the heartstone. She set the insulating matrix, calibrated the first infusion, and began.

Aldric watched without speaking. He stood at the edge of the workbench with his arms folded, his eyes moving between the stone and the instruments. He had examined every Grandmaster candidate for twenty years. He had watched the last three heartstone attempts fail.

The fifth infusion arrived. Sable placed the insulating matrix and adjusted the temperature. The resonance held steady through the transition. The sixth infusion flowed cleanly. The seventh sealed the transmutation. The practice stone turned from grey to the deep amber of a completed transmutation, and the instruments showed a stable crystalline lattice.

Aldric unfolded his arms. He walked to the workbench and examined the stone under the guild's magnifying lens. He checked the resonance readings twice.

"The matrix holds," he said. "I've never seen anyone stabilise the fifth-stage shift before."

"The failures weren't skill failures," Sable said. "They were thermal failures. The stone heats itself past the infusion tolerance. No one accounted for it because the protocols don't mention it."

Aldric set the lens down. "I'll enter this rehearsal as a pass in the trial record. You're cleared for the equinox." He paused at the door. "The assembly will be full. The chancellor has invited every active Master in the region. They want to see this succeed."

Sable cleaned the workbench after he left. She placed the practice stone in the display case by the window, where the afternoon light caught the amber and held it. She would look at it each morning for the next three months, and each morning it would tell her the same thing: the method works.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The guild storehouse, ten days before the trial. Sable had come to inspect the heartstone -- the actual stone, not a practice substitute. It was kept in a sealed chest, packed in silicate and stored at constant temperature.

She opened the chest and lifted the stone onto the examination table. It was larger than she had anticipated -- nearly twice the mass of her practice stones. She set up her instruments and measured the baseline resonance.

The readings were within the expected range but at the upper edge. The stone's natural resonance was higher than any practice stone she had worked with. Higher resonance meant more thermal output at the fifth stage. More thermal output meant the insulating matrix would bear a heavier load.

She calculated the margin. Her matrix had been designed for the practice stone's thermal profile. The heartstone's profile pushed the margin from comfortable to narrow -- not impossible, but narrow. She would need to increase the matrix density by approximately fifteen percent to maintain the same thermal stability.

She could make the adjustment. She had the materials and the time. But she had never tested the denser matrix on a live transmutation. Every rehearsal, every successful run, had used the original density.

She sealed the chest and returned the heartstone to storage. Ten days was not enough time to rehearse with a modified matrix on another practice stone, verify the calibration, and still arrive at the trial rested. She weighed the options and chose the one she could defend: she would use the original matrix and manage the narrower margin with her hands. She had fourteen years of experience. She trusted them.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The guild assembly hall, the first day of the autumn equinox. The benches were full -- every active Master in the region, the chancellor seated in the high chair, Aldric standing at the examination post. Sable stood at the transmutation table in the centre of the hall with the heartstone before her and seven reagent vessels arranged in sequence.

She began. The first four infusions were clean -- each one calibrated, each one absorbed into the stone's lattice without deviation. The instruments showed stable readings. The hall was silent except for the hum of the stone's resonance climbing toward the fifth stage.

She placed the insulating matrix. She calibrated the fifth infusion and delivered it. The stone's resonance shifted -- the same thermal artefact she had studied for two years. The matrix absorbed the heat. The instruments showed the temperature climbing, holding, climbing again. It was higher than any rehearsal. The stone's greater mass was generating more heat than her calculations had predicted, not by the fifteen percent she had estimated in the storehouse but by something closer to twenty.

She adjusted. She reduced the infusion rate, buying time for the matrix to dissipate the excess heat. The sixth infusion required a narrow window -- she had to begin it before the fifth-stage resonance decayed, or the lattice would destabilise. She watched the instruments and waited for the temperature to drop into tolerance.

It did not drop. The matrix held but did not cool. The thermal output from the stone had saturated it. She delivered the sixth infusion two degrees above tolerance because the window was closing and she had no choice.

The stone cracked. A single fissure ran from the base to the crown, and the amber colour drained from it as the crystalline lattice collapsed. The resonance fell to nothing. The instruments went flat.

The hall was silent. Sable stood with the sixth reagent vessel still in her hand and looked at the broken stone. The two halves sat on the table like a fruit split open, dull and grey inside where a moment ago the lattice had been alive.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The guild assembly hall, twenty minutes later. The chancellor had not left the high chair. The Masters remained on the benches. Aldric had completed his examination of the failed stone and returned to his post.

The chancellor spoke. He noted the failure of the heartstone transmutation. He noted that under guild statute, a failed Grandmaster trial invoked an automatic review of the candidate's existing rank. He asked Aldric for his assessment.

Aldric stated for the record that the transmutation had failed at the sixth infusion due to thermal instability. He stated that the candidate's insulating matrix -- her own innovation, untested at the heartstone's thermal load -- had been insufficient for the stone's actual output. He noted that the candidate had chosen not to modify the matrix despite identifying the discrepancy during her pre-trial inspection.

The chancellor asked Sable whether she wished to speak. She said the failure was hers. She said the thermal margin had been narrower than her rehearsals had prepared her for, and that she had chosen to proceed rather than request a delay. She did not offer an excuse.

The chancellor consulted with Aldric and two senior Masters at the bench. The consultation lasted several minutes. Then the chancellor delivered the ruling: the heartstone transmutation had failed; the candidate's existing rank of Master Alchemist, Third Degree, was suspended pending a competency review; the candidate was barred from further Grandmaster trials for a period of five years.

Sable removed her guild sigil from her collar and placed it on the transmutation table beside the broken stone. She left the assembly hall through the side door. The Masters on the benches watched her go, and the chancellor adjourned the session.

## R-arc-Fa2-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A young knight enters a tournament certain to claim the champion's prize, having defeated every opponent in the qualifying rounds. In the final bout, his opponent -- a disgraced former champion fighting under a borrowed crest -- defeats him in three passes and takes the title.

> A = the young knight (Ser Edric Vael); G = winning the tournament champion's title; X = victory in the final bout; ¬X = defeat in the final bout

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The tournament grounds at Ashenmere, the evening before the final bout. The qualifying rounds were finished. Ser Edric Vael sat in his tent with his squire, Wat, and his sponsor, Lord Carradine, who had ridden from the capital to watch.

"Five bouts," Lord Carradine said. He was sitting on the camp chair with a cup of wine, counting on his fingers. "Ser Aldous -- unhorsed in two passes. The Branneth twins -- both down in the first. Ser Morrigan -- retired after the third pass with a cracked shoulder guard. And the Thornfield boy, who lasted four passes and still couldn't touch you."

"The Thornfield boy was good," Edric said. He was checking the straps on his jousting saddle. "Better than Morrigan."

"The Thornfield boy is fifteen years old and had no business in the quarter-finals. You are twenty-three, you've trained under the best lance-master in the western provinces, and you have not lost a tournament bout in two years. Who is your opponent tomorrow?"

Wat looked up from polishing the breastplate. "A man calling himself Ser Caspen. No house, no colours. He's riding under a borrowed crest -- the Dunmore arms, but the Dunmores are extinct. No one in the camps knows him."

"A hedge knight?"

"He fights like one," Edric said. "I watched his quarter-final. Unorthodox seat, heavy lance, rides the rail instead of the centre line. He won his bouts, but he won them ugly."

Lord Carradine set down his wine. "Then tomorrow will be straightforward. You ride clean, you ride centre, and you take the champion's prize home to the capital. I've already written to the court that you'd be bringing it."

Edric tightened the last strap and set the saddle on the rack. "I'll bring it."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The tournament grounds, the following morning, an hour before the final bout. The stands were full. Edric rode a warm-up circuit in the practice yard, his horse moving beneath him with the trained precision of an animal that had done this a hundred times.

Wat ran alongside at the rail. "I found out more about Ser Caspen," he said, breathing hard. "He's not a hedge knight. One of the marshals recognised him. His real name is Corwin Hallam."

Edric reined in. "Hallam. The one who was stripped at Penmarch?"

"Stripped, exiled from the tournament circuit, banned from carrying arms in the eastern provinces. That was eight years ago. He's older now, heavier. The marshal said he barely recognised him."

"What was he stripped for?"

"A lance foul in the final at Penmarch. He struck below the shield line and crippled his opponent. The judges ruled it deliberate."

Edric turned his horse and walked it back to the start of the yard. "So my opponent is a disgraced knight fighting under a dead house's crest, eight years out of competition, carrying the weight of age and exile."

"That's the sum of it."

"Then nothing has changed." Edric leaned forward and patted the horse's neck. "Make sure the lance tips are regulation. I don't want him claiming a foul if I end this early."

**E3 (inversion scene).** The tournament field at Ashenmere, midday. The stands were packed. Lord Carradine sat in the sponsor's box. The marshals lined the rail. Edric took his position at the south end of the lists; Ser Caspen -- Corwin Hallam -- took the north.

First pass. The trumpet sounded. Edric spurred forward, lance levelled, riding the centre line with the form his lance-master had drilled into him since he was sixteen. He aimed for the centre of the shield. His aim was true.

Hallam did not ride the centre line. He shifted his horse half a stride to the right at the last moment -- an unorthodox line that changed the angle of contact. Edric's lance struck the edge of Hallam's shield and glanced wide. Hallam's lance, heavier and angled low, caught Edric's shield full in the centre and drove him backward in the saddle. The impact rang across the field. Edric kept his seat, but barely.

Second pass. Edric adjusted. He aimed for the off-centre line, anticipating the lateral shift. Hallam shifted the other way. Edric's lance found empty air where the shield had been a half-second earlier, and Hallam's lance struck him in the chest plate with a force that lifted him from the stirrups. He landed hard on the saddle, his vision blurring for a moment, and crossed the end of the lists with his shield arm numb.

Third pass. Edric set his lance and rode. He committed to the centre line -- the line he knew, the line he had trained on for seven years. Hallam shifted again, the same half-stride, the same impossible timing. Edric's lance grazed the shield rim. Hallam's lance struck him squarely below the right shoulder. The impact broke Edric's grip on his own lance, drove him sideways over the cantle, and put him on the ground.

He lay on his back in the dirt of the tournament field. Through the slits of his visor he could see the sky above Ashenmere, very blue and very still. The crowd was making a sound he had never heard directed at him before.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The tournament grounds, the marshals' tent, thirty minutes after the bout. Edric sat on the bench with his armour half-removed. Wat worked on the buckles in silence. Lord Carradine had not come.

The chief marshal entered the tent with the official result. He read it aloud, as protocol required: the final bout of the Ashenmere tournament was awarded to Ser Caspen, three passes to none. The champion's prize -- the silver lance and the year's title -- would be presented at the evening feast.

"You should know," the marshal added, "that we've identified Ser Caspen as Corwin Hallam, formerly banned from the circuit. The ban was issued by the eastern provinces and does not extend to this jurisdiction. His entry stands. The result stands."

Edric pulled off his gauntlet and set it on the bench. His right hand was shaking. "He rode the same move three times. The same half-stride. I saw it after the first pass and I still couldn't answer it."

The marshal said nothing. Protocol did not require him to.

Edric looked at Wat. "Find Lord Carradine. Tell him I'll ride back to the capital in the morning." He paused. "And tell him not to send the letter to the court. The one he already wrote."

Wat nodded and left. Edric sat alone in the tent with the half-removed armour around him and the sound of the crowd still carrying across the grounds, cheering for a champion whose name they did not know.

## R-arc-Fa2-3

**6 beats, chronological, embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A temple order's high seer performs a divination declaring that the besieging army will break the city walls within seven days. The city's defenders prepare to evacuate. On the seventh day, the siege collapses when the besieging army's supply train is destroyed by a flash flood, and the attackers withdraw.

> A = the city's ruling council, led by the Lord Protector; G = survival of the city through the siege; X = city falls, walls breached by day seven; ¬X = siege collapses, besieging army withdraws

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The council chamber of the walled city of Tessavere, an evening in late autumn. The Lord Protector, Margrave Orren, convened an emergency session after the besieging army of Kharvane completed its encirclement that morning. Present were the six council members, the captain of the garrison, and the High Seer of the Temple of the Three.

The council first heard the captain's report. The Kharvane host numbered nine thousand -- foot soldiers, siege engineers, and cavalry -- and had established fortified camps on all four approaches. The city's garrison numbered fourteen hundred. The walls were sound but old, and the eastern section had not been reinforced since the last siege, forty years prior.

The Lord Protector then called upon the High Seer, Mother Vashti, to deliver the divination she had performed at the temple that afternoon. The High Seer stood and read from the temple scroll:

> *The bones were cast at the fourth hour upon the stone of sight, in the presence of three acolytes and the temple warden, as protocol requires. The pattern was read thrice and recorded: the eastern wall will be breached before the seventh sunrise. The city of Tessavere will fall to the army of Kharvane. The signs do not equivocate. The bones gave no secondary reading, no conditional, no alternative. I record this with the full weight of my office.*

The council received the divination in silence. The High Seer's record over thirty years was without a documented failure. She had foretold the drought of the previous decade, the death of two margraves, and the exact date of the last Kharvane raid. No council member present could cite an instance in which her casting had been wrong.

The Lord Protector asked the captain whether the eastern wall could be reinforced in seven days. The captain said it could not -- the stonework required a fortnight of labour they did not have.

The Lord Protector ordered the council to begin evacuation planning.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The eastern wall, the following morning, day one of the siege. The Lord Protector walked the parapet with the captain of the garrison and the chief mason. Below them, on the plain, the Kharvane siege engineers were assembling two trebuchets within range of the eastern section.

The chief mason pointed to the base of the wall. A web of cracks ran through the mortar at the foundation -- old damage, patched but never properly repaired. He estimated the wall could withstand four to five days of direct bombardment from engines of the size being assembled. On the sixth or seventh day, depending on the accuracy of the Kharvane engineers, the foundation would give.

"The seer said seven days," the Lord Protector said. "You're confirming her estimate with stonework."

The mason said he was confirming it with cracks. He had no opinion on bones or seers, but the wall would fail within the week. That was masonry, not prophecy.

The Lord Protector returned to the council chamber and authorised the evacuation of civilians through the western tunnels, beginning that night. The first cohort -- the old, the young, the sick -- would leave under cover of darkness. The garrison would hold the walls as long as possible to cover the withdrawal.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The market square, day four. The bombardment had been continuous for three days. Two sections of the eastern wall showed visible damage -- deep fractures running from the battlements to the base. The garrison had attempted one sally to disable the trebuchets and had been driven back with casualties.

The Lord Protector was crossing the square when his aide, Doren, caught up with him carrying a dispatch from the western scouts.

"A report from the hill watch," Doren said. "The Kharvane supply train is camped in the Galden gorge, seven miles west of the siege line. Forty wagons, provisions, and the siege timber reserves."

"The gorge?" The Lord Protector stopped walking. "The Galden runs through the gorge. If the river rises --"

"The river is low. It's been dry for three weeks."

"And if it rains in the highlands?"

Doren shrugged. "The highland weather moves fast this time of year. But the Kharvane quartermasters know the gorge. They'll have positioned the wagons above the flood line."

The Lord Protector looked at the sky. Clear, dry, no sign of cloud from any direction. "Continue the evacuation. We stay with the seer's timeline."

He walked on. Behind him, another impact struck the eastern wall, and dust rose from the battlements.

**E4 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The council chamber, the evening of day five. The council received the captain's damage report: the eastern wall would hold one more day, possibly two. The breach was now inevitable without reinforcement that could not arrive.

Mother Vashti was present. The Lord Protector asked whether she had performed any supplementary divination. She said she had not. The bones had spoken clearly and required no repetition. The city would fall.

The Lord Protector confirmed the final evacuation schedule. The remaining civilians would leave through the western tunnels on the morning of day six. The garrison would hold through day seven and then withdraw, collapsing the tunnels behind them. The city would be abandoned.

The council ratified the order. The High Seer entered her confirmation in the temple scroll: *Day five. The pattern holds. The bones are unchanged.*

**E5 (inversion scene).** The eastern wall, dawn of day seven. The Lord Protector stood on the battlement with the captain, watching the Kharvane lines through the early light. The breach was imminent -- the base of the wall had shifted inward overnight, and the chief mason had reported that the next volley from the trebuchets would likely bring the section down.

The trebuchets did not fire. The morning passed without bombardment. The Kharvane camps, visible from the wall, showed movement -- but the movement was wrong. Soldiers were striking tents, not manning siege positions. Engineers were dismantling equipment, not loading it. Cavalry was forming column, not assault line.

At midday, a rider approached the city gate under a flag of parley. The captain brought the message to the Lord Protector: the Kharvane commander was withdrawing. The message was terse. It did not explain.

Two hours later, the scouts' report arrived from the hill watch. It had rained in the highlands overnight -- a violent, concentrated storm that had not reached the city. The Galden river had risen six feet in four hours and swept through the gorge. The Kharvane supply train -- forty wagons of provisions, siege timber, engineering supplies, and the army's entire reserve of ammunition for the trebuchets -- was destroyed. The wagons had been positioned above the normal flood line but below the line of a highland flash flood, and the quartermasters had miscalculated the drainage speed of the gorge.

Without provisions or ammunition, the siege could not continue. The Kharvane commander had no choice.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The council chamber, the evening of day seven. The Kharvane army was visible from the walls, retreating eastward in column. The city stood.

The Lord Protector convened the council. He noted for the record that the siege had ended, that the city had not fallen, and that the eastern wall, though damaged, had not been breached.

He turned to Mother Vashti. "Your divination said the city would fall before the seventh sunrise. The seventh sunrise has passed. The army is gone."

The High Seer stood. Her face showed nothing the Lord Protector could read. She said: "The bones showed the fall of Tessavere. The pattern was unambiguous. I have reviewed the casting and I stand by the reading."

"The city stands," the Lord Protector said. "Your reading was wrong."

"The reading was correct. The outcome was not the one the bones foretold. I cannot explain the discrepancy. I will record it in the temple scroll as the first failure of a primary divination in my tenure."

The Lord Protector looked at her for a long time. Then he dictated the council's resolution: the evacuation order was rescinded, the garrison was to begin repairs on the eastern wall, and the temple's divination would be entered in the city's chronicle alongside the actual outcome, so that future councils could weigh the seer's record with this result included.

Mother Vashti wrote in the scroll: *Day seven. The city of Tessavere stands. The bones were wrong. I do not know why.*

She closed the scroll and left the chamber. The Lord Protector watched her go and said nothing further.

## R-arc-Fa2-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5, present tense**

**Scenario.** A wizard's apprentice labours for three years to master the binding of a greater elemental, the final test required for admission to the Circle of Masters. On the day of the trial, the elemental breaks the binding circle, and the apprentice is expelled from the order for the catastrophic failure.

> A = apprentice (Tallis); G = successful binding of a greater elemental and admission to the Circle of Masters; X = binding holds, apprentice admitted; ¬X = binding fails catastrophically, apprentice expelled

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E1 (training confirmation), E2 (rehearsal success), E3 (the trial), E4 (aftermath), E5 (expulsion). Depicted order opens with the trial itself.

**E3 (inversion scene -- depicted first).** The binding chamber, sixty feet below the Tower of Tern. The Circle of Masters sits in the gallery above the chamber floor -- seven figures in grey, their faces lit by the phosphorescence of the warding stones set into the walls. The apprentice, Tallis, stands in the centre of the chamber beside a binding circle inscribed in silver on the basalt floor.

The circle is his work. He inscribed it over three days, following the schema he developed during his final year of study -- a modified Varenthi pattern with a double-closed interior ring that he believes will hold a greater elemental where the standard single ring cannot. The Masters examined the circle that morning and approved it for the trial.

Tallis speaks the invocation. The air above the circle thickens, darkens, and the elemental materialises -- a column of heat and pressure that fills the circle from edge to edge. The binding holds. The silver lines glow white with the force pressing against them. The elemental tests the perimeter, pushing outward at three points simultaneously.

The first ring holds. The second ring holds. The double-closure is doing what Tallis designed it to do -- distributing the force across two containment surfaces instead of one. The Masters in the gallery are watching without expression. Two minutes pass. Three.

At four minutes, the elemental changes its approach. It stops pressing outward and begins pressing downward, into the basalt floor beneath the silver. The binding circle is inscribed on the surface. The elemental is attacking the substrate.

Tallis sees the problem but has no remedy for it. His schema accounts for lateral and upward force. It does not account for a directed downward assault on the floor itself. The basalt cracks. The silver line deforms as the stone beneath it splits. The inner ring breaks first -- a gap of two inches where the silver bends into the fissure. The outer ring breaks a second later.

The elemental pours through the gap like water through a broken dam. The warding stones in the walls flare and contain it before it reaches the gallery, but the binding is gone. The trial is over. The chamber fills with the acrid smell of scorched stone and failed silver.

Tallis stands in the centre of the broken circle. He does not move.

**E1 (expectation-set scene -- chronological backtrack).** The office of Master Solen, head of the Circle, three years earlier. Tallis sits across the desk. He is eighteen, newly promoted from novice to apprentice, and Solen has just assigned him the binding track -- the path that leads, in three years, to the greater-elemental trial.

"You are the youngest apprentice I have placed on this track in my tenure," Solen says. "Your aptitude scores in containment theory are the highest this tower has recorded. That is why I am assigning you now rather than making you wait the customary additional year."

Tallis asks what the trial requires. Solen describes it: the apprentice designs and inscribes a binding circle of their own devising, summons a greater elemental, and holds the binding for ten minutes. If the binding holds, the apprentice enters the Circle. If it fails, the consequences depend on the nature of the failure.

"And if the circle breaks?"

"If the circle breaks under a greater elemental, the release of force is significant. The warding stones will protect the gallery, but the failure itself is treated as a disqualifying event. The apprentice is expelled. There is no second attempt."

Tallis absorbs this. One trial. One chance. Success or expulsion.

"I understand," he says.

"Good. You have three years."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene -- chronological backtrack).** The practice chamber, one level above the binding chamber, two months before the trial. Tallis runs the final rehearsal of his modified Varenthi circle against a lesser elemental -- the strongest test subject permitted before the actual trial.

The lesser elemental is perhaps a quarter of the strength of a greater. Tallis summons it into the circle. It presses outward. The double-closed ring distributes the force cleanly. The inner ring takes the initial load; the outer ring absorbs the spillover. The binding holds for twenty minutes without stress. Tallis dismisses the elemental and examines the silver -- no deformation, no heat damage, no signs of strain.

Master Solen observes from the doorway. He enters the chamber and inspects the circle himself, running his fingers along the silver lines.

"The double closure is elegant," he says. "I have not seen it used before. Where did you find the variation?"

"I derived it. The standard Varenthi pattern fails because the single ring concentrates force at the contact points. Two rings distribute it. The mathematics are straightforward."

Solen nods. "A lesser elemental is not a greater. The difference in force is not linear -- it is exponential. Your circle may behave differently under full load."

"I have modelled the force distribution up to three times the lesser-elemental benchmark. The double closure holds at every level I can simulate."

Solen looks at him for a long moment. "Simulation is not containment. But your preparation is thorough, and your design is sound. I will recommend you for the trial."

Tallis thanks him. After Solen leaves, Tallis stands in the practice chamber and looks at his circle -- the clean silver lines, the symmetrical double closure, the work of three years made visible on the stone floor. It is ready. He is ready.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The anteroom outside the binding chamber, immediately after the failed trial. Tallis sits on the stone bench. His hands are steady but his face is grey. The smell of scorched basalt clings to his robes.

Master Solen comes through the chamber door and stands before him. The other Masters remain in the gallery.

"The elemental attacked the floor," Tallis says. "The substrate. I designed for lateral and upward force. I did not design for a directed downward assault on the stone itself."

"No," Solen says. "You did not."

"Could I have? Is that a known behaviour?"

"Greater elementals have broken binding circles through substrate attack on three prior occasions in this tower's history. The records are in the archive. They are available to all apprentices."

Tallis is quiet for a long time. "I did not read them. I read the theory, the mathematics, the force models. I did not read the failure records."

"No," Solen says again.

**E5 (expulsion scene).** The great hall of the Tower of Tern, the following morning. The seven Masters of the Circle sit at the long table. Tallis stands before them. The hall is otherwise empty.

Master Solen reads the finding: the binding trial has failed; the failure was catastrophic, not controlled; the apprentice's circle was breached by the elemental through substrate attack; the warding stones contained the release, preventing harm to the Masters, but the binding was lost entirely at four minutes and twelve seconds, well short of the required ten.

Solen asks Tallis whether he wishes to address the Circle before the ruling.

Tallis speaks. He says the failure was his. He says the double-closure design was sound against the forces he anticipated but that he failed to research the full range of behaviours a greater elemental might employ. He says he studied the theory of containment but not the history of failure. He does not ask for mercy.

Solen delivers the ruling. The trial is recorded as a catastrophic failure. The apprentice is expelled from the Tower of Tern and barred from re-application to any tower in the order. His research notes and circle schematics are retained in the archive for study. His name is entered in the failure record.

Tallis removes the apprentice's cord from his wrist and places it on the table. He leaves the hall through the main door. The morning light outside is bright after the dim interior, and he stands on the tower steps for a moment, blinking. Behind him, the door closes. Below him, the road leads away from the tower and into the lowlands, where there are no circles, no elementals, and no second attempts.
