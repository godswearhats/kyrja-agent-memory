# Reversal arc x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## R-arc-Hi1-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style. Era: 1780s France, pre-Revolution.**

**Scenario.** A provincial tax farmer pursues a royal warrant to collect the gabelle (salt tax) across three new districts, a monopoly that would triple his revenue. After two years of petition, a favourable ruling from the Conseil d'État, and the personal endorsement of the Controller-General of Finances, the warrant is revoked on the day of its issuance -- because the districts in question have just been granted a blanket salt-tax exemption by the king as a concession to quell unrest, rendering the warrant unenforceable.

> A = tax farmer (Jean-Claude Maret); G = obtaining the royal warrant to collect the gabelle in the three new districts; X = warrant granted and enforceable (success); not-X = warrant revoked as unenforceable (failure).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The offices of the Ferme Générale in Paris, a morning in April 1786. Maret sat in the anteroom of the Controller-General's secretary, waiting for the third time that month. He had arrived from Dijon two years earlier with a petition, a letter of introduction from the intendant of Burgundy, and a detailed revenue projection for extending the gabelle into three districts along the Saône valley that had never been taxed for salt.

The districts were a gap in the map -- small enough that the Ferme Générale had never prioritised them, large enough that Maret's projections showed forty thousand livres a year in collectible revenue. He had walked the territory himself, counted the salt merchants, estimated the household consumption, and calculated the yield per district with the thoroughness of a man who understood that the Ferme awarded monopolies to petitioners who did the Crown's arithmetic for it.

The secretary admitted him at noon. Maret presented his revised petition -- the fourth revision, incorporating every objection the Conseil d'État had raised in previous hearings. The secretary reviewed it, noted the Controller-General's prior endorsement, and told Maret that the Conseil would hear the petition at its next session. If the ruling was favourable, the warrant would be drafted, sealed, and issued within the month.

Maret thanked him and returned to his lodgings. Two years. Four revisions. Every objection answered, every number verified, every political courtesy observed. He wrote to his wife in Dijon and told her to expect him home by summer, with the warrant in hand.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The Conseil d'État's hearing chamber, a Thursday in June. Maret stood before the panel of four councillors and presented his petition for the final time. The Controller-General's endorsement was read into the record. The revenue projections were reviewed. The intendant of Burgundy had submitted a letter of support, noting that the districts' current lack of salt-tax collection represented an anomaly in the regional fiscal structure and that extending the gabelle would bring them into conformity with neighbouring jurisdictions.

The senior councillor asked whether any party had registered an objection. The clerk confirmed that none had been received. The councillor asked Maret whether the districts' local authorities had been consulted. Maret produced three letters from the subdélégués of the three districts, each acknowledging the petition and raising no formal opposition.

The panel deliberated briefly and returned a favourable ruling. The warrant would be drafted by the Chancery, sealed with the royal seal, and issued to Maret within fourteen days. The clerk entered the ruling in the register and dismissed the session.

Maret left the chamber and walked to the Palais-Royal, where he sat on a bench in the garden and allowed himself, for the first time in two years, to calculate his future. Forty thousand livres a year. A monopoly that would last until the Ferme's next renewal -- seven years at minimum. He would repay his debts, expand his household, and establish himself as one of the principal tax farmers in eastern France. The petition was granted. The ruling was in the register. The warrant was fourteen days away.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Maret's lodgings in the Rue Saint-Honoré, a Tuesday morning, nine days after the ruling. A letter arrived from the intendant of Burgundy -- not the formal correspondence Maret had expected, but a brief personal note, written in haste.

The intendant wrote that there had been disturbances in the Saône districts over the preceding weeks. The salt merchants -- who had operated freely under the tax exemption that had prevailed for decades -- had organised petitions against the extension of the gabelle. Two of the petitions had reached the provincial parlement. More troublingly, a crowd of several hundred had gathered at the subdélégué's residence in the principal town and refused to disperse until he agreed to forward their grievance to Versailles.

The intendant noted that the disturbances were not yet serious -- no violence, no property destruction, no military intervention required. But he wished Maret to know that the political situation in the districts had changed since the Conseil's ruling, and that the court was aware of the unrest. He advised Maret to collect his warrant promptly and to begin enforcement with discretion.

Maret read the letter twice. The unrest was regrettable but not, in his estimation, material. Tax resistance was endemic in the provinces -- every new collection faced it, and every collection eventually prevailed. The warrant was the instrument of the king's authority. Once it was in his hand, the resistance would give way to the arithmetic of enforcement: penalties, seizures, and the slow capitulation that always followed.

He wrote to the intendant thanking him for the notice and confirming that he would collect the warrant on the appointed day.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The Chancery offices at Versailles, the fourteenth day after the ruling -- the date the warrant was to be issued. Maret arrived at nine in the morning, dressed in his best coat, with his petition file under his arm. The clerk at the Chancery's front desk recognised him and asked him to wait.

He waited for two hours. At eleven, the clerk returned and escorted him not to the warrant office but to the private study of the Controller-General's deputy. The deputy sat behind his desk with two documents in front of him.

The first was Maret's warrant -- drafted, sealed, and ready for delivery. The second was a royal ordinance, dated the previous day, bearing the king's signature and the seal of the Conseil d'État. The deputy read the operative clause aloud: in response to representations from the parlement of Burgundy and in consideration of the present state of public order in the Saône valley, the king granted a blanket exemption from the gabelle to the three districts named in Maret's warrant, effective immediately and without term.

The exemption did not mention Maret. It did not reference his petition or his warrant. It was a separate instrument, issued through a separate channel, in response to a separate political concern. But its effect was total: the warrant in the deputy's hand authorised Maret to collect a tax that no longer applied in the territory it covered. The document was valid. It was also meaningless.

The deputy set both documents on the desk. He said the Controller-General regretted the outcome and wished to convey his personal assurance that the Ferme would consider Maret for future opportunities.

Maret looked at the two documents side by side -- the warrant and the ordinance, dated one day apart, one granting him a monopoly and the other dissolving the tax that made it worth holding.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The garden of the Palais-Royal, that afternoon. Maret sat on the same bench where he had sat fourteen days earlier, calculating his future. The warrant was in his case. The ordinance's text was in his memory.

Two years of petition. Four revisions. A favourable ruling from the Conseil d'État. The Controller-General's personal endorsement. A warrant drafted, sealed, and ready for delivery. And the answer was no -- not because the petition was refused, not because the ruling was overturned, not because a single official had found fault with his arithmetic, but because the king had given away the tax itself to quiet a crowd of salt merchants whose grievance had reached Versailles six days before the warrant reached Maret's hand.

He opened his case and looked at the warrant. The seal was perfect. The calligraphy was precise. It was the most valuable document he had ever held, and it entitled him to nothing.

He closed the case, stood, and walked to the coach office to book his passage back to Dijon. He would arrive home without the revenue, without the monopoly, and with two years of expenses he could not recover. The intendant's letter had warned him the situation had changed. He had read it and concluded that the resistance would yield. The resistance had yielded -- not to him, but to the king, who had resolved the problem by removing the prize.

## R-arc-Hi1-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven. Era: 1860s American Civil War.**

**Scenario.** A Confederate blockade runner captain is certain his ship will reach Nassau with its cargo of cotton -- the most valuable single shipment to leave Wilmington that season. His ship is faster than any Union vessel on the station, the moon is dark, and his pilot knows every channel in the Cape Fear River. A freak mechanical failure -- a cracked steam pipe in the engine room -- leaves the ship dead in the water inside the blockade line at dawn, and the nearest Union corvette takes her without firing a shot.

> A = captain (Tobias Harlan); G = running the blockade and reaching Nassau with the cotton cargo; X = successful run (ship reaches Nassau); not-X = capture (ship taken by Union navy).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The captain's cabin aboard the *Dorada*, moored at the wharf in Wilmington, the evening before departure. Harlan sat at his chart table with his pilot, a man named Rudge, and the ship's engineer, Colson.

"Tide turns at two in the morning," Rudge said. "New moon. No moonlight until we're past Fort Fisher and into open water. I've run this channel nine times. I can do it blind."

Harlan looked at the manifest pinned to the bulkhead. Eleven hundred bales of cotton, packed in the hold and on deck, bound for Nassau. The cargo's value in Nassau was sixty thousand dollars in gold. In Liverpool, through the broker waiting at Nassau, it was three times that.

"Engine status," Harlan said.

Colson wiped his hands on a rag. "Boilers are sound. Packing's fresh. I overhauled the port condenser last week. She'll make thirteen knots with the current. The fastest thing the Federals have on the station is the *Ticonderoga*, and she makes eleven in a following sea."

"So we outrun them."

"We don't even see them," Rudge said. "The *Dorada* draws seven feet. The deep-draft Federals anchor three miles off the bar. I take us through the inner channel, we pass under Fort Fisher's guns, and we're in open water before they know we've moved. By the time the sun comes up, we're twenty miles south and making for Nassau at full steam."

Harlan rolled up the chart. "We sail at two. Get some sleep."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The wheelhouse of the *Dorada*, 2:40 a.m. The ship had cleared the wharf and was moving downriver in darkness. Harlan stood behind Rudge, who was at the wheel. The only light was the dim glow of the binnacle.

Rudge navigated by memory and soundings. A leadsman in the bow called the depth in a low voice: three fathoms, two and a half, three again. The channel narrowed between two shoals that Rudge identified by the sound of the current against the banks. He made the turn without slowing.

At 3:15 a.m. they passed Fort Fisher. A signal lantern blinked twice from the rampart -- the recognition signal, confirming the *Dorada* as friendly. Rudge blinked the reply with a shutter-lamp and held course.

At 3:40 a.m. they crossed the outer bar and entered the open Atlantic. Harlan raised his glass and scanned the horizon. Nothing. The Union blockade line was to the north and east, anchored in deep water where Rudge had predicted. The *Dorada* was south of them, running dark, making twelve and a half knots in a light swell.

"Clean," Rudge said.

"Clean," Harlan confirmed. He went below to check the cargo lashings and told Colson to hold full steam until dawn. By sunrise they would be fifty miles south of the blockade line, in open water, with Nassau two days ahead.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The engine room, 5:10 a.m. Colson was making his rounds, checking pressures and temperatures, when he heard a sound he did not like -- a high, thin whistle from the port-side steam trunk, just above the junction where the main pipe fed the engine.

He put his hand near the trunk without touching it. The heat was wrong -- concentrated in a line along the upper surface, as if the steam was finding a path it shouldn't have. He sent his fireman to fetch a lantern and looked more closely.

There was a crack in the pipe. Not a burst -- a hairline fracture, running along a seam that had been welded during the last overhaul. The steam was bleeding through the fracture in a thin jet that would widen with every minute of operation.

Colson went topside and found Harlan on the quarterdeck.

"We have a problem," Colson said. "Port-side steam trunk. Hairline crack along a weld seam. I can patch it, but I need to shut the port engine down to do it."

"How long?"

"Two hours. Maybe three. I have to let the pipe cool before I can work on it."

"Can we run on the starboard engine alone?"

"Six knots. Maybe seven."

Harlan looked east. The sky was beginning to lighten. Dawn was forty minutes away. At six knots, the *Dorada* would still be within range of any Union cruiser that had spotted them during the night crossing. At thirteen knots, she was uncatchable. At six, she was slow enough that any steam corvette on the station could overhaul her before Colson finished the repair.

"Start the repair," Harlan said. "How bad does it get if we don't shut down?"

"The crack widens. The pipe fails. We lose the port engine entirely, and the flash-steam could kill anyone in the engine room when it goes."

"Shut it down," Harlan said.

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The quarterdeck of the *Dorada*, 6:20 a.m. Dawn. The port engine was cold. The starboard engine pushed the ship south at six and a half knots. Colson was in the engine room with the pipe exposed, waiting for it to cool enough to work.

Harlan stood at the rail with his glass raised. The horizon was clear to the south and west. To the northeast, where the blockade line anchored, a column of smoke rose against the pale sky. Then a second.

"Two ships," Rudge said from the wheelhouse. "They've seen our smoke."

Harlan watched the columns. They were moving -- converging on the *Dorada*'s position. The Union cruisers had been farther south than expected, or they had repositioned during the night. Either way, they were under steam and closing.

"How long until they reach us?" Harlan asked.

Rudge calculated. "Forty minutes. Maybe less. They're making eleven, twelve knots."

"Colson," Harlan called down the engine-room hatch. "How long?"

"An hour," Colson's voice came back. "The pipe's still too hot."

Harlan lowered the glass. Forty minutes to contact. Sixty minutes to repair. The arithmetic was the same kind he had relied on the night before -- simple, clean, and final. The speed advantage that had carried them through the channel and past the blockade line was gone, bled away through a crack in a pipe that had been welded three weeks earlier and had held through nine previous runs.

He could see the lead ship now without the glass -- a corvette, black hull, Federal ensign visible in the morning light. She was not firing. She did not need to. The *Dorada* was making six knots with eleven hundred bales of cotton on deck, and the corvette was making twelve.

At 6:55 a.m. the corvette came alongside and fired a signal gun -- a single blank charge, the formal demand to heave to. Harlan looked at the cotton, at the corvette's guns, at Colson emerging from the engine hatch with the pipe wrench still in his hand.

"Strike the colours," Harlan said.

Rudge looked at him.

"She's faster than we are and she has guns. We don't have a port engine and we're carrying cargo, not ammunition. Strike the colours."

Rudge pulled down the Confederate ensign. The Federal boarding party came across twenty minutes later. The prize officer inventoried the cotton, took Harlan's manifest, and informed him that the *Dorada* and her cargo were seized as contraband of war. Harlan handed over the ship's papers and watched from the quarterdeck as the Federal crew took the wheel.

The fastest ship on the station, the darkest night of the month, and a pilot who knew every sandbar in the Cape Fear River. The run had been perfect until a weld seam three inches long opened in a pipe that wasn't supposed to fail, and the twenty-knot gap between the *Dorada* and the Federal corvette closed to nothing in the forty minutes Colson needed and didn't have.

## R-arc-Hi1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (expectation set) -> E3 (expectation reinforced) -> E4 (first crack) -> E5 (inversion) -> E6 (inversion acknowledged). Era: 1450s, Ottoman siege of Constantinople. Embedded-document voice.**

**Scenario.** A Byzantine engineer proposes a plan to seal the Golden Horn with a reinforced chain and boom, preventing the Ottoman fleet from entering the harbour. The chain is forged, tested, and deployed. The emperor himself inspects it and declares the harbour secure. The Ottomans bypass the chain entirely by dragging seventy ships overland across the Galata ridge on greased logs, launching them into the harbour behind the chain, rendering the defence worthless.

> A = Byzantine engineer (Theodoros Kantakouzenos); G = sealing the Golden Horn against the Ottoman fleet; X = harbour remains sealed (Ottoman fleet excluded); not-X = Ottoman fleet enters the harbour (chain defence fails).

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E5 -- E6 -- E1. Depicted order opens with the aftermath.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The morning after the harbour fell. The harbour-master's log, recovered from the tower above the chain-boom's eastern anchor, contained a final entry in a hand that was not the harbour-master's:

> *The chain holds. I write this so that the record is clear. The chain across the Golden Horn is intact. No ship has broken it. No ship has passed beneath it. The engineering is sound. The forging is sound. The deployment is sound. The harbour has fallen because the Ottoman fleet is inside it -- not through the chain, but over the ridge north of Galata, dragged on wooden cradles across a road that did not exist three days ago. Seventy ships are at anchor behind our line. The chain seals a passage that the enemy no longer needs to use. I am Theodoros Kantakouzenos, and I built this defence, and it has not failed. The enemy has made it irrelevant. That is not the same thing, though the result is identical.*

The entry was undated but the ink was fresh when the harbour-master's tower was abandoned. The chain remained in place across the Golden Horn for several days afterward. No Ottoman vessel ever tested it.

**E2 (expectation-set scene).** The imperial council chamber, four months earlier. Theodoros stood before the emperor and the council with a scale model of the Golden Horn on the table between them.

He described the plan: a heavy iron chain, supported by floating booms, stretched across the mouth of the Golden Horn from the sea walls on the southern shore to the tower at Galata on the northern. The chain would be forged in sections and linked on-site, anchored to stone pylons at each end, and tensioned by windlasses mounted in the towers. Any ship attempting to enter the harbour would have to break or pass over the chain, and the chain's weight and the boom's depth would make both impossible for vessels of the Ottoman fleet's draught.

The emperor asked whether the chain could be broken by ramming. Theodoros said no -- the iron was bridge-grade, the links were eight inches across, and the boom's floating timber absorbed impact energy before it reached the chain. He had tested a quarter-scale model in the harbour and it had held against the heaviest vessel he could find.

The emperor asked how long to forge and deploy. Theodoros said three months. The emperor told him to begin and assigned him the harbour-master's resources, the imperial foundry's iron stock, and a company of engineers from the garrison.

Theodoros bowed and withdrew. The defence of the Golden Horn -- the harbour that protected Constantinople's northern flank and the anchorage for whatever remained of the Byzantine fleet -- was now his responsibility.

**E3 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The chain-boom's eastern anchor tower, three months later. The chain was complete. Theodoros stood on the tower's observation platform with the harbour-master and watched the final section drawn taut by the windlass crew.

The chain stretched across the Golden Horn in a shallow arc, supported by a line of floating booms -- heavy timber logs, chained together, riding low in the water. The combined weight of chain and boom was enormous. The harbour-master noted in his log that the barrier was the heaviest he had seen deployed in the Golden Horn in his lifetime.

The emperor arrived by barge at midday for the formal inspection. He walked the length of the eastern tower, examined the windlass mechanism, and asked Theodoros to demonstrate the chain's tension under load. Theodoros ordered a retired transport vessel -- the largest hull available -- driven at the chain under sail. The ship struck the boom at cruising speed. The timber absorbed the impact. The chain flexed but held. The ship rebounded and drifted clear.

The emperor watched the test in silence. When it was done, he turned to Theodoros.

"The harbour is secure?"

"The harbour is sealed, Majesty. No ship afloat can break this chain."

The emperor placed his hand on the windlass housing. He told the harbour-master to enter the inspection in the official record and to note that the Golden Horn's defence was approved by imperial authority. The harbour-master did so. The emperor departed. The chain remained in place, taut and gleaming in the afternoon light.

**E4 (first-crack scene).** The northern wall's observation post, two weeks later. The Ottoman siege had been underway for a month. Theodoros had been assigned to the harbour defence and had not left the chain-tower complex since the bombardment began. On this morning, a messenger arrived from the northern wall with a report from the garrison commander.

The report noted unusual activity on the slopes north of Galata, behind the Genoese colony. Ottoman work parties had been observed cutting timber and clearing a path along the ridge that ran from the Bosphorus shore to the upper harbour. The path's purpose was unclear. The garrison commander speculated that it might be a supply road for the Ottoman batteries positioned above Galata.

Theodoros read the report and looked at his map. The ridge ran from the Bosphorus -- where the main Ottoman fleet was anchored outside the chain -- to the upper Golden Horn, behind the chain's defensive line. It was a mile and a half of rough terrain, steep in places, wooded, and never used as a road.

He considered the possibility that the Ottomans intended to move ships overland. He dismissed it. Moving warships over a ridge was a feat of engineering that would require thousands of men, purpose-built cradles, and a prepared road surface. The logistics were prohibitive. The terrain was wrong. No army in Theodoros's experience had attempted it on this scale.

He filed the report and returned his attention to the chain's daily maintenance.

**E5 (inversion scene).** The chain-tower's observation platform, a morning two weeks after the garrison's report. Theodoros was conducting his morning inspection when the harbour-master's watch officer called him to the northern face of the tower.

Theodoros looked north, toward the upper harbour. The water there was empty -- it had been empty since the chain was deployed, because no Ottoman vessel could reach it.

It was no longer empty. Ships were in the upper harbour -- Ottoman galleys, emerging from behind the ridge line, sliding down prepared wooden ramps into the water. As Theodoros watched, a second galley appeared, then a third, each riding a wooden cradle that was being lowered on greased logs by teams of men and oxen visible on the slope above.

He counted. Ten ships. Twenty. The launches continued throughout the morning. By noon, he counted more than fifty, with more still appearing on the ridge.

The Ottomans had built a road over the Galata ridge -- cleared the timber, graded the slope, laid greased logs as runners, and constructed wooden cradles to carry the ships' hulls. They had moved their fleet overland, around the chain, and launched it into the harbour behind the defensive line.

Theodoros stood on the platform and watched the galleys take their positions in the upper harbour. The chain was intact. The chain was irrelevant. The enemy fleet was inside the Golden Horn, and it had entered without passing through the mouth of the harbour at all.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The chain-tower's chart room, that evening. Theodoros sat at the harbour-master's desk. The harbour-master stood by the window, watching the Ottoman galleys anchor in formation behind the chain line.

"Seventy-two ships," the harbour-master said. "My watch counted seventy-two."

Theodoros looked at the chain on the map -- a thick black line across the harbour mouth, exactly where he had placed it, exactly as he had designed it. The chain sealed the entrance. The Ottoman fleet had not used the entrance. They had carried their ships over a mountain because the entrance was sealed, and now they were inside the harbour in greater numbers than if they had simply sailed in through an undefended mouth.

"The chain forced them to do this," the harbour-master said. "If the chain weren't there, they'd have sailed in months ago."

"That's true," Theodoros said. "And the result is the same. The harbour is lost."

He pulled a blank sheet from the harbour-master's desk and began writing his report to the emperor. The chain had performed exactly as designed. It had sealed the harbour mouth. It had forced the Ottomans to find another way in. They had found one -- not by breaking the defence, but by making it irrelevant. The engineering had held. The assumption beneath the engineering -- that the only way into the Golden Horn was through the Golden Horn -- had not.

## R-arc-Hi1-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense. Era: 1690s, Salem witch trials.**

**Scenario.** A prosperous Salem Village farmer petitions the court to have his neighbour's contested land claim dismissed, confident of victory because the colony's land records clearly support his title and the presiding magistrate has ruled in his favour on a preliminary motion. Before the hearing concludes, the neighbour's wife accuses the farmer's daughter of witchcraft, and the court suspends all civil proceedings involving the accused family pending the outcome of the examination. The land case is never heard. The farmer's title remains in dispute. His daughter is jailed for seven months.

> A = farmer (Thomas Garfield); G = court dismissal of the neighbour's land claim, confirming his title; X = claim dismissed, title confirmed (win); not-X = proceedings suspended, title unresolved, daughter jailed (loss).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The ordinary of the tavern in Salem Village, a March morning in 1692. Thomas Garfield sits across from his attorney, a man named Phipps, who practices before the county court in Salem Town.

Phipps lays out the case. Garfield's neighbour, a man named Hooper, filed a claim the previous autumn asserting that twelve acres of Garfield's north parcel belong to the Hooper family by right of a 1671 grant from the General Court. Garfield holds the deed to the parcel, dated 1675, which supersedes the 1671 grant because the General Court issued a corrected survey that year and re-assigned the boundary.

"The records are clear," Phipps says. "The 1675 survey is in the county register. The corrected boundary is drawn on the official plat. Hooper's claim relies on a grant that was amended four years after it was issued. I've filed a motion to dismiss, and Magistrate Hathorne granted the preliminary hearing for next month."

"Hathorne is fair?" Garfield asks.

"Hathorne ruled in your favour on the preliminary motion. He reviewed the 1675 survey and found it dispositive. He told the clerk the case is straightforward. If no new evidence is introduced, I expect him to dismiss Hooper's claim at the hearing and enter judgment confirming your title."

Garfield nods. The twelve acres are the most productive portion of his farm -- cleared land, good drainage, the only section that reliably produces a surplus crop. Losing them would reduce his farm to subsistence. He does not intend to lose them.

"Next month," Garfield says.

"Next month," Phipps confirms. "Bring the deed. I'll handle the rest."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The county courthouse in Salem Town, the morning of the hearing's first session, three weeks later. Phipps presents the 1675 survey to Magistrate Hathorne, along with the original deed and the General Court's amendment record. Hooper's attorney, a younger man from Boston, presents the 1671 grant.

Hathorne reviews both documents. He asks Hooper's attorney whether he disputes the authenticity of the 1675 amendment. The attorney says he does not -- the document is genuine -- but argues that the amendment was procedural, not substantive, and did not extinguish the Hooper family's original right.

Hathorne is unmoved. He asks the clerk to read the amendment's operative language, which explicitly re-draws the boundary and assigns the contested acreage to the parcel that Garfield now holds. He tells both attorneys that the documentary record favours Garfield, and that unless Hooper's counsel can produce evidence that the 1675 amendment was issued in error or obtained by fraud, he is prepared to rule at the next session.

Hooper's attorney asks for a continuance. Hathorne grants one week.

Garfield and Phipps leave the courthouse. In the street, Phipps says: "One week. He's giving them a courtesy, nothing more. There is no evidence of fraud because there was no fraud. The next session will be the last."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Garfield's farmhouse, four days later, a Wednesday evening. His daughter Martha is in the kitchen when a constable arrives at the door with a warrant.

The warrant names Martha Garfield and charges her with the practice of witchcraft, upon the complaint of Goodwife Hooper -- the wife of the neighbour whose land claim is pending before the court. The complaint alleges that Martha afflicted Goodwife Hooper's eldest daughter with fits and spectral visitations on three occasions in the past fortnight.

Garfield reads the warrant. He knows what has been happening in Salem Village -- the examinations, the accusations, the girls who fall into fits and name their tormentors. He has attended two of the public examinations as a spectator. He has considered it a matter for other families, other quarrels, other grudges. He has not considered it a matter for his family until this moment.

He tells the constable that Martha is seventeen years old and has been working the farm since dawn. The constable says he is required to bring her before the magistrates for examination. Garfield asks which magistrate. The constable says Hathorne.

The same magistrate who is hearing his land case. The same magistrate who is prepared to rule in his favour in three days.

Garfield tells Martha to get her cloak. He sends a boy to fetch Phipps from his lodgings.

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The county courthouse, the following Monday -- the date of the final hearing on the land claim. Garfield arrives with Phipps and his deed. Hooper arrives with his attorney.

Hathorne is on the bench, but he does not call the land case. He calls the clerk and asks for the status of all civil matters involving parties who are subjects of pending witchcraft examinations. The clerk reads from a list -- seven names, Martha Garfield's among them.

Hathorne announces that, by order of the Governor's council, all civil proceedings involving families of the accused are suspended until the witchcraft examinations are resolved. The suspension applies to both parties -- plaintiff and defendant -- in any matter where a family member is under examination. The purpose, he reads, is to preserve the integrity of civil proceedings from the influence of spectral evidence and to prevent the courts from rendering property judgments while the spiritual standing of the parties is in question.

Phipps rises. "Your Honour, the Garfield land matter is ready for judgment. The documentary record is complete. The case involves a boundary dispute, not a spiritual question. There is no connection between the land claim and the witchcraft examination."

Hathorne looks at him. "The order is from the Governor's council, Mr. Phipps. It is not within my discretion to make exceptions. The Garfield matter is suspended."

Phipps asks when the suspension will be lifted. Hathorne says when the examinations are concluded. Phipps asks how long that will be. Hathorne does not answer.

Garfield sits in the gallery with the deed in his hands. The deed is genuine. The survey is genuine. The magistrate was prepared to rule in his favour. The land is his by every measure the law provides. And the case will not be heard -- not because the evidence is insufficient, not because Hooper produced a better claim, but because Hooper's wife filed a witchcraft accusation against Garfield's daughter four days before the ruling, and the Governor's council has suspended every civil matter that touches the accused.

Martha is examined three days later. She is held for trial. The trial does not come for seven months. The land case remains suspended for the duration. Hooper farms the contested twelve acres through the growing season, harvests the crop in September, and sells the surplus at market. Garfield watches from across the boundary line. His deed is in his strongbox. His daughter is in the Salem jail. The law that was about to confirm his title is the same law that has taken his daughter, and neither instrument will move until the examinations end.
