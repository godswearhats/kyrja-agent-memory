## R-arc-Hi3-01

**Scenario.** Setting: Philadelphia, 1787. A Virginia delegate to the Constitutional Convention has spent weeks marshalling support for a clause that would require a supermajority in Congress to enact any navigation act, protecting Southern states' export trade from Northern shipping monopolies. He arrives at the crucial session confident of the votes and is outmanoeuvred on the floor: the clause is stripped by amendment before the vote is taken. Era: Federal Convention, late summer.

> A = delegate Charles Pinckney of South Carolina (proxied here by a composite Virginia delegate, Edmund Farrow); G = passage of the supermajority clause for navigation acts; X = clause passes and becomes part of the draft Constitution; ¬X = clause is stripped by floor amendment and fails

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — private canvass).** The Indian Queen tavern, Philadelphia, a Saturday evening in late August. Edmund Farrow had spent four days making private calls -- to the Maryland delegates at their lodgings on Walnut Street, to the two North Carolina men after the Friday session, and to the Georgia delegates over Sunday dinner. He kept a list in his coat pocket, marked in two columns: those who had given him an unambiguous yes, and those who had given him something close enough to count.

By Sunday evening the list had eleven names, and he needed nine for a floor majority. He showed the list to his Virginia colleague, who studied it and said: "You have room to lose two and still carry it."

"I know," Farrow said. "But I don't intend to lose two." He put the list away. The session was Tuesday. He had two days.

He wrote to the Georgia lead delegate that evening confirming their understanding. He had a verbal agreement on the clause language. He had not committed anything in writing -- that was not the practice at the Convention -- but the shape of the agreement was as settled as these things got. He went to bed believing the clause would pass.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — Monday caucus).** The State House yard, Monday afternoon. The Maryland delegates had convened an informal meeting of the Southern bloc, ostensibly to review the clause language. Farrow presented the draft text. Two delegates proposed minor amendments to the phrasing -- not to the substance, only to the form -- and Farrow accepted both. The revised language was agreed.

A Connecticut delegate, passing the yard, had paused to observe. Farrow noted him. The Connecticut men had been the most consistent opponents of the supermajority requirement. But they were not at the table, and the bloc was solid.

After the meeting Farrow's colleague said: "It will go Tuesday. I'm certain of it."

Farrow said he thought so too.

**E3 (first crack — an unexpected visit).** Monday evening. A note arrived at Farrow's lodgings from Gouverneur Morris, requesting a private conversation at Morris's rooms at nine o'clock. The note gave no subject.

Farrow went. Morris was brief. He said he was not opposed to the substance of protecting Southern trade, but that the supermajority mechanism was too rigid -- if a bare majority of Congress could not pass a navigation act, the federal government would be paralyzed on commerce. He proposed a compromise: instead of a supermajority, a clause prohibiting navigation acts that discriminated against any single state's exports. Narrower protection, more durable in law.

Farrow declined. The discriminatory-act standard was too weak; it required litigation to enforce, while a supermajority requirement was self-executing. He thanked Morris and left.

He told himself Morris had no votes to move. The list was eleven.

**E4 (inversion — floor session).** The State House chamber, Tuesday afternoon. The commerce clause was under debate. Farrow's colleague presented the supermajority text as a proposed addition. A Virginia delegate seconded.

General Pinckney of South Carolina rose in support and spoke for five minutes. Three of the Maryland delegates nodded as he spoke.

Then Gouverneur Morris rose and moved an amendment -- not the substitute Farrow had rejected the night before, but a procedural amendment: that the question of navigation act regulation be referred to a five-man committee for further drafting, rather than voted on in the current form. He cited the complexity of the commerce power and the need for careful language.

Farrow had not prepared for a committee referral. A referral was not a rejection, but a referred clause died in committee unless there was political will to report it back, and political will was what Farrow had built for a floor vote, not for a committee process. He rose and objected, saying the text had been drafted with care and was ready for a vote.

The presiding officer called for a vote on Morris's amendment to refer. Farrow looked down the room. The Connecticut delegates voted yes. The Massachusetts delegates voted yes. Two of the delegates he had counted in his column said nothing -- they did not vote, which under Convention practice meant abstention, which was the same as not voting for the clause.

The referral amendment passed seven to four.

The clause went to committee. Farrow knew, watching the room clear, that it would not come back.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — evening after the session).** Farrow's lodgings, that evening. His colleague came by after supper and found him at the writing table.

"The two who abstained," his colleague said. "I spoke to them in the corridor. Morris had spoken to both of them before the session. He offered to support their land-ordinance resolution in exchange for standing aside."

"He only needed two," Farrow said.

"He only needed two." His colleague sat down. "The committee will not report the clause. Not this session. There isn't time."

Farrow set down his pen. He looked at the list still on the table: eleven names, two columns, the care of four days' canvassing. He had counted correctly. He had simply not counted the right thing.

"The South will carry the commercial consequences for decades," he said.

"Yes," his colleague said. "And we will have signed the document."

Neither of them said anything else for a while. Farrow folded the list and put it in his traveling case, then went back to the letter he was writing to his wife.

## R-arc-Hi3-02

**Scenario.** Setting: Edo, 1701. A senior master of the Painters' Guild has trained a gifted pupil for eight years and submitted his name to the Guild council for elevation to independent master status -- a promotion that would allow the pupil to open his own studio. The council rejects the pupil on the grounds that he has produced a work deemed imitative of a Western method, which the council rules violates the Guild's exclusivity oath. The master expected the nomination to pass without difficulty.

> A = guild master Tanaka Ryoichi; G = elevation of his pupil Hashimoto Shou to independent master; X = council votes to elevate Hashimoto; ¬X = council rejects the nomination

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — nomination submission, free indirect style).** Tanaka presented the nomination portfolio at the Guild's autumn council session. He had done this four times before for four different pupils, and each time the council had approved. The process was straightforward: the master submitted the portfolio, the council reviewed the pupil's three years of independent work on record, and if the work met the standard, the vote followed.

Hashimoto's portfolio was the strongest he had ever submitted. Eight years of training, careful and meticulous -- more careful than Tanaka himself had been at that age. The brushwork was technically complete. The compositional vocabulary was correct. The council would look at those pages and find nothing to object to. He had reviewed them himself the previous week with exactly that question in mind. He had found nothing.

He set the portfolio before the council's chief examiner, made the formal declaration of sponsorship, and withdrew to the waiting room. He expected to wait one session.

**E2 (inversion — council ruling).** The council chamber, the following morning. The chief examiner called Tanaka in and presented him with the council's finding. There had been a vote: four against, two in favour.

The grounds were contained in a single paragraph. In the portfolio's third set of works -- a series of compositions on paper produced eighteen months prior -- the council's technical reviewer had identified a perspective method characteristic of Dutch copperplate engravings. The lineation of shadow in the background elements followed a receding-plane convention that was not native to the Guild's approved tradition. The Guild's exclusivity oath, sworn by all masters and operative through their pupils during the training period, prohibited the adoption of foreign technical methods whether direct or by influence. The council ruled that the perspective method constituted a prohibited influence and that the pupil could not be elevated until the matter was addressed in a formal remediation process.

Tanaka had not known Hashimoto had encountered Dutch engravings. He did not ask the examiner how the council had identified the influence -- that was not a question a master asked. He thanked the examiner and left.

**E3 (first crack, retroactive — Tanaka reviews the portfolio).** Tanaka's studio, that afternoon. He had retrieved the portfolio and brought it to the long worktable. He sat with the third set of works for an hour.

The council was not wrong. He could see it now that he knew what to look for: the shadow recession in the middle distance of the third composition, and again in the fifth. He had approved these works himself eighteen months ago. He had seen the shadow handling as original, perhaps slightly irregular, and had noted it as something to refine. He had not identified the source.

He had been teaching for twenty-two years and he had not seen it. That was the part he would think about for a long time.

**E4 (inversion acknowledged — confrontation with Hashimoto).** Tanaka's studio, the following morning. He had asked Hashimoto to come early, before the other apprentices arrived.

He laid the third composition on the table and asked Hashimoto where he had seen the method.

Hashimoto was silent for a moment. Then he said that a merchant's son had shown him a book of engravings, a Dutch atlas, three years ago. He had spent an afternoon studying the plates. He had not copied them -- he had never attempted to render them -- but he had watched the way shadow was handled in the distance plane. He had not thought he was adopting a foreign method. He thought he was noticing a structural truth about how shadow behaved.

Tanaka listened to this.

"You should have told me," he said.

"I thought it was observation, not imitation."

"The council does not make that distinction."

Hashimoto asked whether the nomination could be resubmitted after the remediation process. Tanaka said it could, in twelve months, if the remediation was completed to the council's satisfaction.

"Twelve months," Hashimoto said.

"Yes."

Hashimoto said he would begin the remediation. He asked what it required. Tanaka told him: a supervised series of twenty compositions in the approved tradition, reviewed at the six-month mark, and a written declaration of method to be submitted to the chief examiner at the end. It was not a punishment, technically. It was a demonstration. Tanaka did not say this, but they both knew the distance between what it was technically and what it was in practice.

Hashimoto bowed and went to his workstation. Tanaka stood with the third composition for a while, then put it back in the portfolio.

## R-arc-Hi3-03

**Scenario.** Setting: London, 1847. A railway promoter has assembled shareholders, obtained a parliamentary committee endorsement, and has his engineer's survey certified for a new line connecting two underserved manufacturing towns. He goes before the full House of Commons for the second reading of the enabling bill, which is expected to pass, and the bill is killed by an amendment moved at the last session by a rival company's parliamentary agent.

> A = railway promoter Josias Birch; G = passage of the Middlewich & Denton Railway enabling bill; X = bill passes second reading and proceeds to committee; ¬X = bill is defeated on the floor and does not proceed

6 beats, non-linear: E5 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E6

**Note on beat order.** The depicted order opens at the moment of defeat (E5), then moves back through the promotional campaign and parliamentary preparation (E1 through E4), before closing with aftermath (E6). Chronological order: E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E5 → E6.

**E5 (inversion — second reading, depicted first).** The House of Commons, a Thursday evening in June. The second reading had been moved without objection. The committee endorsement was cited by the promoting member; two other members spoke in favour; the line's engineering survey was entered in the record. Birch was in the gallery above, watching. His parliamentary agent had told him that morning there was nothing to worry about.

Then a member for a South Midlands constituency stood and moved an amendment: that the bill be referred to a Select Committee on competing railway routes before proceeding, on the grounds that the Middlewich & Denton route overlapped materially with an already-certified route held by the Trent Valley company. The member produced a brief filed that afternoon by the Trent Valley company's solicitors, claiming prior right over the connecting corridor.

The House divided. The amendment to refer passed by nineteen votes. Referral to the Select Committee, in the late session with Parliament preparing to rise for the summer, was functionally defeat: the committee would not sit until the autumn, and the bill's subscriptions would expire before a fresh session could be called.

Birch sat in the gallery until the next item of business had been called. Then he went downstairs.

**E1 (expectation set — prospectus issued).** Eight months earlier. Birch had printed five hundred prospectuses at a cost of nine pounds and distributed them through his agents in Middlewich, Denton, and four intermediate towns. The prospectus was plain: fourteen miles of track, connecting the two towns and their six combined textile mills to the main north-west trunk line at Crewe. Share capital of sixty-two thousand pounds, to be raised in five hundred shares of one hundred pounds each. The engineering survey was attached in summary.

Subscriptions had come in steadily for six weeks. He had his sixty-two thousand pounds, plus a reserve of four thousand pledged but not called. His committee of shareholders included a Middlewich mill-owner with forty years of local standing and a retired surveyor who had worked on the Grand Junction Railway. He had submitted the provisional orders and engaged a parliamentary agent to handle the enabling bill.

"When does the committee stage begin?" his agent had asked.

"February," Birch had said. "We'll be through by May." That had been the plan, and in January it had looked attainable.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — committee endorsement).** The parliamentary committee room, March. The committee had sat on four bills that week. Birch's engineer, a methodical man named Haverstock, had presented the survey and answered questions from two members about the gradient at the Denton approach. The questions were technical and specific, which meant the committee had read the survey.

The endorsement had come three days later, in a brief letter from the committee's clerk. No objections recorded. Bill recommended for second reading.

Birch had shown the letter to his shareholder committee at their monthly meeting. The retired surveyor had said: "The hard part is done. The second reading is a formality."

**E3 (expectation reinforced — parliamentary agent's report).** Birch's office, first week of June. His agent, Thornton, had come in from London with his quarterly report. The Commons calendar showed the second reading scheduled for the last Thursday in June. Thornton had spoken to the promoting member, a Mr. Haversley, who was comfortable with the bill and had agreed to open the reading.

"Any opposition registered?" Birch asked.

"Nothing in the petitions list," Thornton said. "The Trent Valley company holds a route south of Middlewich but there's no corridor overlap in the survey. I've checked it twice."

"They've been quiet throughout."

"They have." Thornton folded his notes. "I would expect a smooth night."

**E4 (first crack — a note from a Middlewich agent).** The week before the second reading. A letter arrived from Birch's Middlewich agent, forwarded from a contact in London: word in the city was that the Trent Valley company had engaged new parliamentary solicitors and had instructed them to file objections to two bills in the current session. No names given. Birch sent the note to Thornton, who replied that the Trent Valley company had objected to other bills before and that without a registered petition there was no formal ground to block the reading.

Birch had considered delaying the second reading to investigate. He decided against it: the subscriptions had a six-month expiry tied to the parliamentary schedule, and a delay risked losing shareholders. Thornton had advised proceeding. Haversley had not raised concerns. The survey showed no overlap.

He let it go.

**E6 (aftermath — embedded document).** *From Josias Birch to the Middlewich & Denton Railway provisional committee, dated the third of July, 1847:*

*Gentlemen — I write to inform you of the outcome of the second reading on Thursday last and the steps I intend to take in response.*

*The bill was referred to the Select Committee on competing routes at the motion of a member acting on instructions from the Trent Valley company. The Trent Valley solicitors filed a brief that afternoon citing prior right over the connecting corridor between Middlewich Junction and the Denton approach. I have reviewed the brief with Mr. Thornton. The basis of the claim is a provisional order held by Trent Valley for a branch extension, granted in 1843 and never acted upon. The order has lapsed by non-use under the Railway Clauses Act, but the lapse has not been formally recorded in the parliamentary register. Trent Valley intend to argue the order remains operative.*

*The Select Committee cannot be convened before October. Our subscriptions expire in September. I am therefore consulting with counsel on two courses: first, whether the subscription period may be extended by shareholder resolution; second, whether Trent Valley's lapsed order can be formally struck from the register before the committee sits, which would remove the ground for referral.*

*I do not minimise the difficulty of our position. Mr. Thornton and I bear responsibility for not identifying the Trent Valley provisional order before the session. We believed the corridor to be clear. It was not clear in law, whatever the survey showed on the ground.*

*I intend to appear before the committee in October. I ask for your continued confidence until then.*

*Yr. obdt. servt., J. Birch*

## R-arc-Hi3-04

**Scenario.** Setting: Boston, 1851. A freedman who has lived and worked in Boston for nine years files for a writ of habeas corpus after a slave-catcher obtains a warrant under the Fugitive Slave Act for his arrest. His attorney argues before a Massachusetts judge that the Act violates the state's personal liberty laws. The judge rules against the writ. The man is remanded to federal custody.

> A = attorney Samuel Holt (representing client James Morse); G = securing James Morse's freedom through habeas corpus; X = judge issues the writ and Morse is freed; ¬X = writ is denied and Morse is remanded

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — consultation).** Holt's office on Court Street, the evening of the arrest. James Morse was sitting across the desk from him, steady and controlled, his hands flat on his knees. He was forty-one years old and had been employed as a pressman at the Banner printing house for nine years. He had a wife and two children in the West End. He had been born in Virginia, and the warrant named him as the property of a Spotsylvania County estate.

Holt had the warrant in front of him. He had read it twice. The Act was federal and it was operative -- he could not challenge the warrant's face validity -- but the Commonwealth's Personal Liberty Law of 1843 forbade state officers and state courts from participating in the rendition process, and the Personal Liberty Law was, in his view, a valid exercise of the state's police power. The federal circuit had not yet ruled directly on the conflict. If he filed for a writ of habeas corpus and the judge read the Liberty Law generously, there was a defensible argument that the warrant could not be enforced through the state court machinery.

"I want to be honest with you," Holt said. "The federal law is against us. The question is whether the judge reads the state law as limiting his jurisdiction. Some judges have. Some haven't."

Morse said: "Can you make the argument?"

"I can make it well," Holt said. "Whether it carries the day depends on who is sitting."

**E2 (expectation reinforced — law review).** Holt's office, the following morning. He spent four hours pulling the relevant cases. The Personal Liberty Law was clear on its face. Two Massachusetts judges had used it as grounds for refusing to issue rendition process in the preceding three years -- he had their decisions. One case had gone to the circuit court and had not been overturned on appeal, which was not a precedent but was a useful silence. He drafted the habeas petition with three arguments: the jurisdictional argument, the due process argument, and a third argument grounded in the right of the state to protect persons lawfully resident within it.

His partner reviewed the draft that afternoon and said: "The jurisdictional argument is your strongest. Lead with it."

Holt thought the partner was right. The judge assigned to the case, Justice Connors, had written opinions that suggested he read the state's concurrent jurisdiction broadly. Not a guarantee, but a reading of the field. Holt filed the petition.

**E3 (first crack — a clerk's note).** The courthouse, the morning of the hearing. Holt arrived at eight to review the file. The clerk at the records desk flagged him down and handed him a note: Justice Connors had recused himself that morning due to a conflict, and the case had been reassigned to Justice Aldrich.

Holt knew Aldrich. He had appeared before him three times. Aldrich was a careful judge and a federal supremacist -- he believed the Supremacy Clause settled the field where federal and state law conflicted, and he had written that view into decisions on two unrelated cases. The jurisdictional argument, which had been built partly on Connors's likely receptiveness, would need to work against Aldrich's strong prior.

Holt went into the courtroom and reviewed his arguments in the order in which he would present them. The due process argument was now the one to press hardest.

**E4 (inversion — hearing).** The courtroom, ten o'clock. The hearing was forty minutes. Holt argued all three grounds. The due process argument was the most developed -- he cited three English common-law precedents on personal liberty and the right of habeas review, and argued that the Fugitive Slave Act's summary process denied the accused any meaningful opportunity to contest the warrant's factual basis.

Aldrich listened without expression. When Holt finished, the federal commissioner's counsel argued for ten minutes: the Act was the law of the land, the warrant was facially valid, the state court had no authority to obstruct federal process, and the Personal Liberty Law was inoperative to the extent it conflicted with the Act.

Aldrich ruled from the bench. He denied the writ. He held that the Supremacy Clause required him to give effect to the federal Act, that the Personal Liberty Law was inoperative as applied in this proceeding, and that the factual determinations required by the Act were committed exclusively to the federal commissioner. He remanded Morse to federal custody.

The marshals moved immediately. Morse was on his feet. He looked at Holt once, and Holt looked back at him, and then Morse was taken out the side door.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — courthouse steps).** The steps outside the courthouse. Morse's wife had been waiting with two men from the printing house. Holt came out and told her what had happened. She stood very still for a moment and then said: "What comes next?"

"A federal appeal," Holt said. "And a petition to the governor for intervention on state-law grounds. The governor's position on the Act is publicly opposed. I don't know what he'll do, but we should ask."

"How long?"

"Days, not weeks. The commissioner is required to expedite the rendition."

The two printing-house men were already discussing what they could do. One of them mentioned a name Holt had heard before, a man who organized things in cases like this. Holt said whatever they had in mind, it had to happen through the law first, and that he would file the federal appeal that afternoon.

He walked back up the courthouse steps. The decision was bad law, in his considered opinion. He had written that opinion in his notes the night before the hearing, before the ruling. He had always known Aldrich might rule this way. Knowing it was possible and watching it happen were not the same thing, and the gap between them was still open in his chest as he went back inside.
