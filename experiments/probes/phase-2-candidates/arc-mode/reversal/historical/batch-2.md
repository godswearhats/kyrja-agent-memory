# Reversal arc × Historical — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## R-arc-Hi2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven (1780s France)**

**Scenario.** A provincial silk merchant secures a royal warrant to supply the court at Versailles, the culmination of a decade's ambition. On the day his first shipment arrives at the palace, the Controller-General of Finances cancels all new warrants as part of an austerity decree, and the merchant's goods are turned away at the gate.

> A = silk merchant (Jean-Louis Ferrand); G = establishment as a royal supplier through the warrant; X = warrant honoured, goods accepted, trade with the court begins; ¬X = warrant cancelled, goods refused, trade blocked

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The bureau of the Intendant of Commerce, Lyon, a Tuesday morning in April 1786. Ferrand sat across the desk from the Intendant's secretary, who held the sealed document between two fingers as though it might stain his gloves.

"The warrant is genuine?" Ferrand asked.

"Genuine, registered, and countersigned by the Maison du Roi." The secretary set the document on the desk. The royal seal was visible -- red wax, unbroken, the fleur-de-lis pressed deep. "You are hereby authorised to supply silks of the first quality to the wardrobe of Her Majesty the Queen, quantities and specifications to be determined by the Garde-Meuble, payments to be rendered quarterly through the royal treasury."

Ferrand reached for the warrant. His hand was steady. He had spent ten years building the connections, the samples, the introductions that had led to this. His father had been a weaver. His grandfather had been a weaver. He was the first Ferrand to sell directly to the crown.

"When do I deliver?"

"The Garde-Meuble expects the first shipment by the end of May. Forty bolts of brocade, twenty of taffeta, specifications enclosed." The secretary handed him a second sheet -- the order, itemised, with the royal crest printed at the top. "I am instructed to remind you that the standards of the court are absolute. A single bolt below specification will void the entire shipment."

"The silk will be flawless," Ferrand said. He folded the warrant inside his coat. "I have waited a long time for this."

"Then I congratulate you, Monsieur Ferrand." The secretary dipped his pen and returned to his ledger. The meeting was over.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** Ferrand's warehouse in the Croix-Rousse district of Lyon, three weeks later. The sixty bolts -- forty brocade, twenty taffeta -- lay on the inspection tables, each one wrapped in linen and tagged with the lot number. Ferrand walked the tables with his master dyer, Gaspard, checking each bolt against the specification sheet from the Garde-Meuble.

"The crimson is exact," Gaspard said, holding a brocade sample against the colour reference. "I matched it four times before setting the vat. The gold thread is Piedmontese, as specified. The weight is within tolerance."

Ferrand ran his hand along the taffeta. The weave was tight, the drape correct, the sheen consistent from selvage to selvage. He had diverted his best looms to the royal order and delayed three commercial contracts to meet the deadline.

"What did the commercial clients say?" Gaspard asked.

"They understood. A royal warrant changes everything. Once the court accepts the first shipment, every draper in Paris will want our name on their invoices. The warrant pays for itself ten times over in reputation."

He sealed the last bolt and gave the order to load the wagons. The shipment would leave for Paris in the morning -- four days on the road, arrival at Versailles by the twenty-eighth of May, three days ahead of the deadline.

Gaspard watched the loaders work. "And the payment? The treasury pays quarterly, you said."

"I've extended my credit with the wool factor and the thread supplier to cover the gap. The first quarterly payment arrives in September. Until then, I carry the cost."

"That's a great deal of credit, Jean-Louis."

"It's a great deal of silk," Ferrand said. "And it's going to the Queen."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** A coaching inn on the road to Paris, the second night of the journey. Ferrand was eating supper in the common room when a linen merchant from Rouen sat down at his table. The man was travelling south and had news from the capital.

"You're carrying goods to Versailles?" the merchant said, eyeing the loaded wagons visible through the window.

"Silk. A royal warrant."

The merchant lowered his voice. "Then you should know that Calonne has been dismissed."

"The Controller-General?"

"Replaced by Loménie de Brienne, as of ten days ago. The Assembly of Notables demanded reforms, and the King gave them Calonne's head. Brienne is conducting a review of all royal expenditure. Every bureau, every contract, every warrant issued in the last twelve months."

Ferrand set down his spoon. "A review is not a cancellation."

"No. But Brienne's mandate is to reduce the deficit. The court's household expenses are the first target. I spoke to a draper in Paris who had his lace warrant suspended pending review. He was told to hold all deliveries until the review concluded."

"When does it conclude?"

"No one knows. Brienne has been in office ten days and has already frozen disbursements from the royal treasury for new suppliers. Existing contracts are honoured. New warrants --" He shrugged. "New warrants are new warrants."

Ferrand looked at the wagons through the window. Four days of road behind him. Two ahead. Sixty bolts of the finest silk his looms had ever produced, bought on credit, destined for a palace whose treasury had just changed hands.

"I'll deliver on schedule," he said. "The warrant is registered and countersigned. A review doesn't void a registered document."

The merchant said nothing. He finished his wine and wished Ferrand good luck.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The service entrance of the Palace of Versailles, the morning of May 28, 1786. Ferrand's wagons stood in the delivery court. A clerk from the Garde-Meuble met him at the gate with a ledger and a sealed letter.

The clerk checked Ferrand's name against his ledger. He found the entry, read it, and looked up.

"Monsieur Ferrand. Your warrant has been suspended."

"Suspended is not cancelled."

"The letter is from the bureau of the Controller-General." The clerk broke the seal and read it aloud. The decree, issued three days earlier by Brienne's office, suspended all royal warrants granted in the preceding six months to new suppliers, pending a comprehensive review of household expenditure. Payments against suspended warrants were frozen. Deliveries were to be refused until the review was complete and the warrant either reinstated or annulled.

"My goods are on those wagons," Ferrand said. "Sixty bolts, made to the Queen's specification, delivered ahead of schedule. The warrant was registered and countersigned by the Maison du Roi."

"I understand, monsieur. The decree does not distinguish between delivered and undelivered goods. Suspended warrants are suspended in their entirety. I cannot accept your shipment."

Ferrand stood in the delivery court with the sealed letter in his hand and four wagons of silk behind him. The palace rose above the courtyard wall, white stone and glass. The morning sun caught the windows of the upper floors. Inside, the court was conducting its business. Outside, his goods sat on wagons that would turn around and make the four-day journey back to Lyon carrying every bolt they had brought.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Ferrand's warehouse in the Croix-Rousse, eight days later. The wagons had returned. The sixty bolts sat on the same inspection tables where Gaspard had verified them three weeks earlier.

Ferrand stood at the end of the table with two letters in front of him. The first was from his wool factor, requesting payment on the credit extended for the royal order -- due in fourteen days, non-negotiable. The second was from the Garde-Meuble, received that morning by post. It was brief: the review of suspended warrants would take a minimum of four months. No interim payments would be issued. Suppliers were advised to seek commercial markets for goods already produced.

Gaspard came in from the dye works. He looked at the bolts on the tables and at Ferrand's face.

"Seek commercial markets," Ferrand said, holding up the second letter. "Sixty bolts of silk made to the Queen's personal specification -- her colours, her thread, her weave. No draper in Lyon or Paris will buy crimson brocade with Piedmontese gold thread. It was made for one customer, and that customer's door is shut."

"And the credit?"

"Due in two weeks. I can sell the taffeta at a loss -- the weave is standard enough. The brocade is worthless outside the court. I'll have to break the bolts and re-dye the thread for commercial colours. That's three weeks of work and half the value gone."

Gaspard was quiet. He picked up a corner of the crimson brocade and let it fall. It was beautiful cloth -- the finest work the looms of Croix-Rousse had produced that year, and it would be unravelled and re-dyed to pay a debt incurred in the making of it.

Ferrand folded both letters and placed them in his ledger. He closed the ledger and set it on the shelf beside the royal warrant, which was still sealed in its linen envelope. He did not open the envelope. The warrant was a registered document, countersigned by the Maison du Roi, and it was worth nothing.

## R-arc-Hi2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style (1860s American Civil War)**

**Scenario.** A Union colonel leads his regiment on a forced march to reinforce a strategic crossroads, certain from dispatches that the Confederate force there is a small rearguard. When he arrives, the crossroads is held by a full Confederate brigade with artillery, and his regiment is routed within the hour.

> A = Colonel Hiram Weld; G = seizure of the crossroads at Burwell's Ford; X = crossroads held by a small rearguard, easily taken; ¬X = crossroads held by a full brigade with artillery, regiment routed

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The headquarters tent of the Second Division, a farm outside Culpeper, Virginia, the evening of September 11, 1863. Colonel Weld stood before the divisional commander's map table with his orders in hand and the dispatch that had prompted them spread beside the map.

The dispatch was from the cavalry screen, dated that morning: the Confederate force at Burwell's Ford consisted of a single regiment of infantry, perhaps four hundred men, with no artillery observed. The regiment appeared to be a rearguard covering the withdrawal of the main Confederate body southward toward the Rapidan.

The divisional commander tapped the crossroads on the map. "Take your regiment -- the 14th Massachusetts. March through the night. Be at Burwell's Ford by dawn. If the rearguard is still there, push through it. If they've withdrawn, hold the crossroads and wait for the division to come up."

Weld studied the map. The march was twelve miles over good road. His regiment was fresh -- they had been in reserve for four days. Four hundred Confederate infantry without guns was a force he could handle with seven hundred men and the advantage of a dawn approach.

"The cavalry is certain of the numbers?" Weld asked.

"They observed the position for three hours yesterday afternoon. One regiment, no guns, no entrenchments. A screening force, nothing more."

Weld folded his orders and placed them in his coat. He left the tent and walked to the regimental bivouac to give the order to march.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The road south of Culpeper, two a.m. The 14th Massachusetts moved in column, seven hundred men, muskets at the shoulder, the road dry and firm under their boots. Weld rode at the head of the column with his adjutant, Lieutenant Carr.

They had been marching for four hours. The night was clear. No picket fire was visible ahead -- no sign that the Confederate rearguard had posted sentries this far from the crossroads. Weld took this as confirmation: a small force, expecting no pursuit, guarding a ford that the main body had already crossed.

A mounted courier overtook the column at a trot. He carried a message from the cavalry screen, updated as of midnight: no change at Burwell's Ford. The rearguard remained in position. No reinforcements observed.

Carr read the message by the light of a shuttered lantern. "Still four hundred men?"

"Still four hundred men," Weld said. "We'll be in position by five. Form line of battle at the tree line south of the ford and advance at first light. With seven hundred against four hundred and no guns, we take the position in the first assault."

The column marched on. The men were quiet. They had not fought in six weeks, and the prospect of an easy action after a long rest had put the regiment in good spirits.

**E3 (inversion scene).** The tree line south of Burwell's Ford, five-fifteen a.m. Weld halted the column at the edge of the woods and rode forward with Carr and two scouts to observe the crossroads.

The crossroads was eight hundred yards ahead, across open ground that sloped gently down to the ford. The light was grey but growing. Weld raised his field glasses.

The position at the ford was not a rearguard. The open ground between the tree line and the crossroads was covered with earthworks -- fresh dirt, thrown up within the last day, running in a crescent from the ford to the high ground on the eastern flank. Behind the earthworks, Weld could see massed infantry -- not one regiment but at least three, probably four, with colours visible at two points along the line. On the high ground to the east, partially concealed by brush, sat a battery of field artillery -- four guns, possibly six, their barrels angled to cover the approach from exactly the position where Weld was standing.

He lowered the glasses. His hands were not steady. He counted the colours again. Two regimental standards and a brigade flag. A full brigade -- twenty-five hundred men minimum, with artillery support, behind prepared earthworks, covering an open approach that his seven hundred men would have to cross on foot.

"That is not a rearguard," Carr said. He had his own glasses up and his voice was flat.

Weld looked behind him at his regiment, formed in column in the tree line, waiting for the order to advance. Seven hundred men who had marched twelve miles through the night on the strength of a cavalry dispatch that was now twelve hours old and entirely wrong.

He had perhaps ten minutes to decide. The Confederate pickets would spot his column in the tree line as soon as the light improved. If he withdrew now, he could retreat up the road before the artillery found his range. If he advanced, he would be leading seven hundred men across eight hundred yards of open ground into the muzzles of four guns and twenty-five hundred rifles behind earthworks.

He did not withdraw in time. The Confederate pickets fired a signal volley at five-twenty, and the battery on the high ground opened thirty seconds later. The first rounds struck the tree line, and the regiment broke before Weld could give any order at all.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The farm north of Culpeper, that evening. Weld sat at the table in the headquarters tent across from the divisional commander. He had ridden back with the remnants of the column -- four hundred and sixty men out of seven hundred. The rest were dead, wounded, or missing on the road south of Burwell's Ford.

The divisional commander had the cavalry dispatch on the table between them. He also had a second dispatch, received at headquarters at three that morning -- two hours after Weld's column had left -- from a different cavalry squadron. This dispatch reported that a full Confederate brigade had moved into the Burwell's Ford position during the night of the tenth, with artillery, and had begun constructing field works at dawn on the eleventh. The information had been sent to headquarters by courier. The courier had arrived after Weld's departure. No rider had been sent to overtake the column.

"You marched on twelve-hour-old intelligence," the divisional commander said.

"I marched on the intelligence I was given."

"The updated dispatch arrived two hours after you left. I did not send a rider because I did not see it until this morning. The staff officer on night duty filed it without flagging it."

Weld looked at the dispatch -- the one that would have told him to halt, turn around, and bring his seven hundred men back to the farm alive. It lay on the table beside the dispatch that had sent him south, the one that described four hundred men and no guns.

"Two hundred and forty casualties," Weld said. "Because a staff officer filed a dispatch instead of reading it."

The divisional commander did not answer. He picked up both dispatches, folded them together, and placed them in the case file. Weld stood, saluted, and left the tent. Outside, the surviving men of the 14th Massachusetts were making camp in the same field they had left fourteen hours earlier. Some of them looked up as he passed. Most did not.

## R-arc-Hi2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 → E4 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6, embedded-document voice (1530s England)**

**Scenario.** A Benedictine abbot petitions Thomas Cromwell for the survival of his monastery during the Dissolution, presenting evidence of the house's charitable works and financial soundness. Cromwell's commissioners dissolve the house anyway and seize its assets, converting the monastery into a private estate.

> A = Abbot Richard Whiting of Thornecroft (fictional house); G = survival of the monastery through the Dissolution; X = monastery exempted from dissolution, community preserved; ¬X = monastery dissolved, assets seized, community dispersed

**Note on beat order.** Chronological order: E1 (the petition), E2 (Cromwell's assurance), E3 (the commissioners' visit), E4 (the dissolution), E5 (the seizure), E6 (the dispersal). Depicted order: E1 → E4 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6 -- the dissolution is shown immediately after the petition, then the sequence backtracks to show the false assurance and the investigation that preceded it.

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The petition itself survives in the State Papers. It was written in a clear secretarial hand and signed by the abbot over the seal of the house:

> *To the right honourable Thomas Cromwell, Lord Privy Seal, from the Abbot and brethren of the Benedictine house of Thornecroft in the county of Somerset, with all duty and obedience --*
>
> *We humbly petition that the house of Thornecroft be considered for preservation under the Act for the Dissolution of the Lesser Monasteries. Though our revenues fall below the threshold of two hundred pounds annual, we submit that the house performs services to the commonweal that the Act was not intended to destroy: we maintain a hospital of twelve beds for the sick poor of the parish; we keep a grammar school of thirty pupils at no charge to their families; we distribute alms to forty households each Michaelmas and Easter; and we employ nineteen lay servants whose livelihood depends entirely upon the house.*
>
> *We enclose the audited accounts for the last three years, certified by the bishop's registrar, showing that the house is solvent and free of debt. We submit further that the house has received no adverse report from any visitation in the past decade, and that the moral conduct of the brethren has been examined and found without reproach.*
>
> *We ask only that the Lord Privy Seal consider whether the destruction of this house serves the King's purpose, or whether its preservation might better honour the King's name in this county.*

The petition was received at Cromwell's office on the fourteenth of March, 1536. A clerk endorsed it with the date and the notation: *Thornecroft. Somerset. Under threshold. Claims charitable use. Referred to the Lord Privy Seal.*

**E4 (inversion scene -- depicted out of chronological order).** The document that ended the matter was dated the twenty-third of September, 1536. It was a writ of dissolution, signed by Cromwell and sealed with the Privy Seal, addressed to the commissioners of the Court of Augmentations:

> *To the commissioners appointed for the suppression of religious houses in the county of Somerset -- You are hereby directed to proceed to the Benedictine house of Thornecroft and to execute the dissolution of the said house according to the provisions of the Act. All properties, revenues, goods, plate, and movable assets of the house are to be surrendered to the Crown and inventoried for the Court of Augmentations. The brethren are to be offered pensions at the standard rate or transferred to houses not yet suppressed, at their election. The abbot is to surrender the seal of the house and vacate the premises within fourteen days of this writ's service.*

The writ made no reference to the petition, the charitable works, the audited accounts, or the bishop's certificate. It did not acknowledge that the abbot had written at all.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene -- chronological backtrack).** Between the petition and the writ lay a letter. It was written in Cromwell's own hand -- or at least over his signature, which in this period often meant the same thing -- and dated the fifth of April, three weeks after the petition's receipt:

> *To the Abbot of Thornecroft -- I have received your petition and reviewed the accounts enclosed. The house's charitable works are noted and commended. I have instructed my commissioners to take these matters into consideration when they visit your county later this year. Houses that demonstrate sound management and genuine service to the commonweal have been preserved where the evidence supports preservation. I encourage you to prepare for the commissioners' visit with full records and a candid account of the house's affairs.*

The abbot received this letter and read it to the brethren in chapter. The record of the chapter meeting, preserved in the house's own cartulary, noted that the community received the Lord Privy Seal's letter with great relief and that the abbot ordered the preparation of a comprehensive dossier of the house's charitable activities, financial records, and testimonials from the parish.

**E3 (first-crack scene -- chronological backtrack).** The commissioners arrived at Thornecroft on the twelfth of August. There were two of them -- Dr. John Tregonwell and a clerk. They spent three days at the house. The abbot presented the dossier. The commissioners examined the accounts, inspected the hospital and school, interviewed the brethren individually, and took depositions from four parish witnesses regarding the alms distribution.

The commissioners' report, filed with the Court of Augmentations on the twentieth of August, survives in summary:

> *Thornecroft. Revenues: £168 per annum. Brethren: eleven, including the abbot. Moral condition: no faults found. Financial condition: solvent, accounts in good order. Charitable activity: a hospital, a school, and quarterly alms as described in the abbot's petition. The house is well managed and the abbot is competent.*

Beneath the summary, in a different hand -- possibly Cromwell's, possibly a senior clerk's -- was a marginal note:

> *The site is valued at £400 if sold. The lead from the roof is worth £60. Sir Edward Wadham has expressed interest in the property for conversion to a private residence. Proceed with dissolution.*

The abbot did not see this note. He saw only the commissioners' visit -- thorough, respectful, and conducted exactly as Cromwell's letter had promised. He believed the visit had gone well.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Thornecroft Abbey, the thirtieth of September, 1536. The commissioners returned with the writ. The abbot received them in the chapter house. Dr. Tregonwell read the writ aloud. The abbot listened without interrupting.

When the reading was finished, the abbot asked on what grounds the house had been dissolved, given the commissioners' own findings.

Tregonwell said the Act did not require grounds beyond the revenue threshold. The house's income was below two hundred pounds. The Act applied. The Lord Privy Seal's letter had encouraged the abbot to prepare for the visit, not promised the house's survival. Preservation was a possibility that had been considered and declined.

The abbot produced Cromwell's letter. He read the relevant line aloud: *Houses that demonstrate sound management and genuine service to the commonweal have been preserved where the evidence supports preservation.*

"The evidence supports preservation," the abbot said. "Your own report confirms it. The accounts are sound. The charitable works are documented. The moral condition of the brethren is without reproach. On what basis was the evidence found insufficient?"

Tregonwell said the decision was not his. The writ bore the Lord Privy Seal's signature. The commissioners' role was to execute it, not to justify it.

The abbot surrendered the seal of the house. He placed it on the chapter table beside Cromwell's letter. The commissioners began the inventory that afternoon.

**E6 (aftermath scene).** The cartulary of Thornecroft -- the house's own record book, maintained by the brethren for two centuries -- contains a final entry in the abbot's hand, undated but written after the dissolution:

> *The house of Thornecroft is surrendered. The brethren are dispersed: four to Glastonbury, three to Muchelney, two to secular life, one to the hospital of St. John in Wells. The school is closed. The hospital is closed. The forty households that received alms at Michaelmas will receive nothing this year. Sir Edward Wadham takes possession of the property on the feast of All Saints. The lead from the roof has already been stripped. I am offered a pension of six pounds per annum, which I accept, having no other provision. I have served this house for thirty-one years. I petitioned for its survival on the strength of its works and its accounts and the word of the Lord Privy Seal. The works were genuine. The accounts were sound. The word was not.*

The cartulary was removed from the house during the dispersal. It passed through several private collections before entering the county archive at Taunton, where the final entry can still be read.

## R-arc-Hi2-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense (1890s France)**

**Scenario.** An artillery captain in the French Army is tried by court-martial on charges of treason, with the military establishment publicly certain of his conviction. The court acquits him, and the General Staff is forced to acknowledge the verdict before the press.

> A = the General Staff, represented by the military prosecutor; G = conviction of the accused captain on treason charges; X = conviction (guilty verdict, imprisonment); ¬X = acquittal (not guilty, captain released)

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The office of the military prosecutor, the Cherche-Midi prison, Paris, a morning in November 1894. Colonel Mercier's aide delivers the case file to the prosecutor's desk. The file contains the indictment, the forensic report, and a sealed envelope marked *Confidentiel -- pour le tribunal seulement*.

The prosecutor opens the file and reviews the indictment. The accused is Captain Henri Duroc, an artillery officer assigned to the General Staff. The charge is treason: passing classified artillery specifications to a foreign military attaché. The evidence rests on a handwritten memorandum -- a *bordereau* -- recovered from the foreign embassy's wastepaper, listing five items of classified military information. The forensic examiner has attributed the handwriting to Captain Duroc.

The prosecutor reads the forensic report. The examiner states with certainty that the handwriting on the *bordereau* matches Duroc's. He provides a detailed comparison of letter forms, spacing, and pen pressure. The conclusion is unqualified.

The sealed envelope contains supplementary intelligence material, to be shown to the judges in closed session but not to the defence. The prosecutor does not open it -- his instructions are to present it sealed to the tribunal president.

He closes the file. The case is straightforward. The handwriting match is definitive. The sealed envelope provides additional context that the General Staff considers dispositive. The military press has already reported that the evidence is overwhelming. The tribunal is scheduled for December. The prosecutor expects the proceeding to last two days.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The chamber of the military tribunal, the first morning of the trial. The five judges -- all serving officers -- sit behind the long bench. The prosecutor presents the case.

He reads the *bordereau* aloud: five items of classified information, each one corresponding to material that Captain Duroc had access to through his assignment on the General Staff. He presents the forensic report. He calls the handwriting examiner, who testifies for ninety minutes, walking the tribunal through the letter-by-letter comparison. The examiner's conclusion is absolute: the *bordereau* is in Duroc's hand.

The prosecutor then requests a closed session. The public gallery is cleared. The defence counsel protests -- he has not seen the contents of the sealed envelope and cannot challenge evidence he has not reviewed. The tribunal president overrules the objection. The sealed envelope is opened. Its contents are shown to the judges. The prosecutor summarises the intelligence material without reading it into the record. The defence counsel sits in silence, unable to respond to evidence he is not permitted to see.

The closed session lasts forty minutes. The gallery is reopened. The prosecutor rests his case. The military press in the gallery notes the confident demeanour of the prosecution team and the brevity of the proceeding. The evening papers report that the verdict is a formality.

**E3 (inversion scene).** The chamber of the military tribunal, the second morning. The defence counsel, Maître Labon, presents his case.

He calls a second handwriting expert -- a civilian, retained by the defence -- who testifies that the *bordereau* is not in Duroc's hand. The letter forms, he says, are superficially similar but structurally different. He identifies seven specific discrepancies that the prosecution's examiner did not address: inconsistencies in the descenders, the angle of the crossbar on the letter *t*, and the spacing between words, which he says corresponds to a left-handed writer disguising his hand. Duroc is right-handed.

The prosecutor objects. The tribunal president allows the testimony to continue.

Labon then calls Captain Duroc himself. Duroc stands before the tribunal and states that he did not write the *bordereau*, has never communicated with any foreign attaché, and has never possessed the classified documents listed in the memorandum. He identifies two of the five items as materials he had no access to -- they originated in a section of the General Staff where he had never served.

Labon presents personnel records confirming that Duroc was never assigned to the section that produced those two items. The prosecutor does not contest the records.

The tribunal retires to deliberate. They are gone for four hours. When they return, the president reads the verdict: not guilty, by a vote of three to two. Captain Duroc is acquitted of all charges and released from custody.

The prosecutor sits motionless at his table. The military press in the gallery does not move for several seconds. The verdict is the opposite of every prediction published that week.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The courtyard of the Cherche-Midi prison, one hour after the verdict. Captain Duroc walks through the gate in civilian clothes. His wife is waiting. A small crowd of supporters has gathered. The military press is also present, along with two reporters from the civilian papers.

Inside the prison, the prosecutor packs the case file. The sealed envelope -- the intelligence material shown to the judges in closed session -- sits on his desk. Three judges voted to acquit despite seeing it. The material was not enough.

At the Ministry of War, the General Staff convenes an emergency meeting. The chief of the Deuxième Bureau presents the problem: the *bordereau* exists, the leak is real, and the man they charged has been acquitted. Someone on the General Staff passed classified material to a foreign power. If it was not Duroc, they do not know who it was.

The Minister of War drafts a statement for the press. It is three sentences long. It acknowledges the tribunal's verdict, states that the military respects the judgment of the court, and notes that the investigation into the source of the leaked documents will continue. The statement does not mention Captain Duroc by name. It does not mention the sealed envelope. It does not mention that the verdict was three to two, or that two judges believed the captain was guilty, or that the General Staff remains certain of his guilt despite the acquittal.

The statement is released to the evening papers. The papers print it alongside the headline: the captain is free. The General Staff's certainty, expressed for weeks in briefings and leaks to sympathetic journalists, is reduced to three careful sentences that concede nothing and explain less.
