# Reversal arc × Corporate — Batch 3 (Tier C)

## R-arc-Co3-01

**Scenario.** A regional sales director at a logistics firm spends three months building the case for a new territory expansion, assembling a formal investment proposal with market-size projections and signed letters of intent from two anchor clients. He presents to the executive committee fully expecting approval. The committee declines and shuts down the expansion track entirely.

> A = sales director (Marcus Reyes); G = approval of the Midwest territory expansion proposal; X = committee approves the proposal and allocates headcount; ¬X = committee rejects the proposal and closes the expansion track

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — preparation scene).** The sales floor, a Tuesday afternoon in October. Marcus had the LOI from Hartfield Distribution printed and clipped to the top of the binder. Underneath it: the Kopelman Group letter, the market-size model he'd built from six months of call data, and a two-page executive summary he'd had Diane in finance review twice. He'd been building this proposal since July. The Midwest corridor had been underserved since Decker Logistics pulled out eighteen months ago, and Marcus had the data to prove it — not directional data, not intuition, but signed commitments from clients who had already agreed to minimum-volume terms.

He went through the binder one more time, flagging pages. Hartfield alone was worth four hundred thousand annually at the minimums. He'd told his wife the night before: if this doesn't go through, I don't know what will. She'd told him to stop rehearsing and go to sleep. He closed the binder and went to sleep.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — pre-meeting signal).** The CFO's office, the morning of the presentation. Marcus had stopped by to confirm the room booking and found the CFO, Sandra Okafor, pulling her copy of the pre-read from her desk.

"I liked your numbers," she said. "The Hartfield anchor is strong. Gets you past the break-even threshold in month eleven -- that's better than the Kansas City expansion looked at this stage."

"Kopelman adds another floor," Marcus said.

"I know. I ran the downside scenario without them -- you still get there by month fourteen." She turned a page. "I'm going to let you open with the LOIs. Show the committee the paper before the model. Let them see that the demand is already committed."

He thanked her and went to set up the room. Sandra was the one executive who read pre-reads before the meeting. If she was in, that was three of the seven votes.

**E3 (first crack — ambiguous counter-signal).** The conference room, forty minutes before the presentation. Marcus was arranging the binders when the COO, Elliott Marsh, came in early and poured himself a coffee.

"You're up third today," Marsh said.

"That's what's on the agenda."

"Capital priorities item moved to first." He didn't elaborate. He took his coffee and his seat at the far end of the table and opened his phone.

Marcus didn't know what the capital priorities item was. It hadn't been on the agenda when the CFO had confirmed the room. He checked the revised agenda on his tablet: a fifteen-minute slot, no presenter listed, no materials attached. He set the binders out and told himself it was probably a routine reallocation.

**E4 (inversion — committee vote).** The executive committee room, two hours later. Marcus had presented for twenty-two minutes. The LOIs were on the table. Sandra had asked three substantive questions. Two other committee members had asked about the hiring timeline and the distribution partner model. The conversation had felt productive -- the kind of productive where people are already problem-solving implementation, not questioning the premise.

Then the CEO, Howard Lim, spoke.

He said the capital priorities item from the morning had resolved a constraint the committee had not anticipated at the time Marcus submitted his proposal. The company had agreed in principle to acquire a regional carrier in the Pacific Northwest. The terms weren't final, but the letter of intent was signed and the integration budget was allocated. It consumed the capital envelope that the Midwest expansion would have drawn from. The expansion track was closed until the Pacific Northwest integration was complete -- a timeline of eighteen to twenty-four months, depending on regulatory review.

Marcus looked at Sandra. She was writing something in her notebook and did not look up. The room was quiet for a moment.

"The proposal is strong," Howard said. "That's documented in the record. When we reopen the Midwest track, it gets first consideration."

He moved to the next agenda item.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — aftermath).** The parking garage, forty minutes later. Marcus was at his car with the binder when Diane from finance caught up to him. She'd been in the back of the room during the presentation.

"I didn't know about the acquisition," she said. "Nobody told me when I was running your numbers."

"Nobody told me either," Marcus said.

She stood there. "The Hartfield LOI has a ninety-day window. If we don't move by January, they're going to talk to Consolidated."

"I know."

"Do you want me to—"

"No." He put the binder in the back seat. "There's nothing to escalate. The capital is gone. I'm going to call Hartfield tomorrow and tell them we need an extension, and they're going to say no, and that's going to be that." He closed the car door. "Thanks for the help on the model. It was good work."

She went back toward the elevator. He sat in the car for a few minutes before starting it.

## R-arc-Co3-02

**Scenario.** A mid-sized accounting firm's managing partner has spent eighteen months shepherding a merger with a larger regional firm, negotiating terms, running due diligence, and briefing the partnership. At the signing meeting, the acquiring firm's CEO arrives with a counter-proposal that would restructure the deal as an asset acquisition rather than a merger, eliminating partner equity on terms the current firm's partners cannot accept. The merger collapses in the room.

> A = managing partner (Vivienne Carr); G = executing the merger agreement with Aldmore & Rusk; X = merger closes and Carrington partners receive equity stakes in the combined firm; ¬X = deal structure is rejected by the partnership and the merger does not close

6 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — deal announcement).** The Carrington Hartley partners' meeting, the first Thursday of February. Vivienne had the term sheet on the screen and twenty-three partners in the room, including four who'd been skeptical from the beginning.

"The structure is a merger of equals," she said. "Our partners step in at equity parity with the equivalent Aldmore tier. No clawbacks, no earn-out, no lock-up beyond the standard thirty-six months."

Robert Feld, who handled the large-account advisory practice, asked about the Carrington name. Vivienne said the combined entity would operate under both names for a two-year transition period, then the partnership would vote on the permanent branding. Robert said that was acceptable. He'd been the loudest skeptic for seven months. If Robert was satisfied, the room was satisfied.

"Questions on the structure?" Vivienne asked. There were none. "Then I'll proceed to confirm the signing date."

She sent confirmation to Aldmore's counsel that evening.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — due diligence completion).** The Carrington Hartley conference room, six weeks later. Vivienne and her two partners from the deal committee were reviewing the final due diligence summary prepared by outside counsel. Both sides' books were clean. The Aldmore client retention model was conservative and the projections held even at the low end.

"Last issue was the Meridian account conflict," said Patrick, the deal committee's finance lead. "Aldmore resolved it -- they've already moved the account to a different engagement team, conflict cleared."

"Any other open items?"

Patrick checked his list. "Nothing flagged. Outside counsel is ready to prepare the execution copies."

Vivienne looked at the summary. Eighteen months of work. She had negotiated four separate term sheet revisions, managed two near-collapses over partner equity methodology, and personally briefed every senior partner at Carrington at least twice. The deal was clean. She called Aldmore's managing partner that evening and confirmed the signing for the following Thursday.

**E3 (first crack — unusual pre-meeting request).** The day before signing. Aldmore's general counsel called Vivienne's assistant and asked whether the signing meeting could begin thirty minutes early, at nine-thirty instead of ten. He said Aldmore's CEO, Fletcher Roan, wanted time before the formal execution to walk through a "presentation of final terms."

Vivienne called Aldmore's managing partner directly.

"Fletcher wants to present something?" she asked. "We have executed term sheets. There are no open terms."

A pause. "He's been traveling. I think he wants to add some color to the structure for your partners' benefit."

"My partners have been briefed. We're ready to sign."

"I understand. He'll keep it brief. Thirty minutes."

She said she would accommodate the request. She told Patrick afterward that it was probably a formality -- a new CEO making his mark by speaking before the pens went down. Patrick agreed. They both let it go.

**E4 (inversion — signing meeting).** The conference room at Aldmore & Rusk, Thursday morning. Both partnership groups were present -- twenty-three Carrington partners on one side, eighteen Aldmore partners on the other. Fletcher Roan stood at the head of the table with a single-page document in front of him.

He thanked both groups for their work and said he wanted to propose a refinement to the transaction structure before execution. The refinement was this: rather than a merger, Aldmore would acquire Carrington's client book and key personnel as an asset transaction. The Carrington entity would be wound down over twelve months. Carrington partners would be offered employment contracts at Aldmore's standard associate-partner level, with a thirty-month path to equity eligibility.

The room went very quiet.

Vivienne looked at Fletcher Roan, then at Aldmore's managing partner, who was studying his copy of the single-page document. She looked at Robert Feld. Robert's face had gone entirely flat.

"That is not the agreed structure," Vivienne said. "We have a signed term sheet for a merger of equals. This is an acquisition. These are not equivalent."

Fletcher said the economic outcome was substantially similar for most partners. He said the employment path to equity was generous by market standards.

"Our partners are not taking employment contracts," Vivienne said. "We negotiated equity parity. That is what we agreed."

Robert Feld stood up. "We're done," he said, and walked out.

Fourteen of the twenty-three Carrington partners followed him out the door in the next ninety seconds. Vivienne stayed until the room was half empty, then stood and looked at Aldmore's managing partner, who finally met her eyes.

"I didn't know," he said.

She gathered her copy of the unsigned execution documents and left.

**E5 (inversion acknowledged — immediate aftermath).** The street outside the Aldmore building, eleven minutes later. Vivienne was standing with Patrick and two other deal committee members. Robert Feld had already called a car.

"He was going to do this from the start," Patrick said.

"I don't know that."

"He came in as CEO four months ago. This is his strategy -- acquire client books, not firms. He's done it twice before. I should have looked at his track record."

"We all should have," Vivienne said.

Robert got into his car. Before the door closed, he leaned out and said: "Call Hartwell Morrison. They've been interested for two years. Let's have that conversation this time."

Vivienne said she would call them on Monday. She stood on the street with the unsigned documents under her arm for another few minutes, then walked to the corner to get a cab.

**E6 (aftermath — consequence scene).** The Carrington Hartley office, that afternoon. Vivienne sent a firm-wide message to all partners confirming that the Aldmore transaction had not closed and would not proceed. She gave no further details. She said she would hold an all-partners meeting the following Tuesday to discuss next steps.

Twenty minutes later, Robert Feld replied to the message with a single line: *Tuesday at nine. I'll be there.*

She put her phone down, opened her desk drawer, took out the file of Hartwell Morrison correspondence she had set aside twenty months ago when the Aldmore conversation began, and started reading from the beginning.

## R-arc-Co3-03

**Scenario.** A product team at a consumer software company has built and validated a redesigned onboarding flow, passed it through design review, and received the VP's verbal go-ahead for a full-scale rollout. On the day of deployment, the automated compliance scan flags the new flow for a data-handling pattern that blocks release. The rollout is halted.

> A = product manager (Jess Nakamura); G = deploying the redesigned onboarding flow to production; X = rollout executes and new flow goes live; ¬X = rollout is blocked by compliance flag and does not go live

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (expectation set — pre-launch readiness).** The product team's standup, Monday morning. Jess had the launch checklist on the shared display and was going through it item by item.

"Feature flags?" she said.

"Set," said Dev.

"QA sign-off?"

"Signed off Friday afternoon."

"Design review?"

"Closed two weeks ago."

"VP approval?"

"Verbal go-ahead from Torres on Thursday. He said deploy when QA clears."

She marked the last item and closed the checklist. "We're deploying tomorrow morning, eight AM, to the full registered user base. Monitoring runs for four hours before we close the incident channel." She looked around the table. "Anyone have a reason we shouldn't?"

Nobody did. The standup ended in four minutes. Jess sent the deployment confirmation to the engineering lead and the DevOps coordinator before she got back to her desk.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — final deployment prep).** The engineering slack channel, Tuesday at seven forty-five AM. The deployment package was staged. The rollback script was tested. Three team members were in the channel ready to monitor the first wave. Jess was at her desk with her coffee and the monitoring dashboard open on one screen.

At seven fifty-two, Dev posted: *Package is staged. Compliance scan running -- should clear in about four minutes and then we push.*

Jess typed: *Standing by.*

The channel was quiet. Someone posted a thumbs-up emoji. Jess refreshed the dashboard. User counts were nominal. Everything was ready.

**E3 (inversion — compliance block).** The engineering channel, eight oh three AM. Dev's message came through: *Compliance scan flagged. Blocking release.*

Jess stared at the screen. She typed: *Flag code?*

Dev: *DP-114. Data persistence pattern on the third onboarding screen. The new flow stores a partial-completion state in local storage before the user has accepted the updated privacy terms. Compliance scanner catches it as a pre-consent data write.*

She pulled up the compliance rulebook. DP-114 was a Category 2 block -- no override, no same-day waiver. Any pre-consent data write that persisted beyond the session required a privacy review before release, minimum three business days. The rule had been updated in September, two weeks after the design review had closed on the onboarding flow. Nobody on the product team had caught the update.

She typed: *Can we patch it today and re-queue?*

Dev: *The fix is straightforward -- move the persistence call to post-consent. Four hours of work. But the compliance scan re-runs after any patch. Earliest we can redeploy is Friday, assuming the re-scan clears.*

Jess sat with it for a moment. Then she opened the message thread with VP Torres and typed: *Deployment blocked by compliance flag. Explaining in a follow-up.*

**E4 (inversion acknowledged — VP notification and triage).** The VP's office, nine fifteen AM. Jess had sent the written summary ahead and Torres had already read it.

"It's a four-day delay," he said.

"At minimum. Friday assumes the patch clears the re-scan on the first pass."

"What's the user impact?"

"None. The current flow stays live. This is a rollout block, not a rollback."

Torres leaned back. "How did this get through design review without catching the September update?"

"The review was scoped against the August rulebook. The compliance team updated DP-114 in September without a distribution to active design reviews. There's a process gap."

"That's a process gap we need to fix," he said. "Not today, but it needs to go to the compliance lead next week." He looked at the summary again. "Friday is fine. Make sure the patch is solid and tell me if the re-scan finds anything else."

She thanked him and left. In the corridor she sent a message to Dev: *Friday target. Patch confirmed. Re-scan needed before we touch deploy again.* Then she sat down at her desk and updated the launch checklist, marking the deployment line as deferred.

## R-arc-Co3-04

**Scenario.** A startup's founding team has spent four months in due diligence with a Series A lead investor who has verbally committed to the round, provided a term sheet, and cleared internal investment committee review. At the final LP call, a limited partner raises a competitive-conflict objection that the GP cannot override, and the lead investment is withdrawn. The round does not close on its projected timeline.

> A = CEO of the startup (Theo Brandis); G = closing the Series A round with Keswick Ventures as lead; X = Keswick invests and the round closes; ¬X = Keswick withdraws and the round does not close

5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5

**Note on beat order.** The depicted order opens at the LP call (E3, the inversion), then cuts back to the term sheet and due diligence (E1, E2) before returning to aftermath (E4, E5). Chronological order: E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E5.

**E3 (inversion — LP call).** The Zoom grid had eleven faces on it. On the left side: Keswick's managing partner, Anand, and two associates. On the right: three of Keswick's LPs, including a representative from Dalton Capital Management. Theo was in the bottom right with his co-founder Priya, muted, watching.

Anand was summarizing the investment thesis when the Dalton representative, a woman named Christine Holst, unmuted herself.

"I want to flag a conflict," she said. "We have a material position in Cartesian Systems. I don't believe our team ran this against the Cartesian portfolio before the committee vote."

Anand's expression changed. "I'll need to check our conflict register."

"The core product overlap is direct. If Keswick leads this round, we need to understand the conflict resolution path. I don't think we can waive this one at the LP level."

The call continued for another twelve minutes but the shape of it had already changed. Theo watched Anand's face. The investment committee had approved the deal three weeks ago. Nobody had flagged Cartesian.

When the call ended, Anand sent a message to Theo immediately: *Need to speak with you and Priya directly. Can you take a call in twenty minutes?*

Theo closed his laptop and looked at Priya. She was already doing the math.

**E1 (expectation set — term sheet).** Four months earlier. The term sheet had arrived on a Tuesday, twelve days after the first partner meeting with Keswick. The pre-money valuation was within five percent of what Theo had modeled. The pro-rata rights were standard. The board seat assignment was the one issue he'd pushed back on -- he'd wanted an observer seat for the second Keswick partner rather than a full board seat -- and Anand had agreed after two rounds of email.

Theo had forwarded the term sheet to his lawyer with one line: *Let's move fast.* He'd sent a separate message to Priya: *We got it.* She'd replied with a single character. He'd printed the term sheet and put it in the desk drawer he'd been keeping empty since the seed round closed.

**E2 (expectation reinforced — due diligence completion).** Six weeks of data room requests, customer calls, technical reviews. Theo had answered two hundred and twelve questions in the data room, according to the counter his lawyer had set up. He'd done five reference calls with Anand's portfolio founders. He'd sat through a three-hour product session with Keswick's technical advisor, who'd been trying to find a scalability crack in the architecture and hadn't found one.

The investment committee approval had come in at nine PM on a Thursday. Anand had called to deliver it personally. He said the committee had been unanimous and that the LP call was the last procedural step before wire instructions.

"This is essentially done," Anand had said. "The LP call is a presentation, not a vote."

Theo had sat with that sentence for a while after the call ended. Essentially done. He'd told Priya, who'd said: don't count it until the wire clears. He'd said she was right. He'd made dinner and not thought about it again until the night before the call, when he'd slept poorly.

**E4 (inversion acknowledged — Anand's call).** Twenty minutes after the LP call ended. Theo and Priya were in the call with Anand.

"The Dalton position in Cartesian is a fund-level conflict," Anand said. "We missed it in the conflict register because Cartesian is in a different portfolio tier than we mapped your space to. That's our error."

"Can you resolve it?" Theo asked.

"Not within the LP agreement. Dalton's objection triggers a mandatory recusal clause. If they don't waive, we can't lead the round. I'm going to spend the next forty-eight hours trying to get a waiver, but I want to be honest with you: Christine Holst doesn't waive competitive conflicts. I have not seen her do it in four years."

Priya said: "What does that leave us?"

"If we can't lead, I can try to find you a co-investor who can step into the lead position. That's a six-to-eight-week process. Or I can try to stay in as a non-lead participant if you find another lead, which would require re-negotiating the term sheet." He paused. "I'm sorry. I should have caught this."

The call ended. Theo and Priya sat in the conference room for a while without speaking. The desk drawer with the printed term sheet was three meters away.

**E5 (aftermath — next steps).** The startup's office, the following morning. Theo had sent notes to the two other firms that had been in the process six months ago, before Keswick had emerged as the clear lead. Both firms had passed at the time, though one -- Meridian Growth -- had said the timing wasn't right rather than that the company wasn't right.

He'd also called his seed-round lead to brief her. She'd been quiet on the call and then said: "Meridian is still interested. I had lunch with their managing partner two weeks ago. Your name came up."

Theo had written "Meridian" on his notepad and circled it. Then he'd opened the data room, confirmed it was still organized from the Keswick process, and started drafting a new outreach message. He didn't revise the valuation and he didn't change the structure. He'd been told the deal was essentially done, and it hadn't been. He intended to wait until the wire cleared before he felt anything about the next one.

Priya had made coffee by the time he finished the draft. She put a cup on his desk and read the message over his shoulder.

"Send it," she said.

He sent it.
