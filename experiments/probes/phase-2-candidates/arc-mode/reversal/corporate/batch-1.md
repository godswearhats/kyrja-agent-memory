# Reversal arc x Corporate -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## R-arc-Co1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A mid-size pharmaceutical company's CEO stakes the firm's future on an FDA approval for their lead drug candidate. After three years of clinical trials, a unanimously positive advisory committee vote, and a pre-negotiated distribution deal, the FDA issues a complete response letter -- rejection -- citing manufacturing deficiencies that the company's own quality team had flagged and buried.

> A = CEO (Renata Marsh); G = FDA approval of lead drug candidate (PRX-4110); X = approval (binary: approved/rejected); not-X = rejection (complete response letter).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The boardroom at Claren Therapeutics, a Tuesday morning in March. The CEO stood at the head of the table with the Phase III results on the screen behind her.

"Ninety-two percent efficacy across all three cohorts," she said. "Statistically significant on every primary endpoint. The safety profile is cleaner than anything else in the class."

She clicked to the next slide: the regulatory timeline. NDA filed. Review clock started. Advisory committee hearing scheduled for September. PDUFA date -- the FDA's statutory deadline for a decision -- set for November 14th.

The chief medical officer leaned forward. "The advisory committee is a formality at this point. With these numbers, we're looking at a unanimous recommendation."

The CFO asked about the distribution agreement. The CEO said it was signed -- contingent on approval, but signed. Warehouse space leased in three regions. Sales team hired and in training since January. She turned to the head of regulatory affairs. "Where are we on the manufacturing inspection?"

"Scheduled for July," he said. "The facility passed its last two inspections without observations. I don't see an issue."

The CEO closed the presentation. "We launch in Q1. That's the plan, that's the timeline, and everything is tracking."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The lobby of an FDA building in Silver Spring, Maryland, a Wednesday afternoon in September. The advisory committee had been in session since eight that morning. Renata sat in the gallery with her chief medical officer and the head of investor relations.

The committee chair called the vote at 3:47 p.m. Twelve members voted in favour of approval. Zero voted against. Zero abstained. The chair read the tally into the record and noted that the committee's recommendation would be transmitted to the review division.

In the lobby afterward, the head of investor relations was already on his phone. The stock had moved on the vote -- up eleven percent in after-hours trading before the session officially adjourned.

"Twelve-zero," the CMO said. "I've been doing this for twenty years. I've never seen twelve-zero."

Renata nodded. She called her general counsel from the car and told her to trigger the distribution agreement's approval-contingent clauses. "Start the clock," she said. "We'll have the letter in eight weeks."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Renata's office, a Thursday in October. Her assistant put through a call from the head of quality assurance -- a man named Dressler, who reported to the VP of manufacturing, not to her.

"I need fifteen minutes," Dressler said. "In person. Not on the calendar."

She gave him ten. He came in with a folder and closed the door.

"The manufacturing inspection in July," he said. "The FDA inspector noted three observations related to contamination controls in the fill-finish line. They were classified as minor. Your regulatory team responded with a corrective action plan and the inspector accepted it."

"I know," she said. "I signed off on the response."

"The corrective actions weren't completed," Dressler said. "Two of the three were implemented. The third -- the HVAC upgrade in the aseptic suite -- was deferred by the VP of manufacturing because the contractor couldn't meet the timeline without shutting down the line for six weeks. He made the call to defer and documented it internally, but the response we sent to the FDA said all three were complete."

Renata looked at the folder. She did not open it.

"Who else knows?" she said.

"Me. The VP of manufacturing. The contractor. And now you."

She told him to leave the folder and send the VP of manufacturing to her office at four o'clock. Dressler left. She sat with the folder on her desk for several minutes before opening it.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The executive conference room, November 14th -- PDUFA date. The entire leadership team was assembled by seven in the morning. The FDA's decision would arrive electronically, typically between ten a.m. and two p.m. Eastern. The investor relations team had prepared two press releases: one for approval, one -- a template, mostly -- for delay.

Nobody had prepared a release for rejection.

At 11:23 a.m., the head of regulatory affairs received the notification on his laptop. He opened the document. It was not an approval letter. It was a Complete Response Letter -- the FDA's formal instrument of rejection. He read the first paragraph, then the second. He turned his laptop so the CEO could see the screen.

The letter cited the unresolved manufacturing observation from the July inspection. It noted that the company's corrective action response had represented all three items as complete. The FDA's own follow-up review, conducted in October as part of the pre-approval inspection process, had found the HVAC upgrade in the aseptic suite unfinished. The letter stated that the manufacturing facility could not be certified for commercial production until the deficiency was resolved and re-inspected, and that no approval could be issued until certification was granted.

The room was silent. The CFO was the first to speak. "How long to resolve and re-inspect?"

The head of regulatory affairs said six to nine months, minimum.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Renata's office, forty minutes later. She had left the conference room without speaking. Her general counsel followed her and closed the door.

"The distribution agreement," the general counsel said. "The contingency clause gives them thirty days to exit if approval doesn't come by the PDUFA date. They'll exercise it."

"I know."

"The stock is down thirty-eight percent since the letter hit the wire. The board will want a call tonight."

Renata turned her chair toward the window. Three years of clinical work. A unanimous advisory committee. A twelve-zero vote. None of it mattered. The drug worked. The science was clean. The application was sound. And the answer was no -- not because of the medicine, but because a contractor couldn't finish an HVAC upgrade and her VP of manufacturing had lied about it on a federal submission.

"Get me the board chair," she said. "And get me outside counsel. Not the firm we use for regulatory -- someone who does white-collar defence. The FDA is going to want to know who signed that corrective action response, and so am I."

## R-arc-Co1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A management consulting partner leads her firm's bid for the largest public-sector contract in the firm's history. After eighteen months of proposal work, a finalist presentation the client calls "the strongest we've seen," and a verbal confirmation from the procurement lead, the contract is awarded to a competitor whose bid scored lower on every technical criterion -- because the client's newly appointed minister had a standing policy of never awarding sole-source contracts above a threshold, and the firm's bid exceeded it by twelve percent.

> A = partner (Diane Kowalski); G = winning the Department of Infrastructure modernisation contract; X = contract awarded to her firm (win); not-X = contract awarded to competitor (loss).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The situation room on the thirty-second floor, a Monday in January. Diane had converted the corner conference room into a war room six months earlier -- whiteboards covering the windows, three proposal drafts pinned to the wall, a Gantt chart tracking every deliverable between now and the submission deadline.

She had eleven people on the bid. Two were borrowed from the London office. One had deferred a sabbatical. The proposal itself was nine hundred pages -- technical approach, staffing plan, past performance, cost volume -- and Diane had reviewed every section twice. The Department of Infrastructure's modernisation programme was the largest public-sector technology contract issued in the country in a decade. Winning it would double the firm's government practice overnight.

The RFP had been specific about evaluation criteria: technical merit weighted at sixty percent, management approach at twenty-five, cost at fifteen. Diane's team had mapped every requirement to a response, cross-referenced every evaluation criterion to a proof point, and priced the engagement at the top of the competitive range -- high, but defensible, because the technical approach justified it. She believed the proposal was the strongest her firm had ever produced. She had staked her reputation on that belief, and eighteen months of her career on the work behind it.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** A government conference room in the capital, a Wednesday in June. The finalist presentation -- what the procurement office called an "oral defense." Diane brought her deputy and the proposed programme director. The evaluation panel sat across the table: five civil servants and the procurement lead, a woman named Hargreaves who had run the acquisition from the start.

Diane presented for ninety minutes. The panel's questions were sharp but fair. Her programme director handled the technical deep-dive without notes. Her deputy walked through the transition plan and the risk register. When the session ended, Hargreaves walked them to the elevator.

"Off the record," Hargreaves said, standing in the corridor after the panel had left. "That was the strongest presentation we've seen in this competition. I can't tell you anything about the decision, but I can tell you that your team should be proud of the work."

Diane thanked her. In the car back to the office, she allowed herself to believe it. Not hope -- belief. She had seen enough procurements to know the difference between a polite dismissal and a genuine compliment, and Hargreaves was not a woman who said things she didn't mean.

She called the managing partner from the car. "I think we have it," she said.

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Diane's office, a Friday afternoon three weeks later. She was reviewing the staffing plan for the transition when her deputy knocked and came in without waiting.

"Have you seen the gazette?" he said. "New minister appointed to Infrastructure this morning. Margaret Eames."

Diane knew the name. Eames had made her reputation at the Department of Health by cancelling three large sole-source contracts in her first month and re-competing them as multi-vendor frameworks. It was a political position, not a technical one -- she believed that large single-vendor contracts created dependency and that splitting work across multiple providers reduced risk and increased competition. She had said so publicly, in a speech Diane had read, and she had backed it up with policy: no sole-source contract above a defined threshold without ministerial sign-off.

"When does she start?" Diane asked.

"She's already started. Sworn in this morning."

Diane pulled up the cost volume. Their bid was sole-source. Their proposed value exceeded Eames's known threshold by twelve percent. The RFP had not prohibited sole-source bids -- it had been issued before Eames's appointment, under the previous minister's authority. But the contract award required ministerial approval, and the minister was no longer the person who had authorised the competition.

She sat with the numbers for a long time. There was nothing to change. The bid was submitted. The evaluation was complete or nearly so. The decision was in someone else's hands, and the hands had changed.

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The thirty-second floor, a Tuesday morning in August. The notification arrived by email at 9:14 a.m. -- a formal letter from the procurement office, attached as a PDF, addressed to Diane as the firm's authorised representative.

She opened it at her desk. The contract had been awarded to Beckett & Laine, the second-ranked bidder. The letter included the evaluation summary: Diane's firm had scored highest on technical merit, highest on management approach, and third on cost. Beckett & Laine had scored second on technical merit, third on management approach, and first on cost. The overall weighted scores were not disclosed, but the letter noted that the source selection authority -- a title that now reported to the new minister -- had determined that Beckett & Laine's proposal represented the best value to the government "considering all factors, including contract structure and programme risk."

Diane read the letter twice. Then she opened Beckett & Laine's public filing from the previous quarter. Their bid had been structured as a multi-vendor framework with Beckett & Laine as prime and two subcontractors sharing thirty percent of the work. The total contract value fell below Eames's threshold.

They had scored lower on every technical criterion. They had structured their bid to fit under a policy that hadn't existed when the competition was launched. And the source selection authority had used "programme risk" -- a factor not enumerated in the RFP's evaluation criteria -- to justify the award.

Diane closed the letter. She picked up her phone and called the firm's general counsel. "I need to talk to you about a bid protest," she said. "And I need the managing partner on the line when we do."

## R-arc-Co1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (expectation set, six months earlier) -> E3 (expectation reinforced) -> E4 (first crack) -> E5 (inversion) -> E6 (inversion acknowledged), embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A startup CTO is certain his company will be acquired by a major platform after a year of technical due diligence, integration testing, and a signed letter of intent. On the day the deal is scheduled to close, the acquirer's board kills it -- not over price, technology, or team, but because a regulatory filing the acquirer made in a separate matter disclosed the acquisition prematurely, and the resulting antitrust scrutiny made the deal politically untenable.

> A = CTO (Jonas Hertz); G = closing the acquisition of his startup by Meridian Systems; X = deal closes (acquired); not-X = deal killed (not acquired).

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The events' chronological order is: E2 (expectation set, six months earlier) -- E3 (expectation reinforced, three months earlier) -- E4 (first crack, two weeks earlier) -- E5 (inversion, day of) -- E6 (inversion acknowledged, same day) -- E1 (aftermath, next morning). The depicted order opens with the aftermath.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The following morning. Jonas's co-founder found the email chain on the shared legal drive at 6:40 a.m. and forwarded it to the full leadership team without comment. The subject line read: *Re: Meridian Systems -- Transaction Status Update*.

The final message in the chain, sent at 11:47 p.m. the previous night from Meridian's general counsel to Jonas's outside counsel, read:

> *Following today's board session, Meridian Systems has determined that it will not proceed to closing on the proposed transaction. This decision is final and is not subject to further negotiation. Meridian will honour its obligations under the breakup fee provision of the LOI (Section 9.2). We will be in touch regarding logistics. We wish Jonas and the team well.*

Below it, a reply from Jonas's counsel, sent at 12:03 a.m.:

> *Received. We reserve all rights under the LOI and will respond formally within the contractual window. Please preserve all documents related to the board's deliberation.*

Jonas read the chain in the kitchen of his apartment, standing at the counter. He did not forward it. He did not reply. He poured coffee, set the phone face-down, and drove to the office.

**E2 (expectation-set scene -- embedded document, six months earlier).** The conference room at Jonas's startup, a Wednesday afternoon in February. Jonas and his co-founder sat across from Meridian's VP of corporate development and their outside counsel. The document between them was the Letter of Intent.

Jonas's counsel read the key terms aloud: Meridian would acquire 100% of the company's equity for $340 million in cash and stock. The deal was subject to technical due diligence (sixty-day period), regulatory clearance (standard HSR filing), and Meridian board approval. A breakup fee of $8.5 million applied if Meridian terminated without cause after the due diligence period closed. Exclusivity was granted to Meridian for the duration -- Jonas could not solicit or entertain competing offers.

Meridian's VP leaned across the table. "We don't sign LOIs we don't intend to close," she said. "The due diligence is real, but the intent is real too. Our CEO has signed off. Our board has been briefed."

Jonas signed the LOI. His co-founder countersigned. Meridian's VP signed for the acquirer. The executed copy was scanned and distributed to both legal teams before the room cleared.

**E3 (expectation-reinforced scene -- three months earlier).** Meridian's campus, a Thursday in May. The integration lab on the second floor of Building 7. Jonas and two of his senior engineers had been on-site for a week, running compatibility tests between their system and Meridian's core platform.

The results were on the screen: full API compatibility across all twelve endpoints, latency within Meridian's production SLA on nine of twelve, and two of the remaining three resolvable with configuration changes that Jonas's team had already documented. The twelfth endpoint required a patch on Meridian's side -- their engineering lead had already filed the ticket.

Meridian's VP of engineering reviewed the results with Jonas over lunch in the campus cafeteria.

"This is the cleanest integration test I've seen on an acquisition," she said. "Usually we find three months of rework hiding in the API layer. You built this to be acquired."

"I built it to be interoperable," Jonas said. "The acquisition part is new."

She laughed. "Either way, I'm telling the CEO this afternoon that the technical diligence is clear. You'll get the formal notice from corp dev by end of week."

The formal notice arrived on Friday: due diligence complete, no material findings, deal proceeding to regulatory filing and board approval. The breakup fee was now active.

**E4 (first-crack scene -- two weeks earlier).** Jonas's office, a Monday morning in late July. His outside counsel called at nine.

"Have you seen Meridian's 10-Q?" she said. "Filed Friday after market close."

Jonas had not. He pulled it up while she talked.

"Page forty-three," she said. "Footnote twelve. They disclosed the acquisition as a pending transaction. Named the company. Named the price range. It's in the risk factors section -- they're required to disclose material pending transactions, so the disclosure itself isn't unusual. But they filed it before the HSR review closed. The FTC now has the transaction on record in a public filing while the antitrust review is still open."

Jonas found footnote twelve. His company's name, the approximate deal value, and the phrase *subject to regulatory approval and customary closing conditions*.

"Is that a problem?" he said.

"Probably not," his counsel said. "But the FTC has been sensitive about platform acquisitions this year. If anyone in the press or on the Hill picks up the footnote and asks questions, it gives the FTC a reason to extend the review or request additional information. It's not a deal-breaker. It's a complication."

Jonas thanked her and hung up. He forwarded the 10-Q to his co-founder with a note: *Page 43, footnote 12. Probably nothing. But watch the news cycle.*

**E5 (inversion scene).** Meridian's boardroom, a Wednesday morning in mid-August -- the day the deal was scheduled to close. Jonas was not present; the session was a Meridian board meeting, and the closing documents were to be executed immediately after. Jonas was in his own office, eighty miles away, with the signature pages queued on his screen and his counsel on standby.

At 10:15 a.m., his counsel called. The tone was different.

"The board is in executive session," she said. "They pushed the closing to this afternoon. Corp dev says it's procedural."

At 1:40 p.m., she called again. "They're still in session. I've asked for an update and haven't received one."

At 4:22 p.m., Meridian's general counsel called Jonas's counsel directly. The board had voted to terminate the transaction. The reason, as communicated, was that the premature disclosure in the 10-Q had drawn a second request from the FTC -- additional documentation on the competitive effects of the acquisition. Meridian's outside antitrust counsel had advised that the request was manageable but would extend the review by three to four months. During that period, two members of the Senate Commerce Committee had written a public letter to the FTC chair citing the footnote and asking whether the acquisition raised competition concerns in Meridian's core market. The board had concluded that the regulatory and political risk of proceeding outweighed the strategic value of the deal.

Jonas's counsel relayed the conversation to Jonas at 4:51 p.m.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Jonas's office, 5:30 p.m. the same day. His co-founder was in the chair across from his desk. The door was closed. The signature pages were still on his screen -- unsigned, now irrelevant.

"The breakup fee," his co-founder said.

"Eight and a half million," Jonas said. "Section 9.2. They said they'd honour it."

"Eight and a half million on a three-forty deal."

"Yes."

His co-founder looked at the ceiling. "We turned down Kessler's term sheet to sign the exclusivity. We turned down the Series C extension. We've been running the company for six months as if we were ninety days from not existing as an independent entity."

Jonas closed the signature-page window on his screen. The deal had not failed because of the technology. It had not failed because of the price, or the team, or the product. It had failed because a footnote on page forty-three of a quarterly filing had arrived at the wrong moment in a political news cycle, and a board that had approved the deal in principle had decided that the cost of defending it in public was higher than the cost of walking away.

"Call Kessler," Jonas said. "Find out if the term sheet is still open. And call the team in for nine a.m. tomorrow. We need to tell them before the press does."

## R-arc-Co1-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A private equity firm's operating partner takes over a struggling regional hospital chain with a mandate to restructure it for sale within eighteen months. She hits every operational target -- EBITDA doubled, patient volumes up, two facilities renovated -- and enters the sale process expecting competitive bids from three strategic buyers. All three withdraw on the same day after a CMS audit downgrades the chain's Medicare reimbursement classification, cutting projected revenue by twenty-two percent.

> A = operating partner (Sandra Yoon); G = sale of Crestline Health to a strategic buyer at target valuation; X = sale closes at or above target (sold); not-X = no buyer, sale fails (unsold).

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The PE firm's office in midtown, a Monday morning. Sandra Yoon sits across from the managing director with a single-page summary on the table between them.

The managing director taps the page. "Crestline Health. Three hospitals, two urgent-care centres, eleven hundred employees. We bought it fourteen months ago out of a distressed process. It was losing four million a year when we closed."

Sandra picks up the page. She already knows the numbers -- she wrote the turnaround plan that convinced the firm's investment committee to approve the acquisition.

"Eighteen months to sale-ready," the managing director says. "That's the timeline. EBITDA needs to be positive by month six and growing by month twelve. We renovate the two facilities that are dragging census. We stabilise the physician roster. We get patient volumes back to 2022 levels. Then we run a process and sell it to a strategic buyer -- a larger system that wants the geography."

Sandra sets the page down. "What's the target valuation?"

"Two-eighty to three-twenty, depending on the multiple. That's a three-to-four-x return on our basis."

"I'll need full operational authority," Sandra says. "Hiring, firing, capital allocation, vendor contracts. No committee approvals for anything under two million."

The managing director nods. "It's your hospital chain until it's someone else's. That's the deal."

Sandra stands. "I'll be on-site Wednesday."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The boardroom at Crestline's flagship hospital, a Friday afternoon, thirteen months later. Sandra chairs the quarterly operating review. Around the table: the three hospital administrators, the CFO she hired in month two, the chief nursing officer she recruited in month four, and the VP of physician services.

The CFO presents the dashboard. EBITDA is positive and has been for seven consecutive months. The trailing-twelve-month figure is $11.2 million -- up from negative $4 million at acquisition. Patient volumes are at 94% of 2022 levels. Two facilities have completed renovation; census at both is up thirty percent since construction ended. The physician roster is net positive for the first time in three years -- eight new physicians recruited, two departed, a net gain of six.

Sandra reviews each metric in silence, then looks at the table. "We're ahead of plan on every line. The managing director has approved the launch of a formal sale process. Goldman will run the book. The confidential information memorandum goes out to three pre-qualified strategic buyers next week."

The CFO asks about the timeline. Sandra says the process will run sixty to ninety days. Letters of intent by mid-quarter. Closing by year-end.

"What's the expected valuation?" the chief nursing officer asks.

"North of two-eighty," Sandra says. "If the trailing numbers hold."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** Sandra's office at Crestline headquarters, a Tuesday morning three weeks into the sale process. Her phone rings. It is the CFO.

"CMS sent a notification," the CFO says. "They're conducting a routine reimbursement classification audit on all three facilities. They do these periodically -- every hospital in the region gets one eventually. The auditors will be on-site next month."

Sandra asks whether there is anything in the current classification that might not hold up under review. The CFO pauses.

"Our two urgent-care centres were reclassified as outpatient departments of the flagship hospital two years ago -- before our acquisition. That classification gives them a higher Medicare reimbursement rate than they'd get as freestanding urgent-care facilities. The reclassification was approved by CMS at the time. But the criteria have tightened since then, and I'm not certain the current operations still meet the updated threshold."

Sandra asks what the revenue impact would be if the classification were downgraded.

"I'd have to model it," the CFO says. "But it's significant. Those two centres generate about thirty percent of our Medicare volume at the hospital outpatient rate. If they revert to the freestanding rate, the reimbursement drops by roughly forty percent on that volume."

Sandra tells him to build the model and have it on her desk by Thursday. She does not mention the audit to the three prospective buyers. The audit is routine. It may result in nothing.

**E4 (inversion scene).** Sandra's office, a Wednesday morning, six weeks later. The CMS audit results arrive by certified letter. She opens the envelope at her desk.

The audit has downgraded both urgent-care centres from hospital outpatient departments to freestanding facilities. The effective date is retroactive to the beginning of the current fiscal year. The letter includes a recoupment demand for overpayments received since that date and a revised reimbursement schedule going forward. Sandra reads the projected annual revenue impact: a reduction of $14.6 million, approximately twenty-two percent of total Medicare reimbursement across the chain.

She pulls up the CFO's model from six weeks earlier. His estimate had been close: $13.8 million. The actual number is worse.

Her phone begins to ring. The first call is from Goldman's lead banker. He has received calls from all three prospective buyers within the past hour. Each has been notified of the CMS action through its own diligence channels -- CMS audit results are reported in a federal database that the buyers' regulatory teams monitor. The banker's voice is measured but the message is direct: all three buyers have requested a pause in the process pending reassessment of the chain's revenue profile.

Sandra asks him whether a pause means a pause or whether it means withdrawal. He says he will know by end of day. She tells him to call her when he does.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The PE firm's office in midtown, the following Monday. Sandra sits across from the managing director. The Goldman banker is on speakerphone.

The banker delivers the update. All three buyers have formally withdrawn from the process. Each cited the same reason: the CMS reclassification reduces the chain's projected EBITDA by approximately forty percent on a go-forward basis, which puts the valuation below the range at which a strategic acquisition makes financial sense. None of the three is willing to re-engage at a revised price. The market for regional hospital chains in this geography, the banker says, does not support a fourth buyer at this time.

The managing director looks at Sandra. "We hit every target," he says. "Every single one."

"We did," Sandra says.

"And it doesn't matter."

Sandra sets the CMS letter on the table between them. Eighteen months of operational work -- the EBITDA recovery, the renovations, the physician recruitment, the patient volumes -- all of it built on a reimbursement classification that had been approved years before the firm bought the chain and revoked by a routine audit that had nothing to do with the quality of the turnaround.

"The breakup fees from the buyers cover our advisory costs," the banker says. "But they don't cover the hold. You're looking at carrying the chain for another twelve to eighteen months minimum, assuming you can find a buyer at the revised economics."

The managing director asks Sandra what the options are. She says there are two: hold and operate at the lower reimbursement rate while looking for a buyer willing to pay a reduced price, or appeal the CMS reclassification through the administrative process, which will take eight to fourteen months with no guarantee of reinstatement.

"Pick one," the managing director says.

"Both," Sandra says. "We appeal and we market simultaneously. But the number isn't two-eighty any more. It's one-sixty, maybe one-seventy. And the return on this deal just went from three-x to break-even."

The managing director closes the folder in front of him. The banker hangs up. Sandra remains in her chair. She built the machine exactly as specified, and the machine worked. The ground it stood on was the part nobody checked.
