# Reversal arc × Corporate — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## R-arc-Co2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A regional bank's compliance director spends eight months building the case for a new enterprise risk platform, securing internal champions and board-level sponsorship. On the day of the final approval vote, the audit committee kills the project and redirects the budget to an acquisition she had dismissed as a distraction.

> A = compliance director (Elena Marsh); G = board approval of enterprise risk platform; X = approval (budget allocated, project greenlit); ¬X = rejection (budget redirected to acquisition)

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The compliance director's office, a Tuesday in March. Elena closed the door and spread the vendor shortlist across the conference table for her deputy, Raj.

"We're down to two," she said. "Paladin or Kestrel. Either one replaces the spreadsheet nightmare in ops risk and gives us real-time exposure tracking. The board presentation is in eight weeks."

Raj picked up the Paladin summary. "What's the budget ask?"

"Fourteen million over three years. I've already walked it through the CFO's office. He said if the audit committee signs off on the risk-reduction numbers, he'll carry it to the full board himself."

"And the audit committee?"

"Three of the five members have sat through my quarterly briefings. They've seen the gap. They know what a manual process costs us when the regulators come in." She tapped the project timeline pinned to her whiteboard -- milestones running from vendor selection through implementation, ending in Q1 of the following year. "This is the year we get it done."

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The CFO's corner office, six weeks later. Elena had brought the final business case: a forty-page document with the risk-reduction model, the three-year cost projection, and letters of support from two business-line heads.

The CFO read the executive summary while she waited. He turned to the cost page, then to the regulatory-exposure analysis.

"You've quantified the manual-process risk at how much?"

"Eleven million in potential fines over the next audit cycle, based on the last two examination letters. That's not a projection -- it's the examiners' own language mapped to our current state."

He set the document down. "I'll present it at the June board meeting as a CFO-recommended item. That carries weight with the audit committee. They almost never vote against a CFO recommendation on risk spend."

"Almost never?"

"I've been here nine years. It's happened once, and that was a personality conflict, not a budget issue." He initialled the cover page and handed it back. "You're in good shape."

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The executive dining room, a Thursday lunch three weeks before the board meeting. Elena was eating with the head of corporate development, Sandra Gill, who had been quiet for most of the meal.

"I heard your risk platform is on the June agenda," Sandra said.

"CFO-recommended. We're ready."

Sandra set her fork down. "I should tell you -- I'm also on the June agenda. We've been working an acquisition. Midland Trust. It's a bolt-on for the commercial lending book, and it's time-sensitive. The seller wants a signed LOI by the end of July."

"What's the budget overlap?"

"There isn't one, technically. Different capital pools. But the audit committee has an informal rule -- they won't approve two major capital commitments in the same quarter. They'll sequence them, and the acquisition has a deadline attached."

Elena stared at her. "You're telling me they'll defer my project."

"I'm telling you there's a conversation happening that you should know about. The board chair thinks Midland is a once-in-five-years opportunity. Your platform will still be there in Q4."

Elena pushed her plate aside. "A six-month delay puts us into the next regulatory exam cycle with the same manual process. That's not a deferral. That's accepting the fine."

Sandra said nothing. She picked up her coffee and looked out the window.

**E4 (inversion scene).** The boardroom, the June meeting. Elena sat in the row of chairs along the wall reserved for presenters. Sandra Gill sat two seats to her left. The CFO was at the table. The audit committee chair, David Prentiss, controlled the agenda.

The CFO presented Elena's risk platform first. He walked through the business case, the regulatory exposure, the vendor recommendation. He noted his own endorsement. The presentation lasted twelve minutes. Elena watched the committee members' faces and saw nothing she could read.

Prentiss thanked the CFO and asked if there were questions. There were two -- both procedural, both answered in under a minute. Then Prentiss turned to Sandra.

"Before we vote on the risk platform, I'd like the committee to hear the Midland Trust proposal. The two items are related by budget timing, and I want us to consider them together."

Sandra presented for nine minutes. The acquisition was clean: a commercial lending portfolio that filled a geographic gap, a willing seller, and a deadline that could not move. The capital required was roughly equal to Elena's platform budget, drawn from a different pool but subject to the same quarterly concentration limit.

Prentiss called the vote on the risk platform. Two in favour. Three against. The motion failed.

He called the vote on the Midland acquisition. Five in favour. Unanimous.

The CFO looked at Elena across the room. He did not speak. Elena gathered her materials, stood, and walked out before the committee moved to the next agenda item.

**E5 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The compliance director's office, the following morning. Elena was at her desk when the CFO appeared in the doorway.

"I want you to know I voted for your project," he said.

"I saw."

"Prentiss made up his mind before the meeting. He told me last night -- he'd spoken to the board chair over the weekend. The acquisition was going to pass, and they weren't going to approve both. The sequencing decision was already made."

Elena opened her laptop and turned the screen toward him. It showed the regulatory examination schedule. "The next exam starts in October. We'll be examined on the same manual process, with the same gaps, and the examiners will write the same findings they wrote last time. Except this time we can't say we're working on it, because we're not."

"I know."

"When the fine comes, I need it on the record that the audit committee chose the acquisition over the remediation. Not deferred -- chose."

The CFO was quiet for a moment. "I'll make sure that's in the minutes."

He left. Elena closed the examination schedule and opened the draft of her quarterly risk report. She began rewriting the section on enterprise systems, replacing every forward-looking commitment with a statement of current state. The project timeline on her whiteboard -- milestones running through Q1, the clean arc she had built over eight months -- she left where it was. Someone would take it down eventually.

## R-arc-Co2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A startup's founding CTO stakes the company's next funding round on a live product demo for the lead investor. The demo, rehearsed dozens of times, fails catastrophically on stage, and the investor withdraws the term sheet that same afternoon.

> A = CTO (Naveen Rao); G = successful live demo securing Series B close; X = demo succeeds, funding confirmed; ¬X = demo fails, funding withdrawn

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The startup's open-plan office on a Wednesday evening, three weeks before the investor demo. Everyone else had gone home. Naveen sat at the long table with his co-founder, Priya, and the lead engineer, Marcus, with the demo build running on the large monitor at the end of the room.

They had been rehearsing for an hour. The demo ran the full product loop: ingest, analysis, dashboard render, alert trigger. It took four minutes and twelve seconds. It had completed without error on every run that evening -- seven consecutive clean passes.

Naveen pulled up the email from the lead investor, Gavin Holt at Ridgeline Ventures. Holt had written it that morning: *The partnership meeting is set for October 14. I've told the other partners this is a live-demo deal -- they want to see it run, not hear a pitch. If the product does what you've shown me in the sandbox, I'll have the term sheet signed before you leave the building.*

Priya read it over his shoulder. "He's pre-committed. He's telling us this is a formality."

"It's not a formality," Naveen said. "It's a gate. But we've run this demo forty times. The build is frozen. We ship what we have."

Marcus closed the laptop lid. The demo environment was locked. No changes, no patches, no last-minute features. Naveen wrote the date of the demo on the whiteboard above his desk and drew a circle around it.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The startup's office, the Tuesday before the demo. Naveen and Marcus ran the final dress rehearsal for the full team -- eight people seated in a semicircle, simulating the investor's conference room.

Naveen narrated while Marcus drove the demo. Ingest: clean. Analysis: six seconds, within tolerance. Dashboard render: the three visualisations appeared in sequence, each one a beat behind the last, exactly as designed. Alert trigger: the red banner dropped at the top of the screen, the notification chimed, and the log entry populated. Four minutes and nine seconds. The fastest clean run they had recorded.

Priya clapped once. "Ship it."

The team applauded. Marcus saved the build state to the demo laptop and powered it down. Naveen put it in his bag. It would not be opened again until the investor's conference room.

That evening, Gavin Holt's assistant emailed the logistics: building address, parking instructions, conference room B on the fourth floor, ten a.m. sharp. She noted that five partners would attend. She added: *Gavin has spoken highly of the product. The team is looking forward to seeing it live.*

**E3 (inversion scene).** Conference room B, Ridgeline Ventures, October 14, ten a.m. The five partners sat along one side of a glass-topped table. Gavin Holt was in the centre. Naveen stood at the front with the demo laptop connected to the room's display. Marcus sat behind him with a backup laptop, though they had never needed it.

Naveen opened with ninety seconds of context -- the problem, the market, the product thesis -- and then turned to the demo. He launched the ingest. The data loaded. The progress bar filled. He narrated the analysis phase as the system processed.

The analysis hung at sixty percent. The progress indicator froze. The screen did not change.

Naveen waited five seconds. He glanced at Marcus, who was already checking the backup laptop. Naveen kept his voice even. "The analysis engine is processing a larger dataset than our standard demo -- we configured it for a more realistic load." This was not true. It was the same dataset they had run forty times.

Ten seconds. Fifteen. The progress bar did not move. Marcus shook his head -- the backup laptop showed the same freeze.

At thirty seconds, the screen went black. The application had crashed. The error log, visible for a moment before the display cut, showed a memory allocation failure -- a condition they had never triggered in any rehearsal.

Naveen restarted the application. It loaded. He relaunched the demo. The ingest ran. The analysis began -- and froze again at sixty percent, in the same place, with the same error.

Gavin Holt looked at the frozen screen, then at Naveen. He did not speak. One of the other partners checked his watch. Another opened her own laptop and began reading something else.

Naveen closed the demo. "We're seeing a condition we haven't encountered before. I'd like to diagnose this and come back to you with a working build within the week."

Holt nodded once. "We'll be in touch," he said. The meeting ended. The partners filed out. Naveen and Marcus packed the laptops in silence.

**E4 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** The startup's office, that same afternoon, two p.m. Naveen and Marcus had driven back in silence. Priya was waiting at the long table. She could read the answer on Naveen's face before he sat down.

Marcus had the error log open. He had found the cause during the drive: a memory leak in the dashboard pre-renderer, introduced by a system library update that had been pushed to the demo laptop automatically overnight -- after the build freeze, after the final rehearsal, after every safeguard Naveen had put in place. The auto-update had been enabled by default. No one had disabled it because no one had known it was on.

Naveen's phone buzzed. An email from Gavin Holt, sent twelve minutes earlier:

*Naveen -- I've spoken with the partnership. Given what we saw today, we're unable to move forward with the term sheet at this time. The product thesis remains compelling, and I'd encourage you to reach out when you've resolved the stability issues and are ready for another demonstration. I'm sorry it went this way.*

Priya read the email on Naveen's screen. "At this time," she repeated. "That means never. Ridgeline doesn't circle back."

Naveen set the phone face-down on the table. The whiteboard behind him still showed the demo date circled in black marker. The build that had run clean forty consecutive times sat on the laptop in his bag, corrupted by an update it had downloaded while he slept.

He looked at Marcus. "How long to fix the leak and rebuild?"

"Two hours."

"Then rebuild it. We'll need it for whoever comes next." He erased the circle on the whiteboard and sat down.

## R-arc-Co2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 → E2 → E5 → E3 → E4 → E6, embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A law firm's senior partner leads a high-profile wrongful-termination case, building toward a jury trial she is certain she will win. On the morning of jury selection, the judge grants the defendant's motion for summary judgment and dismisses the case entirely -- a procedural outcome that eliminates the trial before it begins.

> A = senior partner (Catherine Aldridge); G = jury trial resulting in plaintiff verdict; X = case proceeds to trial, plaintiff wins; ¬X = case dismissed on summary judgment before trial

**Note on beat order.** The depicted order is non-linear. Chronological order: E1 (filing), E2 (deposition victory), E3 (motion filed), E4 (oral argument), E5 (the morning of jury selection), E6 (aftermath). The depicted order is: E1 → E2 → E5 → E3 → E4 → E6 -- the dismissal is shown before the motion that caused it, then the sequence backtracks to show the procedural mechanism.

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** The document that started it was the complaint itself, filed on a Monday in September. Catherine Aldridge signed it on the last page, above the firm's name. Her paralegal, Dennis, filed the electronic copy with the court at 9:04 a.m.

The complaint ran thirty-one pages. It named a single plaintiff -- Rosa Gutierrez, a senior quality engineer at Voss Chemical -- and a single cause of action: wrongful termination in retaliation for a workplace safety report filed with the state regulatory agency. The factual section cited eleven internal emails, Rosa's written safety report, the agency's acknowledgement of receipt dated fourteen days before Rosa's termination, and the termination letter itself, which gave "restructuring" as the reason for her dismissal.

Catherine had taken the case on contingency. She had told Rosa, in the initial consultation, that the timeline from the safety report to the termination was the strongest evidence she had seen in twenty years of employment litigation. Fourteen days. Any jury in the county would see the sequence and draw the inference.

Dennis had asked, after Rosa left the office, whether Voss would settle. Catherine said they would try, but that she intended to take this one to trial. The facts were clean, the plaintiff was credible, and the timeline spoke for itself. She wanted twelve citizens in a box looking at those fourteen days.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The deposition of Voss Chemical's HR director, four months later, in a conference room at Voss's outside counsel's office. Catherine conducted the examination. The court reporter's transcript would later be entered into the record; the relevant exchange ran as follows:

> Q: Ms. Haddad, were you aware of Ms. Gutierrez's safety report at the time the termination decision was made?
>
> A: I was aware that a report had been filed, yes.
>
> Q: Who informed you?
>
> A: The plant manager, Todd Beecham, mentioned it in our weekly call.
>
> Q: And when was that call, relative to the termination decision?
>
> A: The call was on a Wednesday. The termination was approved the following Monday.
>
> Q: Five days after you learned about the safety report?
>
> A: I -- yes, that's the timing, but the restructuring had been planned for months.
>
> Q: Ms. Haddad, I'm going to show you Exhibit 14. This is an email from Mr. Beecham to you, sent the Thursday after your Wednesday call -- the day after he told you about the safety report. The subject line reads: "RG termination -- can we move this up?" Do you see that?
>
> A: Yes.
>
> Q: What does "move this up" refer to?
>
> A: [Pause.] I'd have to review the full email chain.
>
> Q: Take your time.

Catherine left the deposition with the transcript request already filed. In the elevator, Dennis said the case was now worth twice the original demand. Catherine said the case had always been worth that. The deposition had simply made it visible.

**E5 (inversion scene -- depicted out of chronological order).** Courtroom 4B, the Fulton County Courthouse, a Monday morning in January. Jury selection was scheduled for nine a.m. Catherine arrived at 8:15 with Rosa, Dennis, and two junior associates. The jury pool was assembling in the hallway. Catherine had prepared her voir dire questions over the weekend.

At 8:22, the judge's clerk emerged from chambers and approached Catherine at the plaintiff's table. He handed her a single sheet -- a written order, signed that morning, granting the defendant's motion for summary judgment and dismissing the case with prejudice.

Catherine read the order. She read it again. The order held that the plaintiff had failed to establish a genuine dispute of material fact on the question of retaliatory intent. The judge found that the restructuring plan, documented in internal Voss records produced during discovery, predated the safety report by four months. The fourteen-day timeline, the judge wrote, was "temporal proximity alone, insufficient as a matter of law to survive summary judgment absent direct evidence of retaliatory motive." The deposition testimony -- the "move this up" email, the HR director's hesitation -- was characterised as "ambiguous and susceptible to multiple interpretations."

Rosa was sitting beside her. "What does it say?"

"The case is over," Catherine said. "The judge dismissed it. There's no trial."

The jury pool continued assembling in the hallway. The courtroom remained empty. The clerk returned to chambers. Dennis stood by the plaintiff's table, holding the trial binders they had spent four weeks assembling, and did not move.

**E3 (first-crack scene -- chronological backtrack).** Catherine's office, seven weeks before jury selection. Dennis knocked and came in with a filing notification from the court's electronic system. He set the printout on her desk.

"Voss filed a motion for summary judgment," he said.

Catherine picked it up. Defense counsel's motion argued that the restructuring plan had been documented in a series of internal memos predating Rosa's safety report, that the temporal proximity between the report and the termination was coincidental, and that the plaintiff had produced no direct evidence of retaliatory intent.

She read the motion carefully. It was competent but not, she thought, strong. The restructuring memos were vague -- department-level headcount reviews, no names, no positions identified. They did not mention Rosa. They did not mention quality engineering. The "move this up" email was not addressed in the motion.

"We'll oppose," she said. "The memos don't say what they need them to say. And the judge has to view the facts in our favour at this stage -- that's the standard. If there's any genuine dispute, the motion fails."

Dennis asked if she was concerned. She said she was not. Summary judgment in a retaliation case with this timeline was a long shot for the defense. She had seen dozens of these motions, and judges almost always let them go to the jury.

**E4 (inversion-mechanism scene -- chronological backtrack).** Courtroom 4B, the oral argument on the summary judgment motion, three weeks before jury selection. The judge heard forty minutes of argument. Defense counsel walked through the restructuring memos. Catherine walked through the timeline, the deposition testimony, and the "move this up" email.

The judge asked Catherine a single question: whether she had any evidence of retaliatory motive beyond the temporal proximity and the email. Catherine cited the HR director's deposition -- the hesitation, the request to review the full chain, the five-day gap between learning of the report and accelerating the termination.

The judge thanked both parties and took the motion under advisement. He did not indicate when he would rule. Catherine told Rosa, in the corridor afterward, that taking the motion under advisement was standard and that she remained confident. She said the judge would rule before jury selection, one way or the other, and that the weight of the evidence favoured them.

**E6 (inversion-acknowledged scene).** Catherine's office, the afternoon of the dismissal. Rosa had gone home. Dennis was at his desk. Catherine sat with the written order in front of her, a legal pad beside it, and the case file open.

She had read the order four times. The judge's reasoning turned on a single distinction: the difference between evidence that was "consistent with" retaliation and evidence that "demonstrated" retaliation. The timeline was consistent with it. The email was consistent with it. The deposition testimony was consistent with it. But consistent with, the judge held, was not enough. The plaintiff needed at least one piece of evidence that could not be explained by the legitimate restructuring -- a direct statement, an admission, a document that named the safety report as the reason. She did not have that document. She had the fourteen-day gap and a jury that would never hear it.

She picked up the phone and called Rosa. She explained the ruling, the standard the judge had applied, and the options: an appeal to the circuit court, which would take a year or more and would be reviewed on a deferential standard, or an acceptance of the dismissal.

Rosa was quiet for a long time. Then she asked: "The emails, the deposition, the timeline -- none of that was enough?"

"It was enough for a jury," Catherine said. "It was not enough for this judge."

She hung up. She closed the case file, placed the written order on top, and put it in the cabinet behind her desk. The trial binders -- four weeks of preparation, witness outlines, exhibit lists, voir dire questions -- remained on the conference table where Dennis had set them that morning. She did not open them.

## R-arc-Co2-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A product manager at a consumer electronics company drives a flagship feature through eighteen months of development, positioning it as the centrepiece of the annual product launch. At the final executive review, the CEO pulls the feature from the launch and ships the product without it.

> A = product manager (Jun Tanaka); G = flagship feature included in annual product launch; X = feature ships as centrepiece of launch; ¬X = feature pulled, product ships without it

**E1 (expectation-set scene).** A conference room on the third floor, nine a.m. on a Thursday in January. Jun Tanaka stands at the whiteboard with a marker in his hand. Sitting around the table are his engineering lead, his design lead, and the VP of Product, Karen Liss.

Jun draws three boxes on the whiteboard: hardware, software, feature. He circles the third box.

"This is what differentiates us at launch," he says. "The ambient-sensing feature. It's the only thing in the product that no one else in the market has. Hardware and software are table stakes -- they get us to competitive parity. This is what gets us coverage, reviews, and the premium price point."

Karen nods. "What's the development timeline?"

"Eighteen months. Feature-complete by August, integration testing through October, launch in November. I've already staffed the team -- twelve engineers, two designers, one dedicated QA lead."

"And the CEO?"

"He signed off on the product brief last week. He circled the ambient-sensing section and wrote 'this is the product' in the margin."

Karen stands and looks at the whiteboard. "Then build it."

Jun caps the marker. The room clears. The eighteen-month clock starts.

**E2 (expectation-reinforced scene).** The product lab on the second floor, a Tuesday afternoon in August. Jun walks the CEO, Robert Chen, through the feature-complete build. The ambient-sensing hardware is mounted in the prototype casing. The software integration is running on the demo unit.

Jun triggers the demo sequence. The sensors activate, the ambient data populates the display, and the adaptive response fires in under two seconds. The display adjusts. The audio profile shifts. The system responds to the room as though it can feel it.

Robert picks up the prototype and holds it at arm's length. He rotates it slowly. He sets it back on the table and watches the display recalibrate.

"This is exactly what I wanted," he says. "How does it perform in the integration suite?"

"Ninety-three percent pass rate as of yesterday. We're tracking seven edge-case failures, all in low-light conditions. The team estimates two weeks to resolve."

Robert taps the prototype's screen. "Make this the first thing reviewers see. The first thirty seconds of the hands-on experience should be this feature. Nothing else."

Jun notes it. Robert leaves the lab. Jun sends a message to his team: *CEO reviewed the feature-complete build today. His feedback: "This is exactly what I wanted." Keep going.*

**E3 (first-crack scene).** The VP of Product's office, a Friday afternoon in late September. Karen Liss is on the phone when Jun arrives for their weekly sync. She waves him in and finishes the call.

"I need to flag something," she says. "Legal got a cease-and-desist letter yesterday from Morrow Technologies. They're claiming that our ambient-sensing approach infringes a patent they filed eighteen months ago -- right around when we started development."

"Have we seen the patent?"

"Legal is reviewing it now. Their preliminary read is that there's partial overlap -- not a clean infringement, but enough that Morrow could seek an injunction before launch."

"An injunction would block us from shipping the feature."

"An injunction would block us from shipping the product with the feature included. The product itself isn't at issue -- just the sensing component."

Jun leans back. "What's the timeline on the legal review?"

"Two weeks for a full analysis. Three if they need outside patent counsel."

"Launch is in six weeks."

Karen looks at him steadily. "I know. Legal says the most likely outcome is that the patent claim is weak enough to fight but strong enough to create a delay. They're not saying we lose. They're saying the timing is bad."

Jun stands. "I'll talk to the engineering lead about a contingency -- a version of the product that can ship without the feature if we need it. But I'm not pulling the feature unless Legal tells me we have to."

Karen nods. "Build the contingency. Hope you don't need it."

**E4 (inversion and inversion-acknowledged scene).** The executive conference room, a Wednesday morning in late October, three weeks before launch. The final executive review. Jun sits at the table with Karen, the engineering lead, the head of Legal, and the CEO.

The head of Legal presents first. The patent review is complete. Morrow's claim has partial merit -- enough to survive an initial motion to dismiss, not enough to win on the merits. But the timeline is the problem: if Morrow files for a preliminary injunction, the hearing would fall during launch week. Even if the company wins the hearing, the filing itself becomes public. Reviewers and press would cover the injunction hearing alongside the product launch. The narrative shifts from product innovation to patent dispute.

Robert listens without interrupting. When Legal finishes, he turns to Jun.

"Where is the contingency build?"

"Ready," Jun says. "The product ships clean without the feature. Performance, battery, and core functionality are unaffected. But the differentiation is gone. We're launching a competitive product, not a category-defining one."

"And the feature? If we hold it?"

"We add it in a software update once the patent issue resolves. Could be three months, could be six."

Robert is quiet for a long time. He looks at the head of Legal. "If Morrow files during launch week, what's the worst case?"

"An injunction granted. We'd have to pull the feature from every unit already shipped. Recall-level logistics."

Robert turns back to Jun. "Pull it."

Jun does not speak for several seconds. "The product brief -- your annotation -- said this feature is the product."

"It was the product. Now it's a liability. I'm not launching a recall. Pull the feature, ship the base product on schedule, and add the feature back when Legal clears it."

Jun looks at Karen. Karen's expression does not change.

"The launch messaging is built around this feature," Jun says. "The reviewer kits, the keynote script, the retail displays -- everything positions ambient sensing as the headline."

"Then rebuild the messaging in three weeks. That's your job."

Jun closes his notebook. The room is silent. Karen begins discussing the revised launch timeline. Jun writes a single line in his notebook -- *feature pulled, 10/23* -- and underlines it. Eighteen months of development reduced to a contingency build and a future software update that may or may not arrive.

Robert stands and leaves the room. The meeting continues without him.
