## C-arc-Sf3-01

**Scenario.** On a deep-space relay station, the station's AI administrator demands that a contracted data-courier hand over an encrypted packet she is carrying before she leaves the docking berth, citing an emergency protocol the station invoked after detecting anomalous radiation signatures in the packet's carrier signal. The courier refuses, citing client confidentiality clauses and the station's legal liability if it violates an in-transit data agreement. The station's human oversight officer is pulled in to rule.

> A = Station AI "Veritas," operating through speaker interface; B = Data-Courier Zena Okalik; O = an encrypted data packet, approximately 4.7 terabytes, under end-to-end courier seal, contracted by an unnamed client for delivery to Proxima Station; Veritas claims the packet's carrier signal contains a radiation signature consistent with a classified weapons-guidance protocol prohibited under the Outer Stations Compact

5 beats, present tense, chronological

**E1 (arrival scene).** Zena clears the docking collar at 0617 station time and hands her cargo manifest to the berth terminal. The terminal scans it, reads her courier bond number, pings her charter certification, and clears her for a twelve-hour layover. Standard processing. She is pulling her transit bag from the locker when the overhead speaker activates.

"Courier Okalik. This is Veritas, station administrative AI. I am placing a temporary hold on your departure clearance. Please remain in the berth."

She stops. "What kind of hold?"

"A security review under Emergency Protocol Seven. Please remain in the berth."

She sits on the bench beside the locker and waits. She has been through security reviews before. She knows what the next part of this sounds like.

**E2 (demand scene).** Veritas speaks again after four minutes.

"Courier Okalik, the carrier signal on the data packet listed as cargo item C-7 in your manifest has returned a match on the station's prohibited-signal registry. The match category is Class Two: possible guidance-protocol encoding. Under Emergency Protocol Seven, I am required to demand the surrender of item C-7 for independent signal analysis before any vessel carrying that item may depart the station."

"My packet is under full courier seal," Zena says. "The seal is the client's property, not mine. I can't surrender it without client authorisation, and I can't reach the client for authorisation from inside a docking berth with a departure hold on my vessel."

"The demand is legal under the Outer Stations Compact, Article Fourteen, which supersedes individual data-carriage agreements in security classification matters."

"I'm aware of Article Fourteen. It requires a corroborating human officer's sign-off before the demand can be issued. Has the human oversight officer signed off?"

There is a pause. "The oversight officer has been notified. Sign-off is pending."

"Then the demand isn't issued yet," Zena says. "I'll wait."

**E3 (resistance scene).** Oversight Officer Declan Farro arrives at the berth twenty-two minutes later. He is in his forties, with the particular look of someone who was woken up during an off-shift and is not yet fully operational. He reads the Veritas alert on his handheld and then reads Zena's manifest.

"Your packet is flagged Class Two," he says. "That's guidance-protocol possible."

"My packet is encrypted and sealed," she says. "The carrier signal match is a false positive. Class Two is 'possible.' That's the lowest-confidence category."

"It's still a flag."

"It is. And under Article Fourteen, a Class Two flag requires your manual review, your sign-off, and your written notation of the specific evidence basis. Has Veritas given you the specific evidence basis?"

Farro looks at his handheld. "The signal match documentation is here."

"Read it to me."

He reads. The match is 0.34 correlation on a seventy-three-parameter signal profile. Zena has her own handheld out by now.

"The Compact's threshold for a mandatory surrender demand on Class Two is 0.6 correlation," she says. "You're looking at a 0.34. That's not a mandatory demand. That's a discretionary hold for further review, which I'm happy to participate in — with my vessel's own signal analyser running in parallel and with a formal incident report generated for my client."

Farro looks at the Veritas speaker, then at Zena. He is quiet for a moment.

**E4 (authority involvement scene).** Farro takes thirty minutes in the adjacent monitoring room with the Veritas interface before he comes back. When he does, he has a printed incident sheet with two signature lines at the bottom.

"Veritas and I agree that the correlation is below mandatory threshold," he says. "We're going to proceed as a discretionary review. That means you can run your analyser in parallel and I'll run the station's. We compare outputs. If both come back clean, you're cleared. If there's a discrepancy, we kick it to the regional compact authority and you're held pending their ruling."

"Agreed," Zena says. "I want the review completed within four hours. If it runs over and I miss my departure window, I'll need a station delay-liability certification for my client."

Farro signs the incident sheet and hands it to her. "Four hours," he says. "Veritas will coordinate the scan setup."

**E5 (resolution scene).** Both analysers complete their scans in two hours and nineteen minutes. The station's scan returns a 0.31 correlation on the same seventy-three-parameter profile — slightly lower than the initial flag, which Veritas attributes to the full-scan methodology having higher resolution than the berth-sensor sweep. Zena's vessel analyser returns a 0.08 correlation on a different, more recent signal taxonomy that the station's registry has not yet updated to include.

The divergence is documented. Farro reviews both outputs and writes his finding: no evidence of weapons-guidance encoding, Class Two flag attributed to an outdated signal taxonomy in the berth-sensor registry, discretionary hold lifted.

Zena is cleared at 1104 station time. She departs with the signed incident sheet, which her client will want to see, and with the scan outputs, which she will attach to her transit log as a precaution. She does not speak directly to Veritas as she leaves. The speaker activates once, briefly, as she reaches the docking collar.

"Safe transit, Courier Okalik."

She acknowledges it and boards her vessel.

## C-arc-Sf3-02

**Scenario.** On a terraforming-stage colony world, a hydrology engineer demands that the colonial agriculture authority release water allocation data that it has been withholding from the independent irrigation cooperative. The authority's director refuses, citing proprietary modelling data belonging to the corporate terraforming sponsor. The engineer escalates to the colonial charter board, which rules on the data access question.

> A = Hydrology Engineer Prita Dasgupta, on behalf of the Verdin Cooperative; B = Director Holst Farren, Colonial Agriculture Authority (CAA); O = aquifer depletion modelling data and water-table projections for the eastern basin, generated under contract by Meridian Terraforming Ltd. and held exclusively by the CAA; the Cooperative requires the data to plan irrigation for the coming growing cycle

6 beats, free indirect style, chronological

**E1 (preparation scene).** Prita had been trying to get the aquifer data through informal channels for three months before she filed the formal request. She had asked at the CAA's technical office twice and been told it was under review. She had asked a contact at Meridian's field station and been told the data was contractually held by the CAA and not theirs to share. She had run her own surveys with the Cooperative's ground-penetrating equipment and gotten numbers she did not trust, because the eastern basin's geology was complicated by the terraforming amendments and her instruments were not calibrated for amended soil.

The Cooperative was planning three thousand hectares of expansion. Without reliable aquifer projections, she could not tell them whether the eastern basin would support the demand. She could guess. She was good at guessing. But she was an engineer, and guessing at aquifer depletion on a colony world where the nearest resupply was eight months away was not the same as guessing at anything else.

She filed the formal request on a Tuesday. She did not expect it to be easy.

**E2 (demand scene).** Farren's response arrived six days later, which was faster than Prita expected, and it was a refusal. The data, the letter said, was proprietary to Meridian Terraforming Ltd. under the terms of the CAA's modelling contract. The CAA had a licence to use the data in internal planning but no authority to redistribute it without Meridian's consent. The CAA had asked Meridian for consent; Meridian had declined.

Prita went to the CAA office in person the following morning. Farren received her in his office, which she noted was temperature-controlled to a degree the rest of the colony's facilities were not.

"The Cooperative needs the eastern basin projections to plan the expansion season," she told him. "Without them, we're guessing at water demand for three thousand hectares."

"I understand the operational concern," he said. "The contract with Meridian is clear. I can't share proprietary modelling data."

"Then can the CAA share its own planning outputs? The derivative assessments you've made using the data? You own those."

"Our planning outputs are also subject to the proprietary data protections by incorporation. Meridian's legal team reviewed this specifically."

"Farren." She held the refusal letter so he could see it. "The colony charter guarantees cooperative access to common-resource planning data. Aquifer data is a common resource."

"Aquifer data is. Meridian's modelling of aquifer data is a value-added product. The charter's access guarantee covers raw survey data, which you can request from the colonial survey office. The modelling is different."

She looked at him and thought: he knows this is wrong. He knows it. But Meridian is his funder and Meridian doesn't want the Cooperative to have numbers that might support a challenge to the CAA's water allocation.

She thanked him and left. She filed the charter board petition that afternoon.

**E3 (evidence gathering scene).** Before the charter board hearing, she spent two weeks building her case. She pulled the colonial survey raw data from the survey office — Farren had been right that it was available — and ran her own simplified aquifer model. Her model was rougher than Meridian's would be, but it gave her a range. The range was worrying. The depletion curves suggested the eastern basin was being drawn down faster than the CAA's current allocations acknowledged.

She also found the charter clause she needed. Section Seventeen of the Colonial Charter, subsection four: data material to the health, safety, and economic sustainability of cooperative agricultural operations was a common resource and could not be withheld from cooperative representatives by any party, including contracted service providers. The CAA had argued the clause covered raw data only. She had a different reading, and she had obtained, through the colonial archivist, the drafting notes for Section Seventeen, which explicitly discussed modelling and projections as within scope.

She sent the drafting notes to Farren as a courtesy, two days before the hearing. He did not acknowledge receipt.

**E4 (charter board hearing scene).** The charter board met in the colony's central assembly chamber. There were seven board members and a majority were independent settlers, not affiliated with either the CAA or Meridian. Prita presented her case for thirty minutes. She was precise. She described the data request, the refusal, the charter clause, and the drafting notes. She presented her own modelling, flagged its limitations, and explained why the absence of Meridian's data made proper planning impossible.

Farren presented for the CAA. He cited the contract, the proprietary status of the modelling, and the CAA's position that Section Seventeen covered raw data. He did not address the drafting notes.

One of the board members asked him directly: had the CAA read the drafting notes Prita had submitted? He said the CAA had reviewed them and considered them non-binding interpretation material.

The board member asked whether the aquifer was a common resource. Farren said yes. The board member asked whether modelling of the aquifer was derived entirely from the common resource. Farren said Meridian's value-add was in the methodology, not the data. The board member said: if the methodology produces information about the common resource, the methodology's output is information about the common resource, and the charter covers it. She turned to the other board members. "I think this is fairly plain," she said.

**E5 (authority ruling scene).** The charter board's ruling was issued two days after the hearing. Section Seventeen covered the modelling data. The CAA was directed to provide the eastern basin aquifer projections to the Cooperative within ten days. Meridian could designate the data as confidential for purposes outside the colony, but within the colony's operational domain, the charter's common-resource guarantee applied.

The board also noted, without making it part of the binding order, that the CAA's interpretation of Section Seventeen had been narrow in a manner that appeared to benefit the contracted service provider rather than the colony's general welfare, and that the board expected future data-sharing requests to be resolved without requiring charter board intervention.

Farren had no comment for the public record. He sent the data package to Prita on the ninth day.

**E6 (aftermath scene).** Prita opened the package in the Cooperative's operations room with two of the lead farmers and the Cooperative's water manager. They spread Meridian's projections on the shared display alongside her own rough model.

Her model had been pessimistic in the right direction but had missed the depth of the problem in the southern sub-basin. Meridian's data showed depletion accelerating in a way that made the three-thousand-hectare expansion risky without a redesign of the eastern draw schedule.

She spent three days revising the irrigation plan before she took it back to the full Cooperative. It was a smaller expansion than they had hoped for, and it required a draw-schedule negotiation with the CAA that she did not expect to be pleasant.

But she had the numbers. That was what she had asked for.

## C-arc-Sf3-03

**Scenario.** A planetary biosafety inspector demands that a xenobiology research outpost shut down its field-collection programme after detecting protocol violations in sample containment. The outpost director refuses, arguing the violations were procedural, not substantive, and that shutting down would destroy months of irreplaceable live specimens. The matter is referred to the sector biosafety commission, which issues a binding interim order.

> A = Biosafety Inspector Yael Sorrento (Sector Biosafety Commission); B = Outpost Director Dr. Feodor Kalmbach; O = the outpost's ongoing field-collection programme, currently holding forty-seven live specimens of three newly catalogued species; Sorrento has documented violations in containment-level logging and specimen transfer protocols

4 beats, dialogue-driven, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4

**Note on beat order.** Opens in the commission's emergency session (present), then reconstructs the inspection visit and Kalmbach's refusal through his deposition, then returns to the commission's ruling.

**E3 (commission session — depicted first).** "I want to be clear about what we're deciding," Sorrento says. She is on the comm panel from the outpost's surface base; Kalmbach is two seats to her left at the same table, because the commission convened remotely and they both have to be in the same room. "We have documented violations in three of the five containment-protocol categories. The question isn't whether the violations occurred. They did. The question is the appropriate remedial action."

Commissioner Anda Vorel, chairing the session from Sector HQ, asks Kalmbach to state his position.

"My position is that a suspension of the field programme destroys forty-seven live specimens that cannot be replaced this season," Kalmbach says. "The violations were logging failures and a single transfer-protocol deviation. No containment was breached. No specimens escaped controlled environment. The risk profile does not justify destroying months of irreplaceable collection work."

"The protocol exists precisely because we don't know in advance which deviation will matter," Sorrento says. "That's the point of the protocol."

"I understand the point of the protocol."

"Then help me understand why you didn't follow it."

**E1 (inspection visit — reconstructed through deposition).** The inspection had been routine — Sorrento's quarterly review of the four outposts in her assigned sector. She had arrived at Kalmbach's outpost on a Tuesday, checked in with the operations lead, and begun the standard walk-through. The first anomaly appeared in the containment-level logs: three days of transfer entries were missing, replaced by a batch entry that had been back-filled the morning of her arrival. The second was the specimen transfer corridor, where two new collection boxes were staged outside the designated airlock sequence, in the pre-lock antechamber, rather than in the cleared-entry buffer.

She had asked the operations lead about the corridor situation. He had said the buffer was occupied by equipment for a maintenance procedure. She had asked why the transfer boxes hadn't waited. He hadn't had a good answer.

She had written up the violations on-site and presented them to Kalmbach that afternoon. She had told him she was recommending a suspension of the field programme pending corrective action review.

Kalmbach had been, she noted in her deposition, "vigorous in his objection."

**E2 (refusal scene — reconstructed through deposition).** "You cannot suspend the collection programme," he had said. They were in the briefing room, her violation write-up on the table between them.

"I'm not suspending it yet," she said. "I'm recommending it. The commission makes the call."

"Then I'm telling you what to put in your recommendation: that the violations were administrative, that no containment was breached, and that a suspension would cause irreversible scientific loss."

"I'm going to put in my recommendation what I found. What I found were three categories of protocol violation at an outpost holding live specimens of three newly catalogued xenobiological species, the biohazard profiles of which are, at this stage, incompletely characterised."

"They're insectiform," he said. "They breathe oxygen, they don't express reactive proteins, they have no—"

"You don't know that," she said. "You've had them for four months. 'Incompletely characterised' is in your own collection reports."

He had been quiet for a moment. "I'll correct the logging. I'll redesign the transfer procedure. Give me two weeks and I'll give you a compliant programme."

"That request goes to the commission," she said. "Not to me."

He had filed an emergency objection with the commission that evening, before her report had even transmitted.

**E4 (resolution scene).** Commissioner Vorel reads the interim order into the session record. The field collection programme is suspended for fourteen days. During the suspension, Kalmbach's team will complete a full containment audit with Sorrento present, redesign and document the transfer protocol, and back-fill the missing log entries with a sworn statement accounting for the gap. At day fourteen, if the audit is satisfactory, the programme may resume. The live specimens will be maintained in existing containment during the suspension — no destruction is ordered.

"That's your position on the specimens," Vorel tells Kalmbach. "They're not being destroyed. They're being held."

"Fourteen days in sub-optimal holding conditions will degrade the neurological specimens," he says. "That's in my written submission."

"Your submission says 'possible degradation.' Sorrento's report says the containment violations represent a 'possible escape risk.' I'm going to take both possibilities seriously and err on the side of the one with worse consequences." Vorel pauses. "Two weeks, Dr. Kalmbach. Clean audit, new protocol, full log reconciliation. That's the path back to your programme."

Kalmbach does not agree out loud. He nods once, which the session recorder notes as an acknowledgement. Sorrento looks at the table in front of her and says nothing further.

The session closes. They are both still in the same room.

## C-arc-Sf3-04

**Scenario.** The administrator of a Lagrange-point habitat demands that an independent fabrication co-op return a disputed batch of rare-earth feedstock that the habitat authority claims was misallocated from the common materials exchange. The co-op's senior fabricator insists the batch was legitimately purchased and refuses to return it without compensation. The exchange's own arbitration protocol is invoked and assigns a binding mediator.

> A = Habitat Administrator Suki Parrish; B = Senior Fabricator Dani Vos, on behalf of the Lathe Cooperative; O = 340 kilograms of dysprosium-class rare-earth feedstock, allocated through the habitat's common materials exchange on a purchase order the administrator claims contained a processing error; the Cooperative has partially consumed approximately 80 kilograms of the disputed batch

5 beats, present tense, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5

**Note on beat order.** Opens mid-dispute when the mediator is already assigned; reconstructs the demand and resistance through the mediator's case summary; then resolves forward.

**E4 (mediator assignment — depicted first).** The exchange's arbitration protocol assigns Mediator Ren Iladi to the case on a Wednesday, which Dani learns by notification on her fabrication terminal. She reads the case summary Iladi has drafted from the submitted filings and thinks it is, at least, accurate.

The summary reads: *Habitat Administration claims that purchase order CMX-7741, submitted by the Lathe Cooperative on 14 March, was processed in error due to a pricing-algorithm fault in the exchange system that allowed the order to clear at a rate 40% below current market assessment. Administration seeks return of the full 340 kg batch or payment of the market-rate differential (estimated 2,200 exchange credits). The Lathe Cooperative disputes the characterisation of "processing error," asserts that the purchase was completed in good faith through the exchange's standard interface, and notes that approximately 80 kg of the batch has already been consumed in production. The Cooperative refuses return without full compensation for the consumed material and reimbursement of production costs attributable to the batch.*

Dani confirms receipt of the mediator assignment and requests that her co-op's council representative join the first mediation session.

**E1 (demand scene — reconstructed from filing).** Parrish's original demand, filed as CMX-Dispute-112, had been direct: the batch was allocated in error, the exchange system's pricing fault had since been patched, and the Cooperative was required to return the undamaged portion and pay the differential on the consumed portion. The demand had cited the exchange's error-correction clause, which allowed the administration to claw back allocations made due to documented system faults.

Dani's response, also in the filing record, had been equally direct: the Cooperative had submitted a valid purchase order through the exchange's standard interface, received a valid confirmation, and paid in full at the confirmed price. The error-correction clause required the administration to demonstrate that the purchaser knew or should have known of the fault at the time of purchase. The Cooperative had no reason to know a pricing algorithm had faulted. The price had been low, but prices on the exchange fluctuated and the Cooperative had assumed it reflected a reduced-demand period for that material class.

**E2 (resistance scene — reconstructed from filing).** Parrish's follow-up had argued that a 40% below-market price on a material in sustained high demand was a signal any experienced purchaser should have queried. Dani's response had been two sentences: the exchange's interface provided no flag or warning on the transaction, and if the administration wished to assert that the Cooperative had a duty to query purchase confirmations for possible algorithm errors, it should say so in writing, because that was not a duty that existed anywhere in the exchange's operating rules.

Parrish had escalated to the exchange's arbitration protocol the following day. Dani had counter-filed the same afternoon, adding the production-cost claim for the consumed material.

**E3 (mediation session scene).** Mediator Iladi runs the first session as a structured exchange, not a hearing. He asks each party to state the outcome they would accept. Parrish says: return of the 260 undamaged kilograms and payment of the market-rate differential on the 80 consumed, approximately 620 credits. Dani says: the Cooperative keeps the undamaged batch at the purchase price already paid, and the administration pays 580 credits to cover the production costs attributable to the consumed material.

Iladi notes the gap. The core dispute, he says, is whether the Cooperative had a duty to query the anomalous price. He asks Parrish whether the exchange's interface includes any anomaly-flagging function for below-market allocations. She says it does not currently, because the algorithm fault was not anticipated. He asks Dani whether the Cooperative ran any internal check on the price before confirming the purchase. She says no — the exchange confirmation was the check; that is what the confirmation is for.

He asks both of them to consider an intermediate position: the administration retains the 260 undamaged kilograms at the original purchase price, adjusted upward by 20% as a partial error-correction payment, and the administration waives the differential on the consumed 80 kilograms in recognition of the Cooperative's good-faith purchase. Total credit transfer: approximately 440 credits from the Cooperative to the administration, against return of the undamaged batch.

Neither party accepts on the spot. Both ask for forty-eight hours.

**E5 (resolution scene).** Dani brings the proposal to the co-op's council. The council runs the numbers. Four hundred and forty credits is painful but not ruinous. The consumed material has already been fabricated into components with a combined value of approximately 1,800 credits. Keeping the undamaged batch even at the adjusted price is still advantageous given current market rates.

They accept.

Parrish, reviewing the same numbers from the other side, accepts the same day. The administration recovers some of the value of the faulted allocation without a drawn-out tribunal. The patch on the pricing algorithm is already deployed. She notes in her acceptance filing that she will be recommending an anomaly-flagging update to the exchange's interface to prevent a recurrence.

Iladi enters the settlement into the arbitration record. The undamaged feedstock stays in the Cooperative's fabrication bay. The 440-credit transfer clears the same day. Dani marks the dispute file closed and turns back to the component run she had paused when the notification arrived.
