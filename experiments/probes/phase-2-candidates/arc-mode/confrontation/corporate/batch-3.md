## C-arc-Co3-01

**Scenario.** A regional sales director refuses to hand over her client database when the company restructures and folds her territory into a national account managed by a different team. She argues the accounts were built on her relationships and that transferring them without transition support will destroy the revenue. The VP of Sales demands the transfer by end of quarter.

> A = VP of Sales (Renata Szymańska); B = Regional Sales Director (Dominic Okafor); O = client database and account ownership for the Pacific Northwest territory (approximately 140 named accounts, estimated $11M annual recurring revenue)

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (positioning scene).** The reorg deck had been circulating since Tuesday and by Thursday afternoon Dominic had read it four times. He printed the org chart on the last page and set it on his desk. His name appeared in a grey box labelled "transition," connected by a dotted line to the new National Accounts structure. His accounts — the ones he had spent six years building — were listed in a separate panel under a director he had never met.

He spent Thursday and Friday compiling a counter-proposal. He pulled the CRM records, ran the renewal rates by relationship owner, and built a slide showing that accounts where he held the primary relationship renewed at 94%, compared to 71% for accounts where an inside team held the contact. He sent the deck to Renata at 5:30 on Friday with a note asking to discuss before any transfer decision was finalised.

She did not reply over the weekend.

**E2 (demand scene).** Her office, Monday morning. She had the reorg deck open on her monitor and she did not refer to Dominic's counter-proposal once.

"I need the account records transferred to the National Accounts queue by the thirtieth," she said. "Full contact history, opportunity notes, renewal dates, and the relationship maps you've been maintaining in the custom fields. Everything."

"I sent you the retention analysis," he said.

"I read it. The decision has been made at the level above me. The question isn't whether we restructure. The question is whether we do it cleanly."

"Renata, thirty-four of those accounts have specific agreements with me about transition handling. Two of them have it in the contract — I'm named as the account contact until renewal. I can't just hand the file to someone who's never spoken to them."

She looked at him. "Have the files in the queue by the thirtieth."

**E3 (resistance scene).** He transferred sixty-eight accounts — the ones with no contractual or relational complication — and held the remaining seventy-two. He sent Renata a memo documenting the two contracts that named him by role, the thirty-two accounts that were mid-renewal-cycle and had been promised continuity, and a proposed transition timeline of ninety days with a named handoff protocol.

He copied the General Counsel's office on the memo as a protective measure. He did not copy Renata's manager.

Three days later, Renata forwarded the memo to her manager and to HR with a single line above it: "Dominic is refusing to comply with the restructuring directive. Please advise on next steps."

**E4 (escalation scene).** The meeting was in a conference room on the executive floor that Dominic had never used. Renata was there. Her manager, the Chief Revenue Officer, was at the head of the table. HR had sent a representative. The General Counsel's office had sent a junior attorney who said almost nothing and took notes throughout.

The CRO told Dominic the account transfer was not optional and asked him to explain his position directly. Dominic walked through the two contracts, the renewal-cycle timing, and the customer commitments. He said he was not refusing to transfer — he was requesting a transition plan that protected the company from breach of the contractual language and from the customer attrition risk his analysis showed.

The CRO asked the General Counsel's representative about the contracts. The representative confirmed that two agreements contained language naming the account contact role and that a summary transfer without notification could create a breach-of-service claim.

The CRO looked at Renata. "Why didn't we catch this in the reorg planning?"

She did not answer directly. She said the contracts were non-standard and that the regional team should have flagged them during the initial review.

The meeting ended without resolution. The CRO said he would review the contracts personally and reconvene within the week.

**E5 (resolution scene).** The CRO's decision came in writing. The two contracted accounts would receive a sixty-day formal transition period with Dominic named as the outgoing contact on all communications. The remaining seventy held accounts would transfer to National Accounts within thirty days, with Dominic required to participate in handoff calls for the thirty-two mid-renewal accounts.

Dominic was asked to acknowledge the directive and confirm the transfer schedule. He replied the same day accepting the terms.

Renata sent him a calendar invite for a standing handoff meeting that appeared every Tuesday for the next eight weeks. She did not add a greeting to the invite description. He accepted it without comment.

## C-arc-Co3-02

**Scenario.** A startup's CTO refuses the board's demand that she share the technical architecture documents with a prospective acquirer during due diligence, arguing the acquirer is a competitor and the company has no deal protection in place. The board chair insists the documents are required to close the acquisition. The dispute goes to outside counsel.

> A = Board Chair (Imogen Faulkner); B = CTO (Sebastião Lemos); O = technical architecture documentation, including system design, IP filings in progress, and the proprietary inference stack

4 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4

**Note on beat order.** Opens with the escalation to outside counsel (present), then reconstructs the demand and resistance through the CTO's perspective, then resolves in the counsel's office.

**E3 (escalation scene — depicted first).** The email from outside counsel arrived on a Wednesday. The subject line read: *Re: Meridian/Holloway Acquisition — Document Access Dispute — Joint Call Requested*. Sebastião read it standing at his kitchen counter before he had made coffee. Counsel represented the board. A second firm had been retained to represent him and the founding team. The call was scheduled for Friday.

He had expected it. What he had not expected was the footnote at the bottom of the email, in smaller text, noting that the board had formally documented his refusal as a material obstacle to the acquisition and had requested that legal counsel assess whether his conduct constituted a breach of his fiduciary duty as an officer.

He called his attorney at seven-thirty.

**E1 (demand scene).** Three days earlier, Imogen had called him directly, which she rarely did. The board meeting had run long and she was still in the office, and her voice had the particular flatness it got when she had already finished arguing with herself about something.

"The Holloway team needs the architecture stack by Friday," she said. "Full system design, the inference pipeline documentation, the IP inventory."

"We don't have an NDA with enforcement teeth," he said. "The standard form they signed covers disclosure to third parties, but Holloway is a direct competitor in the inference market. If this deal falls through, we've handed them the core of our technical moat."

"The deal won't fall through if you get them the documents."

"You don't know that. We need a no-shop clause and a breakup fee before I put anything in their hands that they can walk away with."

"Sebastião, the board has reviewed the NDA. It's standard. The attorneys have reviewed it. I'm asking you as chair to release the documents."

"I'm telling you as CTO that I won't. Not without deal protection."

She was quiet for a moment. "I'll be in touch," she said, and ended the call.

**E2 (resistance scene).** He spent the next two days building his position. He pulled the NDA, marked the clauses that he believed were insufficient, and had his own attorney review it independently. The attorney agreed: the NDA covered disclosure to third parties and contained a standard two-year non-use clause, but it contained no specific provisions preventing Holloway from using architectural knowledge in their own product development, only from sharing the documents themselves. The distinction was the one that mattered.

He sent the board a four-page memo. He attached the annotated NDA. He proposed two alternatives: a mutual binding term sheet with a sixty-day exclusivity period before document sharing, or a structured data room with legal observers from both sides and a clause allowing the founding team to pull documents if the deal was terminated without cause.

The board held a closed session that he was not invited to attend. Imogen's assistant called afterward to inform him that the board had reviewed the memo and that outside counsel had been engaged.

**E4 (resolution scene).** The call was forty minutes. Both legal teams, both principals, and the board chair. Outside counsel opened by framing the question as a governance matter: did the CTO have the authority to unilaterally withhold documents requested by the board in the context of a board-approved acquisition process?

Sebastião's attorney said yes, in this specific case, because the CTO's fiduciary duty ran to the company and its shareholders, not solely to the board, and releasing competitively sensitive IP to a buyer without adequate deal protection exposed the company to material harm regardless of whether the acquisition closed.

The board's counsel acknowledged the argument had merit. She said the board was prepared to agree to a limited exclusivity term — thirty days, with a defined list of documents in scope — before the full architecture package was released.

Sebastião asked for forty-five days and a breakup fee trigger. The board counsel said thirty days and no breakup fee, but she would add a document-return clause requiring Holloway to certify destruction of all materials if the deal was not executed.

He accepted. Imogen said nothing during the resolution portion of the call. Her attorney spoke for her throughout.

The exclusivity term sheet was signed the following Monday. Sebastião transferred the first tranche of architecture documents that afternoon.

## C-arc-Co3-03

**Scenario.** A managing editor at a trade publication demands that her editorial director pull a drafted investigative piece on a major advertiser before publication. The editorial director refuses and threatens to resign if the piece is spiked. The publisher is brought in to arbitrate.

> A = Managing Editor (Priya Harland); B = Editorial Director (Cass Rutherford); O = a 4,000-word investigative piece documenting labour violations at Fenwick Industrial, the publication's largest display advertiser

6 beats, present tense, chronological

**E1 (preparation scene).** Cass has had the Fenwick piece for three weeks before Priya reads it. She sends it to Priya as a matter of routine: anything over 2,500 words and touching a named company goes through the managing editor's desk before the copy editor sees it. She does not flag it as sensitive. She assumes Priya already knows about the reporting — the journalist, Tariq, has been working the story for four months and has filed two status updates.

Priya reads it on a Tuesday afternoon and does not respond until Thursday.

**E2 (demand scene).** Thursday, the managing editor's office. Priya keeps the door open, which means she is not planning to shout, but her voice when she speaks to Cass is very even in the way it gets when she has thought carefully about what she wants to say.

"I need you to hold the Fenwick piece," she says. "I'm not saying kill it. I'm saying hold it. We're in renewal negotiations with their marketing team right now and the timing is a problem."

"The timing is the story," Cass says. "Tariq has eleven sourced incidents and three former supervisors on the record. The violations are documented to OSHA. We've given Fenwick ten days to respond and they've responded. If we hold it, we're not holding it for editorial reasons."

"I'm aware of what we'd be doing. I'm asking you to hold it until the renewal closes."

"When does the renewal close?"

"Six to eight weeks."

"Priya, that story is six to eight weeks old right now. If anyone else gets the OSHA records—"

"Then we'll deal with that. Hold the piece."

**E3 (resistance scene).** Cass does not hold the piece. She moves it to the copy edit queue and tells Tariq it is on track for the following issue. She sends Priya a memo that afternoon explaining her decision: the piece meets the publication's editorial standards, the sourcing is verified, the subject has been given full opportunity to respond, and holding it for commercial reasons would require disclosure to the editorial staff and potentially to Tariq, whose contract contains a clause protecting him from advertiser interference in editorial decisions.

She copies the publication's ethics policy to the memo. She does not threaten to resign in the memo, but she tells Tariq privately that she will not stay at the publication if the piece is killed for commercial reasons.

Priya receives the memo, reads it, and forwards it to the publisher with a single line: "We need to talk today."

**E4 (authority involvement scene).** The publisher, Wendell Achebe, meets with both of them separately before he meets with them together. Cass goes first. She presents the story, the sourcing, the timeline, and the ethics policy. She is direct: this is a story the publication has a professional obligation to run. If it is held for advertiser reasons, that decision needs to be made by Wendell himself, in writing, and she will need to know whether the ethics clause in her own contract is operative.

Priya goes second. Wendell does not share what she says with Cass.

**E5 (failed negotiation scene).** The three of them meet in Wendell's office. He proposes a middle path: run the piece but move it from the cover feature slot to the industry news section, reducing the prominence and softening what he calls the "confrontational framing" of the headline.

Cass says the piece was commissioned as a feature and demoting it to news without editorial justification is a form of suppression. The headline is accurate and reflects the tone of the reporting.

Priya says the current headline — "Fenwick's Safety Record: What the OSHA Data Shows" — is appropriate for the news section and that the story's content, not its placement, is what matters.

Cass asks Priya directly: if the Fenwick account were not up for renewal, would she be requesting this demotion? Priya does not answer the question. Wendell intervenes and says that is not a productive line of discussion.

The meeting ends without agreement.

**E6 (resolution scene).** Wendell's decision arrives by email the next morning. The piece will run as a feature, in its original slot, with the headline unchanged. He notes in the email that the publication's editorial independence policy does not permit commercial considerations to determine placement or content decisions, and that he has communicated this to the Fenwick account team directly as a courtesy.

He also notes that the renewal negotiation is ongoing and that he has spoken with Fenwick's marketing director, who expressed displeasure but did not withdraw. He states that he expects both Priya and Cass to manage the fallout professionally.

Cass sends the piece to layout that afternoon. Priya does not reply to Wendell's email. The piece runs the following week. Fenwick's renewal is signed, at a reduced rate, eleven days later.

## C-arc-Co3-04

**Scenario.** A departing senior data scientist refuses to return a proprietary model she developed while employed, claiming the model incorporates methods she originated before joining the company. Her former employer's legal team demands the return of all training artefacts and code. The matter goes to arbitration.

> A = General Counsel, Varco Analytics (Theodora Ngai); B = Senior Data Scientist (Mikhail Brennan); O = a gradient-boosted ensemble model ("Lark") trained on Varco's proprietary dataset, along with associated training scripts, feature engineering notebooks, and model weights

5 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5

**Note on beat order.** Opens with the arbitration session (present), then reconstructs the employment context and the dispute through embedded correspondence, then returns to the arbitration resolution.

**E4 (arbitration session — depicted first).** The arbitration room is on the fourteenth floor of a building that handles commercial disputes and smells faintly of recycled air and carpet cleaner. Mikhail sits on one side of the table with his attorney. Theodora sits on the other with two associates and a paralegal. The arbitrator is a former IP litigator named Chen who has asked both parties to summarise their positions before she asks questions.

Theodora goes first. Varco's position is that Mikhail developed the Lark model exclusively during his employment, using Varco's data, compute, and tooling, under an employment agreement that assigned all work product to the company. The model is Varco's property. She wants all artefacts returned and all copies destroyed, certified by a neutral forensic examiner.

Mikhail's attorney responds that the dispute turns on a single question: whether the feature-engineering methodology at the core of the Lark model was developed before Mikhail's employment began. If it was, then Lark is a derivative of pre-existing work, and the employment agreement's prior-inventions clause — which Mikhail disclosed at hiring — governs the ownership question, not the general work-product clause.

Arbitrator Chen asks Mikhail's attorney to substantiate the timeline.

**E1 (pre-employment record — embedded document).** His attorney produces three exhibits. The first is a GitHub repository, timestamped fourteen months before Mikhail joined Varco, containing a notebook titled "hierarchical_feature_selection_v0.3." The notebook implements a feature importance ranking method using a recursive elimination approach with a cross-validated stability criterion that does not appear in any published paper Theodora can name on the record.

The second exhibit is a conference presentation abstract, submitted and accepted eight months before he joined Varco, describing the method in general terms as a "stability-weighted recursive elimination framework for tabular ensemble models."

The third is his prior-inventions disclosure, signed on his first day at Varco and cosigned by the HR director, listing "recursive feature elimination methodology with stability weighting, applied to gradient-boosted architectures" as a pre-existing invention excluded from the work-product assignment clause.

**E2 (demand scene — reconstructed).** Theodora had sent the initial demand letter six weeks earlier, two days after Mikhail's departure. The letter had required him to return all model artefacts, code, and training data derived from Varco's proprietary datasets within ten business days. It had stated that the Lark model was Varco's property in its entirety, developed entirely during employment, and that any failure to return the artefacts would be treated as misappropriation of trade secrets.

Mikhail had forwarded the letter to his attorney without responding directly.

**E3 (resistance — reconstructed from response letter).** His attorney's response, submitted eight days later, had stated that Mikhail did not dispute Varco's ownership of the training data, the compute records, or the final trained weights of the Lark model. He offered to return all of those without condition.

What he disputed was Varco's claim to the feature-engineering methodology itself — the algorithmic approach that constituted the model's core technical innovation. That method predated his employment, had been disclosed in the prior-inventions schedule, and had been expressly excluded from the work-product assignment. He could not return what Varco did not own. He proposed a joint technical audit to distinguish the prior-invented components from the Varco-specific implementation.

Theodora had rejected the proposal and filed for arbitration the following week.

**E5 (resolution scene).** Arbitrator Chen issues her preliminary finding at the end of the session. She says the prior-inventions disclosure is facially valid and the GitHub timestamps predate employment by a sufficient margin that she will not dismiss the pre-existing-work claim without expert review. She orders a sixty-day technical audit by a jointly agreed expert who will assess the extent to which the Lark model's architecture derives from the prior-invented methodology versus Varco-specific development.

During the audit period, Mikhail will return the trained model weights and training data to Varco in escrow. He will retain a sandboxed copy of the methodology notebooks pending the expert's finding.

Theodora objects: she wanted full return of all artefacts without condition. Chen notes the objection and overrules it. She tells Theodora that an employment agreement's prior-inventions carve-out exists precisely for this situation and that proceeding as if it did not would expose Varco to a counterclaim.

Mikhail's attorney accepts the terms. Mikhail signs the escrow agreement before leaving the building.
