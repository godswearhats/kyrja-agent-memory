# Confrontation arc x Corporate -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## C-arc-Co1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A founding CTO demands that the board reverse its decision to license the company's core algorithm to a strategic partner, arguing the deal will cannibalise their own product within eighteen months. The board chair, who negotiated the license personally, refuses to reopen the vote and insists the CTO execute the integration. The dispute escalates through a failed mediation and ends when the CTO forces a shareholder vote.

> A = the CTO (founding team, board seat, 22% equity); B = the board chair; O = the exclusive license agreement granting partner access to the core algorithm.

**E1 (demand scene).** The boardroom, a Tuesday morning, forty minutes after the quarterly board meeting adjourned. The other directors had left. The CTO closed the door and remained standing.

"I need you to put the Vantage license back on the agenda," she said. "Next meeting. Full board. I want a revote."

The chair set his pen down. "The vote was four to one. Your one. The license was approved, the term sheet is signed, and Vantage's integration team starts onboarding next week."

"The vote was four to one because Mehta and Sondhi didn't see the dependency analysis I sent you on Friday. I know they didn't see it because I asked them this morning and they said they'd never received it."

"I forwarded it Saturday afternoon with my summary."

"Your summary said the risks were manageable. My analysis said the risks were existential. Those are not the same document."

The chair leaned back. "The board reviewed the deal. The board voted. I'm not reopening a closed vote because you don't like the outcome."

"Then I'll request the revote myself. Section 7.2 of the shareholder agreement gives any director with more than fifteen percent equity the right to demand a special session on any board action taken within the prior sixty days. I have twenty-two percent and fifty-three days left."

The chair looked at her for a long moment. "If you invoke 7.2, you're telling every investor on that cap table that the board is split. That's not a revote. That's a signal."

"I know what it is," she said. "Put it on the agenda or I'll file the request with counsel by Friday."

She left. The chair remained seated. He did not reopen the agenda.

**E2 (escalation scene).** The following Thursday. A glass-walled conference room on the fourteenth floor. The company's outside counsel sat at the head of the table. The CTO sat on one side. The chair sat on the other. The CEO -- who held no strong position on the license itself but held the tiebreaking relationship with both parties -- had asked the lawyer to mediate before the Section 7.2 clock expired.

The counsel opened by reading the relevant clause of the license agreement: exclusive access to the algorithm's API for Vantage's enterprise product line, eighteen-month term, automatic renewal unless terminated by supermajority board vote. He asked the CTO to state her objection.

She opened her laptop and turned it toward the table. The dependency analysis showed that Vantage's enterprise product and the company's own mid-market product shared sixty-one percent of their target accounts. Within eighteen months, Vantage would be selling the company's own algorithm against the company's own sales team, at a lower price point, under a brand with three times the distribution.

"This isn't a partnership," she said. "This is a subsidised competitor."

The chair responded that the license fee -- four point two million annually -- represented more than their entire mid-market revenue. Vantage's distribution would validate the algorithm in a market segment they couldn't reach alone. He said the dependency analysis assumed zero growth in their own product, which was the CTO's engineering pessimism, not a forecast.

The counsel asked whether a compromise existed: narrower exclusivity, a shorter term, a revenue-sharing clause.

The CTO said she had proposed all three before the original vote and the chair had rejected each one. The chair said the modifications would have killed the deal. Vantage wanted exclusivity or nothing.

The mediation ended without agreement. The counsel noted the impasse for the record.

**E3 (preparation scene).** The following Monday, the CTO's office. She sat with the company's general counsel -- internal, not the outside mediator -- and reviewed the Section 7.2 filing. The general counsel confirmed the procedure: a written demand to the corporate secretary, copy to all board members and shareholders above the five-percent threshold, specifying the board action to be reconsidered and the basis for reconsideration.

The CTO signed the demand letter. The general counsel filed it electronically with the corporate secretary at 4:17 PM. The system timestamped the filing and distributed it automatically to the fourteen parties on the notification list.

Within the hour, the CTO's phone showed three missed calls from the chair and one from the CEO.

**E4 (authority-involvement scene).** Wednesday morning. The CEO's corner office. The CEO sat behind his desk. The chair sat across from him. The CTO arrived and took the remaining chair.

The CEO spoke first. He said the Section 7.2 filing had triggered exactly the reaction the chair had predicted: two institutional investors had called his office asking whether the board was functional. One had used the word "governance risk." He said he was not taking sides on the license, but he was asking both of them to agree to a structured process before the special session.

The chair said there was nothing to structure. The board had voted. The CTO was using a procedural lever to override a legitimate decision. If every director with enough equity could reopen any vote they lost, the board would never close anything.

The CTO said the board had voted on incomplete information because the chair had summarised her analysis instead of forwarding it. She said the structured process she wanted was simple: present the full dependency analysis to the full board, with Vantage's integration timeline on the table, and let the directors decide with everything visible.

The CEO asked the chair directly: would he agree to a presentation session before the formal revote, with the CTO's analysis distributed unedited to all directors seventy-two hours in advance?

The chair was quiet. Then he said he would agree to the presentation session, but only if the CTO agreed that if the board voted the same way a second time, she would execute the integration without further objection. No third vote. No public dissent.

The CTO said she would agree to that.

The CEO asked both of them to confirm. They did. He asked his assistant to schedule the special session for the following Tuesday and to distribute the CTO's analysis to all directors by end of day Friday.

**E5 (resolution scene).** The following Tuesday, the full boardroom. All five directors present. The CEO sat at the head. The CTO stood at the presentation screen. The chair sat in his usual seat.

The CTO presented the dependency analysis in full. She walked through the account overlap, the pricing differential, the eighteen-month projection. She showed the three compromise structures she had proposed before the original vote: narrower exclusivity, a twelve-month term with no auto-renewal, and a revenue-sharing clause tied to account overlap. She showed the chair's rejection of each.

When she finished, the chair responded. He presented Vantage's commitment letter, the revenue projections, and the distribution analysis. He argued that the overlap risk was real but manageable, and that the license fee funded eighteen months of runway the company did not otherwise have.

The CEO called the vote. Each director voted in turn. Mehta changed her vote -- she cited the account overlap data, which she said she had not seen before the first vote. Sondhi held his original position. The final tally was three to two in favour of renegotiating the license terms: narrower exclusivity, twelve-month term, no automatic renewal.

The chair accepted the result. He noted for the record that the renegotiation might cause Vantage to withdraw entirely, and that if it did, the revenue shortfall was the board's responsibility, not his alone.

The CEO asked the corporate secretary to record the vote and begin the renegotiation process. The meeting adjourned at 11:40 AM.

## C-arc-Co1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect**

**Scenario.** A regional sales director demands that the VP of Product reverse a feature deprecation that will eliminate the integration her largest client depends on. The VP refuses, citing an architectural migration that cannot accommodate the legacy integration. The dispute ends when the CEO rules in the VP's favour but grants the sales director a six-month transition window.

> A = regional sales director; B = VP of Product; O = the deprecated API integration used by the sales director's largest enterprise client.

**E1 (demand scene).** The VP of Product's office, a Wednesday afternoon. The regional sales director had flown in from Dallas that morning, which was itself a statement -- she did not fly for things that could be handled by email, and the VP knew it.

She sat down across from him and placed a single sheet of paper on his desk: the deprecation notice his team had sent to the partner portal the previous Friday, effective in ninety days. She told him she needed him to reverse it. Not delay it, not phase it -- reverse it. Pull the notice, restore the integration to the supported tier, and issue a correction to every partner who had received the original announcement.

The VP had expected this. The integration in question served one client -- her client, Hargrove Industrial, whose annual contract was worth one point eight million dollars and whose renewal was sixty days away. But the integration sat on an authentication layer his team was replacing entirely. Keeping it alive meant maintaining a parallel auth system for a single customer, which meant staffing, which meant budget, which meant a trade-off against the migration his team had spent nine months planning.

He told her he understood the revenue at stake, but the deprecation was not a product decision he could reverse unilaterally. It was an architectural consequence. The old auth layer was being decommissioned. The integration could not exist without it.

She said the architecture was his problem. The client was hers. And the client had been told, by her, six months ago, that this integration would be supported through the next contract cycle. She had made that commitment based on the product roadmap his team had published, which had listed the integration as supported through Q4. The deprecation notice arrived five months after that roadmap.

He said the roadmap had changed when the security audit came back in January. The old auth layer had three unpatched vulnerabilities. Maintaining it was a liability.

She said the liability she was looking at was a one-point-eight-million-dollar client walking to a competitor who would build the integration in sixty days and never deprecate it.

He did not reverse the deprecation. She told him she would escalate to the CEO by end of week. He said that was her right.

**E2 (escalation scene).** Friday morning. The CEO's conference room. The CEO had read both briefs overnight: the VP's architectural assessment and the sales director's revenue-impact analysis. He had asked them both in and allotted thirty minutes.

The sales director went first. She laid out the Hargrove relationship: seven years, four contract renewals, one point eight million current annual value, three point two million in projected expansion if the next renewal closed. She said the deprecation notice had reached Hargrove's IT team before she could brief them, and that Hargrove's CTO had called her personally to ask whether the company was stable. She said that question -- "are you stable" -- was the question that preceded every client departure she had seen in eighteen years of enterprise sales.

The VP went second. He explained the auth migration: the old layer, the vulnerabilities, the engineering cost of maintaining a parallel system. He showed the staffing model -- two engineers full-time for six months, pulled from the migration team, delaying the broader rollout by a quarter.

The CEO asked the VP whether a six-month bridge was technically possible. The VP said it was possible but expensive and inadvisable. The CEO asked the sales director whether six months was enough to migrate Hargrove to the new auth layer. She said she would need Hargrove's CTO to agree, and Hargrove's CTO was not currently inclined to agree to anything.

The thirty minutes passed. The CEO said he would decide by Monday.

**E3 (authority-involvement scene).** Monday morning. An email from the CEO to both parties, copied to the CFO and the CTO. The deprecation stood. The architectural migration would proceed as planned. But the CEO was authorising a six-month transition bridge for Hargrove specifically, funded from the CEO's discretionary budget rather than the product team's allocation, staffed by contractors rather than the migration engineers. The sales director would have six months to migrate Hargrove to the new integration or negotiate an exit. The VP would provide technical support for the migration but would not delay the broader rollout.

The VP read the email in his office and noted that the CEO had given the sales director everything she needed without giving her what she had asked for. The deprecation was not reversed. The integration was not restored. But Hargrove had a runway, and the sales director had something to tell her client's CTO.

The sales director read the email at the gate in Dallas, waiting for her connecting flight. She forwarded it to her account manager with a note: call Hargrove's CTO today, before he reads the deprecation notice again.

**E4 (aftermath scene).** Six weeks later. The sales director's quarterly review, conducted by video from the Dallas office. The CFO sat in. The CEO listened.

She reported that Hargrove's CTO had agreed to the migration timeline after her team flew to Houston and walked his engineering group through the new auth layer in person. The renewal was signed -- same terms, same value, with a clause tying the next expansion to successful completion of the integration migration by September.

She said one more thing. She said the deprecation notice had reached forty-three other partners on the same Friday it reached Hargrove, and that twelve of them had contacted her team with the same question Hargrove's CTO had asked: are you stable? She said the product team's communication process -- a portal announcement on a Friday afternoon with no advance notice to the sales organisation -- had created a relationship problem that the six-month bridge did not solve.

The CEO asked the VP of Product, who was also on the call, whether he wanted to respond. The VP said the communication timing had been a mistake and that his team would coordinate with sales on future deprecation announcements. He did not say more than that.

The CEO closed the item and moved to the next agenda topic.

## C-arc-Co1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (positioning) -> E3 (demand) -> E4 (resistance) -> E5 (failed negotiation) -> E6 (resolution), present tense**

**Scenario.** A startup's lead investor demands that the two co-founders accept a new CEO hired from outside the company, arguing the founders lack the operational experience to scale past Series B. Both co-founders resist, counter-demanding that the board instead approve an operational COO who reports to them. The dispute moves through failed negotiation to a formal board vote.

> A = lead investor (Series B lead, two board seats); B = the two co-founders (joint CEO structure, combined 41% equity); O = the CEO role -- who holds it and under what structure.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** A Thursday afternoon. The co-founders' shared office, the one with the glass wall that looks out over the engineering floor. The engineers have gone home early -- someone sent a Slack message at three o'clock saying the all-hands scheduled for four was cancelled, no explanation. The co-founders sit across from each other at the long table. Between them, a printed copy of the board resolution passed forty minutes ago.

Priya reads it aloud, though they have both already read it: the board, by a vote of three to two, declines the investor's motion to appoint an outside CEO. The co-founders will retain the joint-CEO structure. The board approves the hire of a Chief Operating Officer, reporting to the co-founders, with a search to begin immediately. The investor's two board seats are unchanged. The resolution is effective immediately.

"He'll call within the hour," Tomasz says.

"Let him call," Priya says. She folds the resolution and places it in the desk drawer where they keep the original articles of incorporation. The drawer is getting full.

**E2 (positioning scene).** Three weeks earlier. A Saturday morning, the co-founders' apartment -- they are still roommates, a fact the investor's operating partner mentioned at the last board dinner as evidence of immaturity, though he framed it as charm.

Priya opens her laptop and shows Tomasz the email that arrived at 11 PM the previous night. It is from the investor's managing partner, addressed to both co-founders and both independent board members. The subject line reads "Leadership Structure -- Confidential." The body is three paragraphs. The first paragraph congratulates them on the Series B metrics. The second paragraph notes that the company's headcount has tripled in nine months and that operational complexity is outpacing the founders' management experience. The third paragraph proposes that the board approve the appointment of an outside CEO with public-company operating experience, with both co-founders transitioning to President and CTO respectively.

Tomasz reads the email twice. He asks Priya whether she knew this was coming. She did not. She says the managing partner sat next to her at the board dinner two weeks ago and said nothing about leadership changes. He talked about his daughter's lacrosse season.

They spend the morning reviewing the shareholder agreement, the board composition, and the voting mechanics. The board has five seats: the investor holds two, the co-founders hold two, and the fifth is an independent director they all agreed on during the Series A. The independent, a woman named Adesanya who runs a mid-market SaaS company in Chicago, has not responded to the email.

Priya calls Adesanya that afternoon. The call lasts forty minutes.

**E3 (demand scene).** The following Tuesday. A video call. The managing partner is in his office in San Francisco; the co-founders are in their conference room; the two independent board members -- Adesanya and a second independent named Rourke -- are on separate feeds.

The managing partner makes the demand formally. He says the company needs an outside CEO. He names a candidate his firm has already approached: a woman who scaled a logistics company from Series B to IPO in four years. He says the co-founders would retain their board seats, their equity, and significant operational roles. He says this is not a demotion. It is a recognition that the next phase requires a different skill set.

Priya responds. She says the co-founders will not accept an outside CEO. She says the company's growth metrics -- the metrics the managing partner cited in his email -- were produced under their leadership, not despite it. She says the operational gaps are real, and that the co-founders propose a different structure: a COO hire, reporting to the co-founders, with a board-approved mandate covering operations, finance, and HR. The co-founders retain the CEO role jointly. The COO handles the scaling complexity the investor is worried about.

The managing partner says a COO who reports to the co-founders is not an independent check on the co-founders. It is an employee. He says the board needs a CEO who can make decisions the founders might not want to make -- headcount reductions, market pivots, saying no to the founders' own product instincts when the numbers don't support them.

Priya says the founders' product instincts built the numbers he is citing. The call ends without resolution. The managing partner says he will put the CEO appointment on the formal board agenda for the next meeting.

**E4 (resistance scene).** The following Friday. A phone call between the managing partner and Tomasz. The managing partner has called Tomasz directly, without Priya.

He tells Tomasz that he respects what both founders have built, but that Priya's rigidity on this issue is putting the company at risk. He says he has seen this pattern before: founders who cannot distinguish between their identity and the company's needs. He says Tomasz is the more operationally minded of the two. He asks whether Tomasz would consider stepping into the sole CEO role, with Priya as CTO, as a compromise that keeps leadership in-house.

Tomasz is quiet for a moment. Then he tells the managing partner that he and Priya make decisions together, that the managing partner's decision to call him separately is noted, and that splitting the co-founders is not a path the managing partner should pursue. He says the COO proposal stands. He says if the managing partner wants to bring the CEO motion to a board vote, the co-founders will vote against it and they will make their case to Adesanya and Rourke before the meeting.

The managing partner says he understands. He says the motion will be on the agenda.

Tomasz calls Priya immediately after. He tells her everything.

**E5 (failed negotiation scene).** The following Wednesday. The managing partner's office in San Francisco. Priya has flown out alone. The meeting is in person, at the managing partner's suggestion, without other board members present.

The managing partner offers a modified proposal. The outside CEO candidate would serve as Executive Chair rather than CEO. The co-founders retain the CEO title jointly. The Executive Chair would have authority over board-level strategy and investor relations but would not have operational authority over the co-founders' direct reports. He says this preserves the founders' autonomy while giving the board the independent oversight it needs.

Priya considers it. She asks who the Executive Chair would report to. The managing partner says the board. Priya asks whether the Executive Chair would have hiring or firing authority over any company employee. The managing partner says the role would include authority over VP-level hires and above, subject to board approval.

Priya says that means the Executive Chair could block any VP hire the co-founders want and approve any VP hire the co-founders don't want, subject only to a board where the investor holds two of five seats. She says the proposal gives the investor operational veto power through a proxy, which is what the original CEO proposal did with a different title.

The managing partner says she is reading the proposal adversarially. Priya says she is reading it accurately. She declines the modified proposal and reiterates the COO structure. The managing partner walks her to the elevator. Neither of them mentions the board vote.

**E6 (resolution scene).** The board meeting, the following Tuesday. All five directors present by video. The corporate secretary reads the two motions: first, the investor's motion to appoint an outside CEO; second, the co-founders' motion to approve a COO hire reporting to the co-founders.

The managing partner speaks to his motion. He presents the candidate's credentials, the scaling precedent, and the governance rationale. He does not mention the Executive Chair compromise.

Priya speaks to the co-founders' motion. She presents the COO job description, the reporting structure, and a list of three candidates the co-founders have already identified through their own network. She presents the growth metrics, the retention numbers, and the customer NPS scores, all produced under the current leadership structure.

Adesanya speaks. She says she has spoken with both parties separately over the past two weeks. She says the company's operational gaps are real but do not require removing the founders from the CEO role. She says she will vote for the COO structure.

Rourke speaks. He says he agrees with Adesanya.

The vote on the investor's motion: two in favour (the investor's two seats), three against (both co-founders and Adesanya). Motion fails.

The vote on the co-founders' motion: three in favour (both co-founders and Adesanya), one against (the managing partner's seat), one abstention (Rourke, who says he supports the COO concept but wants to review the candidate list before voting yes). Motion passes with a three-to-one majority.

The corporate secretary records both votes. The managing partner thanks the board and disconnects. The co-founders sit in their conference room after the other feeds go dark. Priya closes her laptop.

"He'll call within the hour," Tomasz says.

## C-arc-Co1-4

**5 beats, chronological, embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A departing partner at a consulting firm demands the return of her client book -- twelve accounts she originated over nine years -- arguing the partnership agreement entitles her to portable clients upon departure. The managing partner refuses, citing a non-solicitation clause. The dispute escalates through competing legal interpretations and is resolved by the firm's arbitration panel.

> A = the departing partner; B = the managing partner (on behalf of the firm); O = the client book -- twelve originated accounts and the right to solicit them post-departure.

**E1 (demand scene -- embedded document).** The managing partner received the letter at 8:15 AM on a Monday, hand-delivered by the departing partner's assistant. He read it standing at his desk:

> *Dear Martin,*
>
> *This letter constitutes formal notice of my departure from the firm, effective sixty days from today's date, and a concurrent demand under Section 11.3 of the Partnership Agreement for the release of my originated client accounts.*
>
> *Section 11.3 provides that any departing partner who originated a client relationship -- defined as the partner who brought the client to the firm and served as primary relationship holder for a continuous period of no fewer than thirty-six months -- may, upon departure, notify those clients of her departure and offer continued service through her new practice. The section requires only that the departing partner provide the firm with sixty days' written notice and a complete list of the accounts she intends to solicit.*
>
> *Attached is the list: twelve accounts, each of which I originated, each of which I have served as primary relationship holder for no fewer than five years, and none of which were acquired through firm-wide business development or institutional referral. These are my clients. I built these relationships before several of the current partners joined the firm.*
>
> *I expect the firm to honour Section 11.3 without modification. I am available to discuss transition logistics at your convenience.*
>
> *Regards,*
> *Catherine*

The managing partner read the letter twice, then asked his assistant to pull the Partnership Agreement and schedule a call with outside counsel for that afternoon.

**E2 (resistance scene -- embedded document).** The departing partner received the firm's response three days later. It was on firm letterhead, signed by the managing partner, and copied to all equity partners:

> *Dear Catherine,*
>
> *We acknowledge your notice of departure and wish you well in your next chapter.*
>
> *Regarding your demand under Section 11.3: the firm declines to release the accounts listed in your letter. Section 11.3 is subject to Section 14.1, the non-solicitation clause, which provides that no departing partner may solicit, contact, or otherwise divert any client of the firm for a period of twelve months following departure. Section 14.1 was added by partnership vote in 2021 and applies to all partners regardless of origination status.*
>
> *Our outside counsel has reviewed both sections and confirms that 14.1 supersedes 11.3 in any case of conflict. The accounts you listed are firm clients. They will be reassigned upon your departure according to the standard succession protocol.*
>
> *We are happy to discuss a cooperative transition that serves the clients' interests and respects the firm's contractual rights.*
>
> *Regards,*
> *Martin*

The departing partner read the letter in her office, forwarded it to her own attorney, and drafted no reply that day.

**E3 (escalation scene).** Two weeks later. The firm's main conference room. The departing partner sat on one side of the table with her attorney. The managing partner sat on the other side with the firm's outside counsel. The firm's general counsel sat at the head, nominally mediating.

The departing partner's attorney opened. He argued that Section 11.3 was specific -- it addressed origination rights explicitly and predated Section 14.1 by eight years. Section 14.1, the non-solicitation clause, was general -- it addressed all client contact by departing partners. Under standard contract interpretation, a specific clause controlled over a general clause when both could apply. The non-solicitation clause was never intended to nullify origination rights; it was added to prevent departing partners from poaching institutional clients they had not originated.

The firm's outside counsel responded. She argued that Section 14.1 was not general -- it was a later-enacted, comprehensive restriction that the partnership had adopted by supermajority vote precisely because Section 11.3 had proven too permissive. Three partners had departed in 2019 and 2020 and taken a combined thirty-one accounts under 11.3, costing the firm fourteen million dollars in annual revenue. Section 14.1 was the partnership's response. It was adopted with full knowledge of 11.3, and its language -- "no departing partner may solicit, contact, or otherwise divert any client of the firm" -- contained no exception for originated accounts.

The departing partner spoke. She said she had voted against Section 14.1 in 2021 and had stated on the record at the time that it conflicted with 11.3. She said the minutes of the partnership meeting would show that. She asked the general counsel to pull them.

The general counsel said she would pull the minutes but noted that the departing partner's objection at the time did not change the vote. Section 14.1 passed by supermajority.

The managing partner spoke for the first time. He said the firm valued Catherine's nine years and her client relationships. He said the firm would offer an enhanced severance package -- eighteen months of draw rather than the standard six -- in exchange for her agreement to the twelve-month non-solicitation period. He said this was a generous offer, and he recommended she consider it.

The departing partner declined. She said she did not want severance. She wanted her clients. She asked the general counsel to invoke the partnership agreement's arbitration clause -- Section 19 -- and refer the dispute to the firm's three-member arbitration panel.

The general counsel confirmed that either party could invoke Section 19. The managing partner did not object. The arbitration was scheduled for the following month.

**E4 (authority-involvement scene -- embedded document).** The arbitration panel convened four weeks later in the firm's largest conference room. The panel consisted of three retired partners, selected per Section 19's protocol: one chosen by the departing partner, one by the managing partner, one by mutual agreement.

Both sides presented written briefs. The hearing lasted one day. At the end, the panel retired for two hours, then delivered its written ruling:

> *In the matter of Partner C. Aldworth's demand for release of originated client accounts under Section 11.3 of the Partnership Agreement, and the firm's refusal under Section 14.1, this panel rules as follows:*
>
> *1. Section 11.3 and Section 14.1 are in conflict as applied to originated accounts. The Partnership Agreement contains no reconciliation clause.*
>
> *2. This panel declines to adopt either party's interpretive framework in full. The specific-controls-general canon favoured by the departing partner is persuasive but not dispositive, because Section 14.1 was enacted with knowledge of Section 11.3 and with the evident purpose of restricting the very conduct Section 11.3 permits.*
>
> *3. However, the firm's position -- that 14.1 entirely nullifies 11.3 -- renders 11.3 a dead letter, which this panel is unwilling to endorse absent explicit repeal language, which is absent from the agreement.*
>
> *4. This panel therefore rules that the departing partner may notify the twelve originated clients of her departure and her new practice, but may not actively solicit their business for a period of six months following departure. The clients may contact the departing partner of their own volition during this period, and any such client-initiated engagement is not a violation of Section 14.1. After six months, the non-solicitation restriction expires and the departing partner may solicit freely.*
>
> *5. The firm's offer of enhanced severance is noted but is not relevant to this ruling.*
>
> *This ruling is final and binding under Section 19.*

**E5 (aftermath scene).** The following Monday. The managing partner's office. He sat at his desk reviewing the transition plan his operations team had drafted overnight. The departing partner's twelve accounts would be notified of her departure by the firm, per the arbitration ruling, in a letter co-signed by the managing partner and the departing partner. The letter would state that the departing partner was establishing a new practice and that clients could contact her directly if they wished.

His assistant knocked. The firm's general counsel was on the line.

He picked up. The general counsel told him that three of the twelve clients had already called the departing partner's mobile that morning -- before the notification letters went out. She said the departing partner had not solicited them. The clients had seen her updated LinkedIn profile, which listed her new firm and its address. The profile did not mention any of the twelve accounts by name. It did not reference the firm at all.

The managing partner asked whether the LinkedIn update violated the arbitration ruling. The general counsel said it did not. The ruling prohibited solicitation, not existence. The departing partner was permitted to have a public profile, and clients were permitted to read it.

He thanked the general counsel and hung up. He looked at the transition plan on his desk. Three of twelve accounts had already moved, and the notification letters had not yet been sent. He picked up his pen, crossed out the projected retention estimate, and wrote a lower number.
