## C-arc-Fa3-01

**Scenario.** A master artificer of the Copperwright Guild is ordered by the Guild Warden to dismantle an automaton she built outside the Guild's sanctioned commission list. She refuses, claiming the automaton qualifies as a lawful apprentice-piece under an obsolete charter clause. The dispute is brought before the Guild Council.

> A = Guild Warden (Aldric Vestmoor); B = Master Artificer (Tessaly Orvaine); O = a self-directing brass automaton named "Hetch," constructed by Tessaly over two years without a commission charter, capable of independent locomotion, object manipulation, and rudimentary spoken instruction

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (positioning scene).** The Guild hall smelled of solder and old oil, and Tessaly had brought Hetch to the quarterly inspection because the charter required her to present all active projects to the Warden's review. She had prepared a nineteen-page technical ledger documenting every component, every hour of labour, and the applicable charter clause under which she claimed the work was lawful: the Apprentice-Piece Provision of the Founding Charter, Article Eleven, subsection four, which permitted a master artificer to construct one unchartered work per decade for the purposes of demonstrating craft advancement.

Aldric reviewed the ledger for eleven minutes, then looked at Hetch, then looked at Tessaly.

"This is not a demonstration piece," he said. "It moves. It speaks. It takes instructions. Article Eleven covers static masterworks."

"Article Eleven does not specify static," she said. "It specifies 'unchartered.' Hetch is unchartered."

He closed the ledger. "I'll need to take this to the council."

"Of course," she said. She did not dismantle Hetch in the interim.

**E2 (demand scene).** The Warden's office, three days later. He had the Founding Charter open on his desk to the page he wanted, and he stood rather than sat, which was how he conducted formal proceedings.

"I am directing you to dismantle the automaton," he said. "Within seven days. The moving components are to be catalogued and surrendered to the Guild vault. The instruction cores are to be destroyed."

"On what authority?" she said.

"The Charter's proscription on unlicensed autonomous mechanisms. Article Twenty-Two, subsection one: no mechanism capable of acting on verbal instruction without the continuous oversight of a licensed artificer may be operated within Guild jurisdiction without a Class Three Commission Seal."

"Hetch requires my presence to operate beyond the second level of instruction. That is continuous oversight."

"The inspectors' report says he crossed the workshop unaccompanied and retrieved a tool from the upper shelf when asked by the apprentice. You were in the adjacent room."

She was quiet for a moment. "The adjacent room is eight feet away."

"Tessaly. I need your confirmation that you will dismantle the mechanism within seven days."

She looked at him steadily. "I will not dismantle Hetch."

**E3 (resistance scene).** She spent the following two days in the Guild archive. The Founding Charter was four hundred years old, and no one had read Article Eleven in living memory because the Apprentice-Piece Provision had been invoked only twice. She found both prior invocations and read the council records for each. In the second case, from a hundred and nine years ago, the piece had been a clockwork model of the night sky with moving parts, and the council had accepted it under Article Eleven without dispute.

She also found what she was looking for in the supplementary rolls: the Artificers' Clarification of Year 213, a secondary document that had been incorporated by reference into the Charter but was not typically bound with it, which defined "static" works and "kinetic" works for the purposes of Guild classification. The Clarification explicitly excluded apprentice-pieces from the kinetic licensing requirement provided the work was registered with the Warden within twelve months of completion.

Hetch was two years old. She had not registered him.

She copied the Clarification and brought it to the Warden's office.

**E4 (authority involvement scene).** She laid the Clarification on his desk and pointed to the relevant passage. He read it twice.

"You didn't register him," he said.

"No. I didn't know about the registration requirement. The Clarification isn't in the main Charter volume."

"That's not a defence."

"It may be. If the requirement was effectively hidden, the council may find it unenforceable against a good-faith constructor."

He studied the document for a long time. "I'm going to put this before the full council," he said. "Not because I think you're right, but because I don't want to be the one who sets the precedent either way."

She nodded. "I'll prepare a submission."

"You have three days. And Tessaly — Hetch stays in your workshop. He doesn't leave it, and he doesn't operate beyond arm's reach of a master, until the council rules."

She accepted the condition.

**E5 (resolution scene).** The council session lasted most of a morning. Three of the seven councillors were sympathetic to her argument about the hidden registration requirement; two held that ignorance of a charter document was not a lawful excuse; two abstained pending a fuller review of the Year 213 Clarification's status as binding versus advisory.

The ruling was a partial resolution. Hetch would not be dismantled. However, Tessaly was required to apply for a Class Three Commission Seal within thirty days, paying the full retrospective licensing fee. The Clarification's registration requirement was declared operative but the council found that its practical obscurity warranted a one-time good-faith exemption. The decision was entered into the rolls as a precedent for kinetic apprentice-pieces going forward.

Aldric presented the ruling without visible satisfaction. Tessaly thanked the council and left without speaking to him directly. She had Hetch registered within the week.

## C-arc-Fa3-02

**Scenario.** The high priest of a river-god's temple demands that a river-clan chieftain yield the clan's ancestral fishing weirs, which the temple claims are on consecrated water. The chieftain refuses and invokes a pre-temple grant from the crown. The dispute escalates when the temple blocks the river with a ritual barge, and the clan appeals to the regional governor.

> A = High Priest Sorvan of the Temple of the Drowned God; B = Chieftain Maren Thael of the Greywing Clan; O = the fishing weirs at the Threefork confluence, constructed by the Greywing Clan six generations ago, now claimed by the temple as within the bounds of consecrated water under a recent high-priestly proclamation

4 beats, dialogue-driven, chronological

**E1 (demand scene).** The temple steps, midday. The high priest had come down to the water himself, which was unusual, and Maren had come upriver from the weirs with two of her warden-kin because she had been told it would be a greeting, not a proceeding.

It was a proceeding.

"Chieftain." Sorvan's voice carried well. "The Threefork confluence falls within the consecrated bounds as measured from the Offering Stone to the eastern fork. The temple requires you to remove your clan's structures from the consecrated water and cease all harvesting within those bounds."

"High Priest." She matched his register. "The Greywing weirs have stood since before this temple was raised. My clan's right to the Threefork waters is recorded in a crown grant of the third Harrowmont reign. No priestly proclamation issued without the crown's co-seal supersedes a crown grant."

"The consecration of the Threefork was proclaimed by the council of temple elders and confirmed by the First Augury. It does not require a crown co-seal."

"Then we are at a disagreement about precedence. The clan will not remove its weirs."

"I advise you to reconsider, Chieftain. The god's waters must be kept."

"And our people must eat," she said. "I'll hear a lawful order from the governor's seat, or I won't hear one at all."

She turned and walked back to the river. He did not call after her.

**E2 (escalation scene).** The temple moored the ritual barge at the entrance to the Threefork confluence three mornings later. The barge was anchored mid-channel, garlanded with river-rush and bearing two novice priests in white. It was not physically large enough to block the full channel, but it sat directly across the approach to the main weir and any fishing craft drawing more than three feet of water would have to thread between the barge and the shallows to reach it.

Maren did not attempt to push through on the first day. She counted the fish that were not caught and sent a rider to the governor's seat at Carrwater, carrying a writ of complaint and a copy of the Harrowmont grant. The rider carried also a private letter she had written to the governor's steward, who had been the Greywing Clan's ally in three previous boundary disputes and who understood what a blocked weir meant for winter provisions.

She did not speak to the priests on the barge.

**E3 (positioning scene).** Four days after the barge was moored, the governor's steward arrived at the temple to serve a preliminary injunction: the barge was to be moved pending a formal hearing before the governor's court, the Greywing Clan was to have immediate access to their weirs, and both parties were to present their claims to the governor within twelve days.

The high priest refused to move the barge. He told the steward that the governor's court had no jurisdiction over consecrated water and that the injunction was void ab initio.

The steward told him the injunction had been issued under the civil compact of the third reign — the same reign that had issued the Greywing grant — which explicitly preserved the governor's authority over waterway access disputes regardless of religious designation. He told Sorvan that if the barge was not moved within twenty-four hours, the governor would authorise the river wardens to clear the channel by force and the temple would bear the costs.

The barge was moved before morning.

**E4 (resolution scene).** The governor's hall at Carrwater. The high priest presented first, citing the council proclamation and the augury record. Maren presented the Harrowmont grant, the survey records showing the weirs predating the temple's founding, and a map her warden-kin had drawn marking the claimed consecrated boundary against the actual Offering Stone measurement, which showed the boundary as the temple had drawn it was significantly wider than the augury's own recorded language supported.

The governor — a careful, unspectacular administrator named Revith Calarn, who had held the seat for twenty-two years by avoiding exactly these disputes — read both submissions twice before he spoke.

He ruled that the crown grant was prior in time and law, and that it stood. He noted that the temple had the right to petition the crown directly to modify or supersede the grant, but that no proclamation issued by the temple alone had that authority. The Greywing weirs would remain.

He added, looking at Sorvan: "High Priest, the barge."

Sorvan straightened. "The barge was a ritual observance."

"It was a blockade," Calarn said, without inflection. "The temple will make good the clan's loss of seven days' harvest, valued by the market warden's current rate. That sum will be transferred to Chieftain Thael within thirty days."

Neither party spoke. The governor's clerk noted the terms in the record. The session closed.

## C-arc-Fa3-03

**Scenario.** A grand inquisitor of a mage-hunters' order demands that a city's Arcanist Guild surrender a member accused of unlicensed working of the memory arts. The Guild's Grandmaster refuses to yield him without a trial of examination conducted under Guild law, not Inquisitor law. The king is petitioned and rules on the jurisdictional question.

> A = Grand Inquisitor Aldith Crane of the Order of the Bound Eye; B = Grandmaster Severin Mow of the Arcanist Guild of Vethis; O = Master Arcanist Torvald Luce, accused of performing memory-reading without a crown licence, a capital offence under the Inquisitors' Compact; the Guild's charter grants it the right of internal examination for members accused of craft violations before surrendering them to external bodies

6 beats, free indirect style, chronological

**E1 (positioning scene).** Severin had known the Inquisitors would come eventually. The reports of Torvald's work had been circulating in the Guild's closed correspondence for two months, and Severin had convened three private hearings without concluding that a violation had occurred, partly because the evidence was ambiguous and partly because he suspected the principal witness was lying about the nature of what he'd experienced. Memory-reading left traces a trained arcanist could detect. Severin had examined Torvald himself and had found nothing of the kind.

None of this would matter to Aldith Crane, he understood. What mattered to her was the accusation, which was formal and sworn, and the Compact, which gave her the right to demand.

He spent the night before her arrival reviewing the Guild charter, specifically the chapter that had been written into law precisely to prevent the Order from conducting its own examinations on Guild members. The chapter was old. It had been inserted at a time when the Order's examinations had a poor record of accuracy and a notable record of ending in fire. Severin thought it remained a reasonable precaution.

He had the charter on his desk when she arrived the next morning.

**E2 (demand scene).** She came with four Inquisitors and a crown writ, which she placed on his desk without preamble. The writ authorised her to take custody of Torvald Luce for examination under the Compact. She told Severin she was here to collect him.

Severin read the writ carefully and then set it aside and opened the Guild charter to the relevant chapter. He read the first clause to her: a Guild member accused of a craft violation could not be surrendered to any external body before the completion of a formal Guild examination, conducted by a panel of three masters, which would determine whether the accusation had merit sufficient to warrant transfer of jurisdiction.

She said the crown writ superseded Guild procedural rules.

He said the Guild charter was a crown document, issued under the seal of the second Vethian monarch, and that it had not been revoked by any subsequent instrument. A crown writ could not, in law, supersede a prior crown charter without explicit revocation language, which this writ lacked.

She said she would return with stronger authority.

He said she was welcome to do so, and that in the meantime Torvald Luce remained under Guild protection.

**E3 (escalation scene).** She returned four days later with a second writ, this one countersigned by the Lord Chancellor, which included a specific clause invoking the Compact's override provision — a clause that allowed the Order to bypass subsidiary examination requirements in cases where imminent public danger was asserted.

Severin read the new writ. The imminent-public-danger clause was real; he had known about it and had hoped she would not think to use it. But the clause required an accompanying sworn declaration from a senior crown official attesting to specific documented evidence of public danger, and the Lord Chancellor's countersignature was not sufficient on its own — the Compact required the declaration to identify the nature of the danger with particularity.

The new writ contained no such declaration. It contained only the Chancellor's seal and the clause citation.

He told her the invocation was procedurally deficient. He told her that if she filed a complete invocation — with the required declaration, the particularity requirement satisfied — he would review it and respond in good faith.

She looked at him for a long moment. She was not, he noted, a person who was accustomed to being told the procedure was wrong. She took the writ back from his hands and left.

**E4 (authority involvement scene).** The petition to the king was filed jointly — by the Order on one side, by the Guild on the other, each seeking a definitive ruling on which body held primary jurisdiction over accused Guild members under the current legal instruments. It was the kind of dispute the crown generally preferred to avoid, and the king's chamberlain sent word that the matter would be heard in the king's own court in thirty days.

Severin spent those thirty days preparing his submission. He was meticulous. He arranged the charter, the Compact, the succession of writs, and the procedural history in a single document with marginal citations and cross-references. He had three senior Guild masters review it for accuracy. He sent a courtesy copy to Aldith Crane's office, which she did not acknowledge.

**E5 (hearing scene).** The king's court was not a comfortable room and the king himself, a young man who had been on the throne for six years, had the look of someone who had been briefed on both sides of the dispute and found both sides irritating.

Aldith presented first. The Order's position was that the Compact, as a security instrument of the crown, necessarily took precedence over any guild charter when public safety was concerned, and that the procedural requirements Severin was citing were administrative mechanisms that could not be used as shields for accused practitioners.

Severin presented second. He noted that the procedural requirements she was calling administrative shields had been written into law by her Order's predecessors to correct a documented history of wrongful examinations, and that weakening them required a reasoned legal case, not the assertion that they were inconvenient.

He placed the particular document on the king's reading table: a roll from the Order's own archive, dated seventy years earlier, listing twenty-three names of Guild members who had died in Order examinations and been subsequently vindicated by independent magical audit. He had obtained it through the Guild's correspondence with the crown archivist. He had not mentioned it in any prior proceeding.

The king read it.

**E6 (resolution scene).** The king's ruling was issued the following morning. Guild members accused of craft violations would be examined first by a Guild panel; the Guild panel's finding would be delivered to the Order within thirty days; if the panel found merit, the member would be transferred to Order jurisdiction for further examination. If the panel found no merit, the Order could appeal the finding to a joint tribunal of three crown-appointed judges, two masters, and one Inquisitor.

Torvald Luce would be examined under the new procedure. The Guild examination panel would convene within seven days.

Aldith Crane received the ruling in her office and said nothing when the courier read it. Severin received it in the Guild hall and read it twice, then set it in the charter folio alongside the original charter, the Compact, and both writs. He noted the date in the margin. He thought it would be useful, eventually, to have all of this in one place.

Torvald Luce's examination found no evidence of unlicensed memory-reading. The Order did not appeal.

## C-arc-Fa3-04

**Scenario.** A clan-elder demands that a hedge-witch return a naming-stone she holds as surety for an unpaid debt owed by the elder's dead son. The hedge-witch refuses, arguing the stone belongs to her by right of the dead man's last word before his binding-oath creditors are paid. The village judgment-keeper is called to rule.

> A = Clan-Elder Brecht Dunlow; B = Hedge-Witch Sable Marsh; O = the Dunlow naming-stone, a carved hearthstone bearing the clan's lineage-mark, which Brecht's son Hale pledged as surety for a healing debt of twelve silver-weight before his death; the naming-stone carries inheritance and legitimacy significance beyond its material value

4 beats, present tense, chronological

**E1 (demand scene).** Brecht comes to Sable's door in the morning, before the dew is off the thatch, and does not knock. He opens it and steps in and sets both hands on her worktable, and Sable is grinding dried bark at the far end of it and does not step back.

"Give me the stone," he says. "My son is dead. The debt dies with him. The clan's stone comes back to the clan."

"The debt doesn't die," she says. "Hale pledged the stone as surety. A surety pledge survives the debtor. You know this."

"You treated him for a fever. He would have lived without you. The stone is worth forty times what you charged."

"He would have lost the hand," she says, and keeps grinding. "And then he would have lost the rest of him, slower. The stone stays until the debt is paid from the estate, in coin or in kind, as we agreed."

"There is no estate." His voice is flat. "He owed more than he owned. The naming-stone is the only thing left. Give it back."

"I won't," she says. "Come back with the judgment-keeper if you want a ruling. Come back alone and I'll close the door."

He looks at her for a long moment, then turns and walks out. She listens to his footsteps on the path until she can't hear them.

**E2 (preparation scene).** Sable spends the afternoon locating the pledge-marks. Hale had made the surety in front of two witnesses — the miller's wife and the smith — and had pressed his thumbprint into the pledge-wax alongside Sable's own mark. She finds the wax seal in her record-box, intact, the thumbprint still clear. She finds also the old law-scroll her predecessor left her, the one with the surety provisions copied out in a careful hand, and she reads the relevant section twice to make sure she has it right.

A surety pledge made before witnesses survives the debtor's death. The pledged object passes to the creditor in satisfaction of the debt if the estate cannot pay. The pledgor's blood-kin have no recourse against a lawfully made surety unless the surety was obtained by coercion or fraud, and the burden of proving coercion falls on the kin, not the creditor.

She wraps the wax seal in clean linen and sets it on the table. Then she grinds more bark, because the work still needs doing.

**E3 (resistance escalation scene).** Brecht returns the next morning with his two eldest sons and without the judgment-keeper. He tells Sable that the naming-stone is a clan heirloom under clan law, and clan law holds that a heirloom cannot be pledged as surety for a personal debt without the elder's countersign.

Sable asks him to show her where that is written.

He says it is spoken law, passed down through the elder's holding.

She tells him that spoken clan law does not override witnessed contract law in a matter between a clansman and an outsider, and that if his clan's internal rules prevented Hale from pledging the stone, that was a matter between Brecht and Hale's memory, not between Brecht and her.

One of the sons takes a step toward her worktable. She picks up the grinding-stone and holds it in one hand without looking at him, and he stops.

"Bring the judgment-keeper," she says to Brecht. "Or I'll send for him myself, and I'll tell him you came here twice without him."

**E4 (resolution scene).** The judgment-keeper is an old woman named Farrow who has held the village post for thirty-one years and who arrives at Sable's threshold that afternoon with a three-legged stool, which she sets down between the two parties and sits on as if she is entirely comfortable.

She hears Brecht first. He presents the clan-law argument: heirlooms cannot be pledged without the elder's countersign. He has no written record of the law, only his word that it exists.

Farrow hears Sable next. Sable presents the wax pledge-seal, the two witness names, and the law-scroll section. She is specific about each one.

Farrow asks Brecht whether any elder or clan record can confirm the heirloom-pledge rule. He says he can bring two clansmen who know the rule. She says clansmen's testimony about their own clan's internal rules, unsupported by any written record, cannot override a witnessed contract with an outsider. She says if his clan wants to enforce such a rule in future, they should write it down and have the regional magistrate enter it in the common rolls.

"The stone stays with Marsh," Farrow says, "until the debt is satisfied from the estate. If the estate cannot satisfy it, the stone is hers in settlement." She looks at Brecht with the particular patience of someone who has said this kind of thing many times before. "Your son made the pledge. That was his to make."

Brecht stands for a moment, then turns and leaves without speaking. His sons follow. Farrow folds her stool and tucks it under her arm.

"Keep the seal," she tells Sable. "He'll be back, and you'll want to show it again."

Sable sets the kettle on the fire and agrees.
