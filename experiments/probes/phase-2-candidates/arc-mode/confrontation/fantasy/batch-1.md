# Confrontation arc x Fantasy -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## C-arc-Fa1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** The head of a guild of cartographers demands that a border lord return a set of master survey charts his predecessor stole from the guild archive during a succession dispute. The border lord refuses, claiming the charts were payment for a debt the guild still owes his house. The dispute escalates through a failed negotiation at a trade council and is resolved when the guild invokes an ancient charter right to appeal to the High Seneschal.

> A = the guild master of the Cartographers' Guild; B = Lord Edren of Thornwall, the border lord; O = the master survey charts -- seven original vellum maps of the northern passes, irreplaceable and essential to the guild's monopoly on route licensing.

**E1 (demand scene).** The guild hall in Carrend, a wet morning in late autumn. The guild master stood at the head of the long table with the inventory ledger open to the page that mattered -- the page listing seven master survey charts, their dates of creation, and the notation added in red ink eighteen months ago: *removed by Lord Aldren of Thornwall during succession dispute; not returned*.

Lord Edren -- Aldren's son, who had inherited the title and, it seemed, the charts along with it -- sat across the table with one retainer and no expression.

"I'll be direct," the guild master said. "Your father took seven charts from this archive. They are guild property. They have been guild property for ninety years. I am asking you to return them."

"My father didn't take them," Edren said. "He accepted them."

"He walked into this hall during a guild election, while our membership was divided, and removed them from the archive room. The archivist's log shows the date. The watch register shows his men carrying a sealed case through the south gate."

"The archivist at the time was Brennick, who owed my father a debt of forty gold marks for the lease on his farmstead. Brennick offered the charts as settlement. My father accepted. The debt was cleared. If your guild's internal governance allowed an archivist to offer guild property as personal surety, that's a failure of your bylaws, not a theft by my house."

The guild master closed the ledger. "The charts are guild property, Lord Edren. An archivist cannot convey what he does not own. I am not here to litigate Brennick's debts. I am here to request the return of stolen goods."

"And I am here to tell you that the charts are in Thornwall's archive, that they will remain there, and that if you want to dispute the transfer, you're welcome to bring the matter before the trade council at the winter session. I'll answer any claim you file."

"Then I'll file it," the guild master said.

Edren stood, collected his retainer with a glance, and left through the south door. The guild master remained at the table. He reopened the ledger and wrote beneath the red notation: *Formal demand made. Refused. Trade council claim to follow.*

**E2 (positioning scene).** Three weeks later. The guild master's private study, evening. The guild's advocate -- a woman named Halvorsen who had argued three previous property disputes before the trade council -- sat across from him with the case file.

She laid out the guild's position: the charts were created by guild members, on guild commission, using guild materials. They had been registered in the guild archive for ninety years. No instrument of transfer existed -- no deed, no bill of sale, no council-witnessed conveyance. Brennick's alleged debt settlement was unilateral. The guild's bylaws did not grant an archivist the authority to alienate guild property, and the guild had never ratified the transfer.

The guild master asked about Edren's position. Halvorsen said it was weaker than it looked. Edren would argue that the guild had waited eighteen months to make a formal demand, which implied acquiescence. He would argue that Brennick's authority as archivist gave him apparent power to dispose of archive materials. And he would argue that the forty-gold-mark debt was real, documented, and now settled -- meaning the guild owed his house restitution if the charts were returned.

The guild master asked what the trade council would likely do. Halvorsen said the council hated property disputes between guilds and lords. They would try to broker a compromise: return three charts, keep four, or return all seven with a compensating payment for Brennick's debt. She said the guild should decide in advance which compromises it could accept and which it could not.

The guild master said the guild could accept no compromise that left any chart in Thornwall's archive. The charts were the guild's working surveys of the northern passes. Without them, any competitor could commission new surveys and break the guild's route-licensing monopoly. The charts were not valuable -- they were structural.

Halvorsen noted this. She began drafting the claim.

**E3 (failed-negotiation scene).** The winter trade council, held in the stone hall at Dunmore. The council consisted of five elected merchants, one guild representative (not from the Cartographers' Guild, to avoid conflict), and the town's chief magistrate, who presided.

The guild master presented the claim. He described the charts, their provenance, their registration in the guild archive, and the circumstances of their removal. He presented the archivist's log and the watch register.

Lord Edren presented his defence. He produced a written statement from Brennick -- now retired and living on the farmstead whose lease had been the subject of the debt -- confirming the transfer. He produced the debt ledger showing the forty-mark obligation and its settlement. He argued that the guild's eighteen-month delay in making a formal demand demonstrated that the guild had accepted the transfer.

The magistrate asked both parties whether they would accept a mediated settlement. The guild master said he would listen. Edren said he would listen.

The magistrate proposed: Lord Edren returns the seven charts to the guild. The guild pays Edren forty gold marks to settle Brennick's original debt, which the guild would then recover from Brennick as an internal disciplinary matter.

Lord Edren refused. He said the charts' value to his house was not forty gold marks. His house controlled the northern passes. The charts gave him knowledge of every route, every waypoint, every seasonal variation. That knowledge was worth more than forty marks a year in toll revenue alone. He would not trade strategic intelligence for the price of a farmstead lease.

The guild master said Edren had just admitted the charts' value was strategic, not sentimental -- which meant his house was using guild property for commercial advantage, which was precisely the harm the guild was claiming.

The magistrate asked Edren whether he would accept a higher compensating payment. Edren said no amount of money replaced the information on the charts. The negotiation collapsed. The magistrate recorded the failure and asked both parties to submit final positions in writing within ten days.

**E4 (escalation scene).** The guild master's study, the following week. Halvorsen arrived with a document she had retrieved from the guild's charter archive: the original charter of incorporation, granted by the High Seneschal's office one hundred and twelve years ago.

She read the relevant passage aloud: any dispute concerning guild property that the local trade council could not resolve could be referred to the High Seneschal's court for binding adjudication, provided the guild master submitted a petition within thirty days of the trade council's failure to reach settlement. The High Seneschal's ruling would override any local finding.

The guild master asked whether Edren could block the referral. Halvorsen said he could not. The charter right was unilateral -- it belonged to the guild, not to both parties. Edren could argue against jurisdiction, but the High Seneschal's court had not declined a guild charter petition in living memory.

The guild master signed the petition that evening. Halvorsen dispatched it by bonded courier to the seneschal's court the following morning.

**E5 (resolution scene).** Six weeks later. The High Seneschal's court, a stone chamber in the capital with a vaulted ceiling and a gallery for observers. The seneschal -- an elderly woman with a reputation for brevity -- sat at the raised bench. Her clerk read the petition aloud.

The guild master stated the claim: seven charts, guild property, removed without authority, held by Lord Edren of Thornwall.

Lord Edren stated his defence: the charts were transferred in settlement of a debt by the guild's own archivist, and the guild waited eighteen months to object.

The seneschal asked the guild master one question: did the guild's bylaws grant the archivist authority to alienate guild property? The guild master said they did not, and produced the relevant bylaw.

The seneschal asked Lord Edren one question: did he possess any instrument of transfer signed by the guild master or ratified by the guild membership? Edren said he did not. He said the archivist had acted with apparent authority.

The seneschal said apparent authority did not convey what actual authority withheld. She said the archivist had no power to transfer guild property, and therefore no transfer had occurred. The charts remained guild property. She ordered their return within twenty days.

She then addressed the debt. She said Brennick's forty-mark obligation to Lord Edren's father was a separate matter between Brennick and the house of Thornwall. The guild was not party to it. If Lord Edren wished to recover the debt, he could pursue Brennick through the magistrate's court.

Lord Edren asked whether he could appeal. The seneschal said the charter granted no appeal from her ruling on guild property matters. He could petition the Crown Council, but she noted that the Crown Council had not overturned a seneschal's property ruling in forty-one years.

Edren accepted the ruling. He told his retainer to arrange the transport of the charts to Carrend within the twenty-day period.

The guild master thanked the court. The seneschal nodded once and called the next case.

## C-arc-Fa1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect**

**Scenario.** A temple's high oracle demands that the newly appointed warden of the city's granary release the temple's tithe-grain, which the warden has withheld on the grounds that the city's food reserves have fallen below the emergency threshold. The warden resists, and the dispute is resolved when the city's governing elder rules that the tithe is suspended until reserves recover.

> A = the high oracle of the Temple of the Veil; B = Warden Soel of the city granary; O = the temple's tithe-grain -- one-tenth of the autumn harvest, stored in the granary and traditionally released to the temple on the first day of winter.

**E1 (demand scene).** The granary office, a cold room built into the stone wall of the storage complex, smelling of dust and dry barley. The high oracle had sent no messenger. She came herself, which was unusual enough that the warden's two clerks stopped their tallying and stood when she entered.

She was tall and wore the grey robes of her office, and she did not sit when the warden offered a chair. She told him that the temple's tithe-grain -- stored in vault seven, marked and consecrated at harvest -- was fourteen days overdue for release. The first day of winter had passed. The vault remained sealed. She had sent two written requests through the customary channel and received no response to either. She was now making the demand in person: release the grain by sundown, or explain why the granary was withholding what belonged to the temple by covenant.

The warden had expected this visit since the day he sealed the vault. He was young for the post -- appointed three months ago after his predecessor died of the fever that had also killed four hundred head of cattle in the valley and reduced the autumn harvest by a third. He told the oracle that the city's food reserves had fallen below the forty-day threshold established by the emergency statutes. The statutes gave the warden authority to suspend all non-essential disbursements until reserves recovered. The temple tithe, under the statutes, was classified as a religious allocation, not a survival ration. It fell within the suspension.

The oracle's expression did not change. She said the tithe was not a disbursement. It was a covenant obligation predating the emergency statutes by two hundred years. The grain in vault seven had been consecrated at harvest -- set apart, blessed, marked with the temple's seal. It was not the city's grain to withhold. It was the temple's grain stored in the city's facility as a matter of convenience, not jurisdiction.

The warden said he understood the oracle's position. He said the statutes did not distinguish between grain the city owned and grain the city held in trust. Below the forty-day threshold, all grain in the granary was subject to the warden's authority. He would not release the vault.

The oracle stood in the cold office for a long moment. Then she told the warden she would bring the matter to the governing elder. The warden said that was her right.

She left. The clerks returned to their tallying. The warden sat down and wrote a summary of the conversation for his records, noting the date, the demand, and his refusal.

**E2 (escalation scene).** Four days later. The temple's audience chamber, a circular room lit by oil lamps set into alcoves in the stone walls. The high oracle sat on the raised stone seat. Before her stood the temple's advocate -- an elderly priest named Voss who had argued the temple's interests before the governing elder on six previous occasions.

Voss reviewed the covenant document, which the oracle had retrieved from the temple's sealed archive that morning. It was old -- the vellum brittle, the ink faded to brown -- but the language was clear. The city's founders had granted the temple one-tenth of every harvest in perpetuity, in exchange for the temple's maintenance of the city's water-blessing rituals, which the founders believed protected the wells from contamination. The covenant had been honoured without interruption for two centuries. No warden had ever withheld the tithe. No emergency statute had ever been tested against it.

Voss said the legal question was simple: did the emergency statutes override the covenant, or did the covenant -- as a founding instrument -- take precedence? He said the governing elder had never ruled on this question directly. He said the temple's strongest argument was continuity: two hundred years of unbroken practice created an expectation that no statute could extinguish without explicit language, and the emergency statutes contained no explicit language overriding the covenant.

The oracle asked what the warden would argue. Voss said the warden would argue necessity -- that the statutes existed precisely for moments when ancient obligations collided with survival. He said the warden was not wrong that the reserves were low. The harvest had been poor. The fever had reduced the workforce. The city was, by any honest measure, closer to privation than it had been in living memory.

The oracle said the temple fed sixty acolytes and their families from the tithe-grain. If the tithe was withheld, the temple would have to purchase grain on the open market, at famine prices, with funds it did not have. The warden's suspension did not merely delay a ceremony. It threatened the temple's ability to sustain its community through the winter.

Voss said he would present the case to the governing elder at the next open session, three days hence. The oracle approved.

**E3 (authority-involvement scene).** The elder's hall, a low-ceilinged room in the civic quarter, heated by a firepit in the centre. The governing elder -- a woman named Torild, elected two years ago by the assembly of property holders -- sat on the stone bench at the far end. Voss stood to her left. The warden and his clerk stood to her right.

Voss presented the temple's claim. He produced the covenant, read the operative clause, and recited the unbroken history of the tithe. He argued that the covenant was a founding instrument, superior to the emergency statutes in any conflict, and that the tithe-grain had been consecrated and set apart at harvest, making it temple property held in the city's custody, not city property allocated to the temple.

The warden presented his defence. He did not dispute the covenant's existence or its history. He said the emergency statutes granted the warden authority over all grain stored in the granary when reserves fell below the forty-day threshold. He said the statutes used the phrase "all grain stored within the city's granary facilities, without distinction as to origin, purpose, or prior allocation." He said the phrase "without distinction" was dispositive. The drafters of the statutes had anticipated exactly this kind of claim.

The elder asked the warden how far below the forty-day threshold the reserves had fallen. The warden said they were at thirty-one days. The elder asked how much the tithe-grain represented. The warden said it was equivalent to four days of reserves.

The elder asked Voss whether the temple could accept a partial release -- enough to feed the acolytes through the immediate crisis, with the remainder held until reserves recovered. Voss said the oracle had authorised him to accept no compromise that treated the tithe as a conditional allocation. The tithe was absolute. Partial release implied partial authority, which the temple would not concede.

The elder was quiet for some time. The fire shifted in the pit. Then she spoke.

She ruled that the emergency statutes applied to all grain in the granary, including consecrated grain, for the duration of the emergency. The tithe was suspended, not cancelled. When reserves returned to the forty-day threshold, vault seven would be released to the temple in full, with no reduction and no conditions. The covenant remained in force. The suspension was temporary and would be recorded as an emergency measure, not a precedent.

She added one further provision: the city would supply the temple with a weekly allocation from the general ration -- enough to feed the sixty acolytes and their families at subsistence level -- until the tithe was restored. The allocation would come from the warden's emergency reserves and would be logged separately.

Voss accepted the ruling on the oracle's behalf, noting for the record that the temple did not concede the governing elder's jurisdiction over covenant obligations and reserved the right to bring the matter before the assembly of property holders at a future date.

The warden accepted the ruling and said the weekly allocation would begin the following day.

The elder dismissed both parties and asked her clerk to enter the ruling in the civic record.

**E4 (aftermath scene).** The following morning. The granary yard, grey light, a thin frost on the cobblestones. The warden stood at the loading dock and watched his clerks measure out the first weekly allocation for the temple: three sacks of barley, two of millet, one of dried pulse. It was less than the tithe would have provided. It was enough.

The temple's quartermaster arrived with a handcart and two acolytes. He inspected the sacks, counted them, and signed the warden's receipt. He did not speak to the warden beyond the transaction.

The warden watched the handcart roll out through the gate and disappear into the morning fog toward the temple quarter. He returned to his office, opened the reserves ledger, and adjusted the forty-day projection downward by the amount of the weekly allocation. Thirty-one days became thirty and a half.

He closed the ledger and began drafting his next supply requisition to the valley farms.

## C-arc-Fa1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (demand, three weeks earlier) -> E3 (resistance) -> E4 (escalation) -> E5 (authority involvement) -> E6 (resolution), embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** A clan matriarch demands that the keeper of the river-crossing surrender a stone bridge her clan built three generations ago, which the keeper now controls under a royal writ. The keeper refuses, citing the writ's authority. The dispute moves through a failed appeal to the local thane and is resolved by the king's circuit judge, who splits the rights.

> A = Matriarch Brenna of Clan Dalfoss; B = Keeper Halvard of the Greystone Crossing; O = the Greystone Bridge -- a stone bridge over the River Mael, built by Clan Dalfoss eighty years ago, now controlled by the keeper under a royal writ granting toll authority.

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The chronological order is: E2 (demand) -- E3 (resistance) -- E4 (escalation) -- E5 (authority involvement) -- E6 (resolution) -- E1 (aftermath). The depicted order opens with the aftermath.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The bridge at midday, three days after the circuit judge's ruling. The matriarch's eldest grandson stood at the western approach and watched the first travellers pay their toll -- not to the keeper's man, who had collected tolls alone for five years, but to a pair of collectors, one in the keeper's grey and one wearing the Dalfoss clan-mark on his sleeve. Both collectors checked the same rate-board, mounted on a new post at the bridge's head. The rates were unchanged. Only the hands that collected them had doubled.

The grandson had been present at the ruling. He had not spoken -- the matriarch did not permit her family to speak at proceedings where she was party -- but he had watched the keeper's face when the judge read the division of rights. The keeper had not argued. He had looked at the bridge, then at the matriarch, then at his own hands, and said nothing. The matriarch had nodded once, the way she nodded when a thing was finished.

The grandson watched the paired collectors work. The travellers did not seem to notice the difference. A toll was a toll. He turned and walked back toward the clan hall to report that the arrangement was functioning.

**E2 (demand scene -- embedded document).** Three weeks earlier. The matriarch's letter, delivered by bonded rider to the keeper's house at the eastern approach of the bridge:

> *Keeper Halvard,*
>
> *I write to make formal demand for the return of the Greystone Bridge to Clan Dalfoss.*
>
> *The bridge was built by my grandmother's father, Torsten Dalfoss, at his own expense, with stone quarried from the Dalfoss lands and labour drawn from the Dalfoss households. The clan maintained the bridge for fifty years before the crown issued its writ of crossing-authority to your predecessor. That writ granted toll-collection rights. It did not transfer ownership of the structure. The bridge is Dalfoss property, and the clan has never conveyed, sold, or surrendered it.*
>
> *I demand that you vacate the toll station, remove your men from the bridge approaches, and return control of the crossing to Clan Dalfoss within twenty days. The clan will honour existing toll agreements with the crown and will continue to maintain the bridge as it has since its construction.*
>
> *If you decline, I will bring the matter before Thane Korren at the autumn assembly.*
>
> *Brenna Dalfoss, Matriarch*

**E3 (resistance scene -- embedded document).** The keeper's reply, delivered by his own rider four days later:

> *Matriarch Brenna,*
>
> *I have read your letter and I reject your demand.*
>
> *The royal writ of crossing-authority, granted to this office twelve years ago and renewed by the crown twice since, gives the keeper of the Greystone Crossing full authority over the bridge, its approaches, its toll station, and its maintenance. The writ does not distinguish between the structure and the crossing-right. It grants authority over both.*
>
> *I acknowledge that Clan Dalfoss built the bridge. I do not dispute that. But the crown's writ supersedes private claims to structures of public importance. The bridge is a route of trade and military passage. The crown has determined that its control belongs with an appointed keeper, not a private clan. I hold the writ. I hold the bridge.*
>
> *If you wish to bring the matter before Thane Korren, that is your right. I will answer any claim.*
>
> *Halvard, Keeper of the Greystone Crossing*

**E4 (escalation scene).** The autumn assembly at Thane Korren's hall, six days later. The hall was full -- the autumn assembly drew every landholder in the district, and the Dalfoss-Halvard dispute had been discussed in the market for weeks.

The matriarch presented her claim in person. She was seventy years old and she stood without support. She described the bridge's construction -- the quarrying, the masonry, the four years of labour -- and produced the original building accounts from the clan archive, written in her great-grandfather's hand. She said the bridge was Dalfoss property. The crown's writ granted toll authority, not ownership. She demanded the bridge back.

The keeper presented his defence. He produced the royal writ -- a heavy vellum document with the crown seal intact -- and read the operative clause: "full authority over the crossing known as Greystone, including the bridge, its approaches, its toll facilities, and their maintenance, for the duration of the keeper's appointment." He said the word "including" made the bridge part of the crossing-authority. He said the crown had granted him control of the bridge itself, not merely the right to collect tolls on it.

The thane listened to both. He asked the matriarch whether she would accept a compromise: the clan retains nominal ownership of the bridge structure, but the keeper retains toll authority and operational control. The matriarch said nominal ownership without operational control was an insult, not a compromise. The keeper said he would not share operational control with a private clan because the crown's writ made him accountable for the bridge's condition, and he could not be accountable for something he did not control.

The thane said the matter exceeded his authority. The dispute turned on the interpretation of a royal writ, which only the king's court or its circuit representative could adjudicate. He referred the case to the circuit judge and set a hearing date for the following month.

**E5 (authority-involvement scene).** The circuit judge arrived in the district on the appointed day. She was a younger woman than the matriarch had expected -- appointed by the king's chancellor the previous year, trained in the capital's law college, and reputed to be impatient with local custom when it conflicted with crown law.

The hearing took place in the thane's hall, which the judge used as her court. The matriarch and the keeper sat on opposite sides of the hall. The judge read both parties' written submissions, examined the building accounts and the royal writ, and asked three questions.

To the matriarch: did the clan ever receive compensation from the crown for the bridge? The matriarch said it did not.

To the keeper: did the writ contain any language of purchase, transfer of title, or compensation to the builder? The keeper said it did not.

To both: had either party attempted to negotiate a shared arrangement before the dispute reached this stage? Both said they had not.

The judge retired for one hour.

**E6 (resolution scene).** The judge returned and read her ruling aloud. The hall was silent.

She ruled that the royal writ granted the keeper authority over the crossing -- the right to collect tolls, to regulate traffic, and to maintain the bridge in serviceable condition. But the writ did not contain language of ownership transfer. The bridge structure remained the property of Clan Dalfoss, which had built it, maintained it, and never conveyed it.

She then addressed the practical conflict: how could the clan own a bridge that the keeper was required to control? She divided the rights. The keeper retained toll authority and the obligation to maintain the bridge in its current condition at his own expense. The clan retained ownership of the structure and the right to a share of the toll revenue -- one-fifth, paid quarterly, as compensation for the use of clan property under the crown's authority. If the keeper wished to alter or expand the bridge, he required the clan's written consent. If the clan wished to demolish or modify the structure, it required the keeper's consent while the writ remained in force.

The keeper asked whether he could appeal to the crown. The judge said he could, but noted that the crown's practice was to uphold circuit rulings unless they conflicted with statute, and no statute addressed this question.

The matriarch accepted the ruling. She asked the judge to set the date of the first quarterly payment. The judge set it for the winter solstice and instructed the thane's clerk to record the arrangement in the district rolls.

## C-arc-Fa1-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** The master of a mages' college demands that a former student return a grimoire she took when she left the college, arguing it is college property enchanted with irreplaceable binding-spells. The former student refuses, claiming she wrote the grimoire herself and the college's enchantments were applied without her consent. The dispute is resolved when a neutral arcane assessor determines that the grimoire contains both the student's original work and the college's proprietary enchantments, and orders a separation ritual.

> A = Archmaster Caelen of the Thornmark College of Applied Thaumaturgy; B = Ysmay, former student and now independent practitioner; O = a grimoire -- a hand-written book of spell-work containing both the student's original compositions and the college's proprietary binding-enchantments.

**E1 (positioning scene).** The Thornmark College, a stone complex on a hill above the river, mid-morning. Archmaster Caelen sits in his study with the college's registrar and reviews the exit inventory for students who departed at the end of the previous term. The registrar points to a discrepancy: Ysmay, a fourth-year student who left voluntarily before completing her qualifying examinations, was issued a standard departure clearance by the dormitory warden. The clearance lists her personal effects -- clothing, writing instruments, two commercial spell-texts purchased at the town bookshop. It does not list a grimoire.

But Ysmay had a grimoire. Caelen knows this because he assigned her one of the college's blank binding-frames -- a specially prepared volume whose pages carry the college's proprietary enchantment lattice, a lattice that allows spell-work written on the pages to be activated directly from the book rather than memorised and cast from the practitioner's mind. The binding-frames are the college's most closely held technique. They are numbered, tracked, and returned when a student departs. Ysmay's binding-frame -- number forty-one -- is not in the armoury.

Caelen asks the registrar to confirm. The registrar checks the armoury log: frame forty-one was issued to Ysmay two years ago. It has not been returned. The dormitory warden's clearance makes no mention of it.

Caelen dictates a letter to Ysmay, now practising in the market town of Elkhaven, three days' ride south. He demands the return of binding-frame forty-one within fifteen days.

**E2 (demand scene).** Fifteen days later. Ysmay's workshop in Elkhaven, a ground-floor room off the market square. Caelen has not come himself. He has sent the college's proctor -- a man named Drest, grey-haired and formal, who serves as the college's enforcement officer for matters of property and discipline.

Drest presents Caelen's letter and asks for the grimoire.

Ysmay invites him to sit. She says she received the letter. She says she will not return the grimoire. She says the binding-frame the college issued her was blank -- empty pages carrying the college's enchantment lattice but no content. Every spell in the grimoire was composed by her, in her own hand, during her four years at the college. The college provided the frame. She provided the knowledge. The book is hers.

Drest says the binding-frame is college property. It carries the college's proprietary enchantment lattice, which is itself a form of intellectual property more valuable than any student's spell-work. The frame was issued for use during her studies and was to be returned upon departure. She signed an acknowledgment to that effect at the time of issuance.

Ysmay says she signed an acknowledgment that she would return the binding-frame "in its issued condition." The issued condition was blank. The grimoire is no longer blank. It contains four years of her work. Returning the frame means returning her work along with it, because the spells are inscribed into the enchantment lattice and cannot be separated without destroying one or the other.

Drest says that is not the college's problem. The college wants its frame. What she wrote in it was written on college property, using college enchantments, in college facilities. If she wanted to keep her spell-work, she should have copied it to her own materials before she left.

Ysmay says the college's enchantment lattice does not permit copying -- it binds the inscribed spells to the frame's pages and prevents transcription. The college designed it that way deliberately, to prevent students from extracting college-enhanced spell-work. She says the college built a trap into its own property: a frame that cannot be returned without also returning the student's original work, because the frame makes the work inseparable from itself.

Drest does not respond to this argument. He says the archmaster's demand stands. He asks whether she will comply.

Ysmay says she will not.

Drest says the college will petition the Arcane Assessors' Court in the capital for a recovery order.

Ysmay says she welcomes the petition. She says she has already retained an advocate.

**E3 (escalation scene).** Six weeks later. The Arcane Assessors' Court, a hall in the capital built from pale stone and lit by enchanted glass panels that cast no shadows. The presiding assessor -- a woman named Thyra, appointed by the Crown Arcane -- sits at the high bench. Caelen sits to her left with the college's advocate. Ysmay sits to her right with her own advocate.

Caelen's advocate presents the college's claim: binding-frame forty-one is college property, issued under a signed acknowledgment, and must be returned. The enchantment lattice is the college's proprietary technique and represents decades of research. The advocate produces the acknowledgment Ysmay signed, the armoury log, and a description of the enchantment lattice authored by the college's head enchanter.

Ysmay's advocate presents the defence: the grimoire contains four years of original spell-work composed by Ysmay. The binding-frame's enchantment lattice makes the spell-work inseparable from the frame. Returning the frame means surrendering the spell-work. The college's own design created the inseparability -- the lattice prevents transcription by design. The college cannot claim its property back when it deliberately made returning the property destructive to the student's own work.

The assessor asks Caelen directly: did the college inform Ysmay, at the time of issuance, that spell-work inscribed in the binding-frame could not be extracted or copied? Caelen says the properties of the lattice are explained during the second-year enchantment practicum.

The assessor asks Ysmay: did she understand at the time of inscription that her spell-work would become inseparable from the frame? Ysmay says she understood the technical limitation. She says she did not understand that the college would use the limitation as a mechanism to claim her work upon departure.

The assessor says she will commission an independent arcane examination of the grimoire before ruling. She appoints a neutral enchanter -- not affiliated with the college or with Ysmay's practice -- to examine the grimoire and report on whether the spell-work and the enchantment lattice can be separated without destroying either.

**E4 (authority-involvement scene).** Three weeks later. The assessor's court. The neutral enchanter -- a man named Falk, who operates an independent enchantment consultancy in the capital -- presents his report.

He examined the grimoire over eight days. His findings: the binding-frame's enchantment lattice and Ysmay's inscribed spell-work are technically distinct layers. The lattice is a substrate; the spell-work sits on top of it. They are functionally entangled -- activating a spell requires the lattice, and the lattice stores charge from the spell-work's activation. But they are not structurally fused. A separation ritual exists. It is complex, it is expensive, and it carries a risk of degradation to both layers -- he estimates a twelve percent chance of partial damage to the spell-work and a seven percent chance of partial damage to the lattice. But it is possible.

He adds one further finding. The spell-work Ysmay inscribed is, in his professional assessment, original. It does not replicate any of the college's standard curricula. Several of the compositions are inventive enough that he would consider them publishable in the capital's arcane journals.

The assessor thanks Falk and asks both parties to prepare final arguments.

**E5 (resolution scene).** The following week. The assessor's court. The assessor reads her ruling.

She finds that binding-frame forty-one is college property and must be returned to the college. She finds that the spell-work inscribed in the grimoire is Ysmay's original composition and is her intellectual property. She orders the separation ritual to be performed by the neutral enchanter, at the college's expense, since the college designed the lattice that created the inseparability. The ritual will produce two objects: a binding-frame containing the college's enchantment lattice, returned to the college; and a set of extracted spell-pages containing Ysmay's compositions, returned to Ysmay.

She addresses the risk of degradation. She rules that if the separation damages Ysmay's spell-work, the college will compensate Ysmay for the loss at a rate determined by Falk's independent valuation. If the separation damages the lattice, the college bears its own loss.

Caelen's advocate objects. He says the college should not bear the full cost and risk of the separation. The inseparability was a known property of the frame, and Ysmay accepted it when she inscribed her work.

The assessor says the college designed a tool that trapped student work inside college property. The college cannot benefit from a trap it built. The cost falls on the designer of the constraint, not the person constrained by it.

Ysmay's advocate accepts the ruling. Caelen's advocate accepts the ruling under protest, noting the college reserves the right to appeal to the Crown Arcane. The assessor records the reservation and sets the separation ritual for the following month, to be performed in the court's own facilities under neutral supervision.
