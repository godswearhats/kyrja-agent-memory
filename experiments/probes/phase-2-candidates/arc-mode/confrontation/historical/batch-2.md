# Confrontation arc × Historical — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## C-arc-Hi2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A Venetian glass-master demands that the Council of Ten revoke a rival's monopoly patent on a new crystalline formula, claiming the formula was stolen from his workshop. The rival resists, insisting the work is original. The Inquisitors of State investigate and the council renders a binding verdict. Setting: 1590s Venice.

> A = Master Girolamo Seguso, glass-master of Murano; B = Master Enzo Barovier, holder of the contested monopoly patent; O = the monopoly patent on the crystalline glass formula (privilegio granted by the Council of Ten).

**E1 (demand scene).** The antechamber of the Council of Ten, the Ducal Palace, a morning in November. Seguso stood before the secretary's desk in his guild coat -- the dark wool with the Murano glassmakers' furnace badge on the left breast. He had waited two hours. The secretary had made him wait because petitions from guild artisans were heard in order of submission, and Seguso had submitted his petition only the previous day.

"I am here to contest the privilegio granted to Enzo Barovier on the twenty-third of September," Seguso said. "The formula described in his application is mine. It was developed in my workshop over three years. Barovier obtained it through a former apprentice of mine who left my service in June and entered Barovier's furnace in July."

The secretary recorded the petition without expression. He asked whether Seguso had evidence beyond his claim.

"I have my workshop ledgers. Three years of batch records -- every variation, every failed pour, every adjustment to the sand ratios and the manganese content. The ledgers are dated and witnessed by my journeymen. Barovier's application describes a formula that matches my final iteration exactly. He filed the application eight weeks after my apprentice joined his workshop. The sequence speaks for itself."

The secretary asked whether Seguso had filed his own application before Barovier's.

"No. I had not yet perfected the formula for commercial production. I was still testing the annealing temperatures. Barovier filed first because he had my result without my process. He did not need to test. He already knew."

The secretary sealed the petition and told Seguso the Council of Ten would review it at its next session. He told Seguso not to approach Barovier or the apprentice in question before the council's response. Seguso agreed and left the palace.

**E2 (resistance scene).** Barovier's glassworks on Murano, ten days later. The Inquisitors of State -- the three-man investigative arm of the Council of Ten -- had sent a notary to take Barovier's deposition. Barovier received the notary in his office above the furnace floor, a room that smelled of ash and hot sand.

The notary read Seguso's petition aloud. Barovier listened with his arms crossed.

"The formula is mine," Barovier said. "I developed it independently. Seguso is a competent glass-master, but he is not the only man on Murano capable of working with crystalline compounds. The sand ratios he claims as his own are variations on techniques that have been known in this trade for fifty years."

The notary asked about the apprentice -- a young man named Luca Morin, who had left Seguso's workshop and entered Barovier's.

"Morin came to me because Seguso's wages were poor and his temper was worse. He is a skilled furnace worker. He did not bring me a formula. He brought me his hands and his training. I was already working on the crystalline compound when he arrived. His presence accelerated my progress because he is good at the furnace -- not because he carried another man's secrets."

The notary asked whether Barovier would submit his own workshop ledgers for comparison with Seguso's.

"I will not. My ledgers contain proprietary methods that extend beyond the single formula in question. Seguso's petition concerns one compound. My ledgers document dozens. I will not expose my entire body of work to resolve one man's jealousy."

The notary recorded the refusal, noted the date, and returned to Venice.

**E3 (escalation scene).** The Ducal Palace, three weeks later. The Inquisitors of State had completed their preliminary investigation. They had interviewed the apprentice Luca Morin separately, at the Inquisitors' office on the second floor. Morin had been nervous -- the Inquisitors' reputation ensured that -- and had given a detailed account.

Morin said he had worked in Seguso's workshop for four years. He had assisted in the development of the crystalline formula during the final year. He knew the sand ratios, the manganese proportions, and the annealing schedule. When he joined Barovier's furnace, Barovier had asked him what techniques Seguso was working on. Morin had told him. He had not thought of it as theft -- Barovier had asked, and Morin had answered, and the conversation had taken place over a meal as though it were ordinary shop talk.

The Inquisitors noted that Morin's account contradicted Barovier's deposition on one essential point: Barovier had said Morin brought no information. Morin said Barovier had asked for it directly.

The Inquisitors forwarded their report to the Council of Ten with a recommendation: summon both masters to appear in person. The council set the hearing for the first week of January.

**E4 (authority involvement scene).** The council chamber of the Ten, January. Both masters stood before the long table where the ten councillors sat with the Doge at the head. The Inquisitors' report lay open before the senior councillor.

The senior councillor addressed Barovier. He noted the contradiction between Barovier's deposition and Morin's testimony. He asked Barovier to explain why Morin would claim that Barovier had solicited the formula if the claim were false.

"Because Morin is a disgruntled apprentice who left one master and is now afraid of the consequences," Barovier said. "He told you what he thought you wanted to hear. He is protecting himself by placing the initiative with me rather than admitting he volunteered the information."

The senior councillor asked whether Barovier would now submit his workshop ledgers. He noted that the council had the authority to compel production under the terms of the monopoly statute.

"If the council compels them, I will comply. But I will not surrender them voluntarily. And I will note for the record that Seguso's petition is motivated by commercial rivalry, not by justice. He wants the privilegio for himself. If the council revokes mine, he will file his own within the week."

Seguso spoke. "I will file my own because the formula is mine. I do not deny wanting the privilegio. I deny that Barovier deserves it."

The senior councillor asked both men to withdraw while the council deliberated.

**E5 (resolution scene).** The same chamber, two hours later. Both masters were recalled. The chamber was quiet. The Doge did not speak; he sat with his hands folded while the senior councillor read the verdict.

The council found that Barovier's privilegio had been obtained on the basis of a formula that was substantially derived from Seguso's prior work, as evidenced by the correspondence between Seguso's ledgers and Barovier's application, and by the testimony of Luca Morin, which the council found credible. The privilegio was revoked.

However, the council did not grant a new privilegio to Seguso. The senior councillor explained: the formula was now known to two workshops and had been disclosed in testimony before the Inquisitors of State. The council could not grant a monopoly on knowledge that was no longer exclusive. Both masters were free to produce the crystalline glass. Neither held a monopoly.

Barovier said the decision was unjust. He said he had invested his own capital in developing the production process and that revoking the privilegio without compensation destroyed that investment.

The senior councillor said the council's authority over monopoly patents was absolute and that the terms of the privilegio statute did not provide for compensation upon revocation. The decision was final.

Seguso said nothing. He bowed to the council and left. Barovier followed a moment later, and the two men descended the palace stairs on opposite sides of the staircase without speaking.

## C-arc-Hi2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A widowed plantation owner in Saint-Domingue demands that the colonial intendant enforce her late husband's will, which manumits twelve enslaved workers and grants them the family's secondary plot. The intendant refuses, citing the Code Noir's restrictions on manumission and property transfer to free people of colour. The dispute is referred to the colonial governor for final ruling. Setting: 1780s Saint-Domingue.

> A = Madame Claudine Dessources, widow and estate holder; B = Intendant Barbé-Marbois (the colony's chief civil administrator); O = enforcement of the will's manumission and land-transfer provisions for twelve enslaved persons.

**E1 (demand scene).** The intendant's office in Port-au-Prince, a stifling morning in August. Madame Dessources had dressed for the visit as though for church -- the indigo silk, the lace at the throat, the gold cross her husband had given her on their twentieth anniversary. She understood that the intendant dealt with planters every day and that what distinguished one petitioner from another was rarely the substance of the petition. It was the weight of the person delivering it.

She sat across from Barbé-Marbois and laid the will on his desk. The notary's seal was intact. The document had been executed in the presence of two witnesses, both men of standing in the parish, and registered at the colonial notariat within the required thirty days of her husband's death.

She told the intendant that her husband's will manumitted twelve enslaved workers by name and granted them the family's secondary plot -- eighty arpents of marginal coffee land on the northern slope, land that had not turned a profit in four years. The will was legally executed. She was asking the intendant to register the manumissions and the land transfer in the colonial records so that the provisions could take effect.

The intendant read the will carefully. He was a precise man -- she could see that in the way he traced each clause with his finger, pausing at the specific provisions. When he finished, he set the document down and folded his hands.

He told her that the Code Noir, as amended by the royal ordinances of 1771 and 1775, required the intendant's approval for any manumission and imposed restrictions on the circumstances under which that approval could be granted. A testamentary manumission of twelve persons at once was irregular. The ordinance of 1775 further restricted the transfer of real property to free persons of colour. He could not register the will's provisions without violating the standing ordinances.

She had expected this. She asked which specific provision of the Code Noir or its amendments prohibited a property owner from disposing of his own property and his own enslaved workers by will.

The intendant said the prohibitions were not on the act of willing but on the act of registration. The will was a private document. The colonial records were a public instrument. He could not enter into the public record a manumission that the ordinances did not permit him to approve.

**E2 (resistance scene).** The same office, the same morning, continuing. Madame Dessources did not raise her voice, but the intendant noticed -- she saw him notice -- that she shifted forward in her chair, and that the silk rustled, and that the gold cross caught the light from the window.

She told him that her husband had been one of the largest taxpayers in the northern parish for twenty-six years. He had served on the colonial assembly. He had contributed to the militia fund, the road fund, and the church restoration. His will was a reflection of his conscience, and the twelve people named in it had served the family for decades. Three of them had been born on the estate. Her husband believed he owed them their freedom and a piece of land that was, in any case, worth less than the taxes he had paid on it.

The intendant said he did not question her husband's character or his intentions. He said the ordinances existed for reasons that had nothing to do with individual cases. The colony's stability depended on the regulation of manumission and on the separation of property classes. Twelve manumissions and a land transfer in a single act would set a precedent that every planter's will in the colony could follow. He was not at liberty to create that precedent.

She asked him what recourse she had.

He said she could petition the colonial governor, who had discretionary authority to approve exceptions to the manumission ordinances on a case-by-case basis. He said he would forward her petition to the governor's office with his own summary of the legal obstacles and with a note -- which he would show her before sending -- that described the particular circumstances of the Dessources estate.

She asked whether the note would recommend approval or refusal.

He said the note would be neutral. It was not his place to recommend. It was his place to describe the law and the facts. The governor would decide.

She took back the will, thanked the intendant without warmth, and left.

**E3 (escalation scene).** The governor's residence in Cap-Francais, six weeks later. Madame Dessources had travelled from Port-au-Prince by coastal sloop, three days on rough water, because the governor did not visit the southern capital more than twice a year and the petition could not wait. She was received in the governor's study -- a cooler room than the intendant's, with louvred shutters and a view of the harbour.

The governor -- the Marquis de la Luzerne's deputy, a career naval officer named Bellecombe -- had the intendant's summary on his desk. He had also received, she learned, a separate letter from the parish priest in the northern district, who had written to confirm that the twelve enslaved persons named in the will were known to him, that several were baptised communicants of the parish church, and that the late Monsieur Dessources had discussed his intentions with the priest before his death.

Bellecombe told her he had read the will, the intendant's summary, and the priest's letter. He said the case was not simple. The ordinances were clear, but the circumstances were particular, and the governor's discretionary authority existed precisely for cases where the ordinances produced results that the crown's broader interests did not require.

He asked her a question she had not expected: whether the twelve persons in the will wished to be manumitted. She said they did. He asked how she knew. She said she had asked each of them. He asked whether she had asked them in the presence of other enslaved persons or privately. She said privately, one at a time, in the estate's kitchen, with no overseer present.

He wrote something in his notes. She could not read it from where she sat.

He told her he would render his decision within fourteen days and that she should remain in Cap-Francais until the decision was communicated. She agreed.

**E4 (resolution scene).** The governor's study, eleven days later. Madame Dessources sat in the same chair. Bellecombe had a single sheet of paper on his desk, written in his own hand.

He read the decision aloud. The governor, exercising his discretionary authority under the colonial charter, approved the manumission of nine of the twelve enslaved persons named in the will. The three exceptions were persons under the age of twenty-five, for whom the ordinance of 1775 imposed additional restrictions that the governor could not waive without referral to the minister of marine in Paris. The nine approved manumissions would be registered in the colonial records immediately.

The land transfer was denied. The ordinance prohibiting property transfer to free persons of colour was, in the governor's judgment, beyond his discretionary authority to override. The eighty arpents would remain in Madame Dessources's name. She was free to lease the land to the newly manumitted persons on whatever terms she chose, but the title could not pass.

Madame Dessources sat with the decision for a moment. Nine of twelve. The land held but leasable. The three youngest still enslaved pending a referral to Paris that might take a year or might never arrive.

She asked whether she could file an appeal on behalf of the three.

Bellecombe said she could. He said the appeal would go to the minister of marine and would require the endorsement of the intendant, which he could not guarantee. He offered to write a cover letter to accompany the appeal. She accepted.

She left the governor's residence and walked to the harbour, where the evening breeze was coming off the water. She stood there for a long time before returning to her lodgings to write letters to the nine.

## C-arc-Hi2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath) -> E2 (demand, three months earlier) -> E3 (resistance) -> E4 (failed negotiation) -> E5 (escalation) -> E6 (resolution). Embedded document voice.**

**Scenario.** A guild of physicians in Leiden demands that the university's Board of Curators revoke the teaching licence of an anatomist who has been conducting public dissections using bodies that the physicians claim were obtained without lawful warrant. The anatomist resists, arguing the bodies were provided by the city's executioner under an existing arrangement. The States of Holland intervene. Setting: 1630s Dutch Republic.

> A = the Guild of Physicians of Leiden; B = Dr. Adriaan van der Spiegel, university anatomist; O = the anatomist's teaching licence and his authority to conduct public dissections.

**Note on beat order.** Depicted order: E1 (aftermath, present) -- E2 (demand, three months earlier) -- E3 (resistance) -- E4 (failed negotiation) -- E5 (escalation) -- E6 (resolution). Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E5 -- E6 -- E1.

**E1 (aftermath scene).** The anatomical theatre at the University of Leiden, an evening in March. The gallery is empty. The tiers of seats rise in concentric rings around the central table, where a skeleton hangs on its wire frame -- a permanent exhibit, not a teaching specimen. The room smells of vinegar and dried herbs.

Dr. van der Spiegel enters through the lower door, alone. He carries a leather case containing his instruments and a folder of lecture notes. He sets the case on the table beside the skeleton and opens it. The instruments are clean. They have not been used in eleven weeks.

He opens the folder. Inside is the resolution from the States of Holland, dated five weeks ago, printed on heavy paper with the provincial seal. He has read it many times. He reads it again now, standing in the empty theatre, and then he places it back in the folder and closes the case.

Tomorrow is his first public dissection since the resolution. The body has been provided under the new warrant system -- signed by the schout, countersigned by the guild, documented in the university record. It took three weeks to obtain. Under the old arrangement, it would have taken an afternoon.

He looks up at the empty seats and wonders how many will be filled tomorrow. Then he closes the theatre and goes home.

**E2 (demand scene).** Three months earlier, December. The Board of Curators' meeting chamber at the university. The guild's delegation entered at the appointed hour: three physicians in their formal gowns, led by Dr. Willem Heurnius, the guild's eldest member and its elected representative to the city council.

Heurnius carried a written petition. He read it aloud without sitting:

> *To the Honourable Board of Curators of the University of Leiden, from the Guild of Physicians of Leiden, greetings.*

> *We, the undersigned members of the Guild, do hereby petition the Board to revoke the teaching licence of Dr. Adriaan van der Spiegel, anatomist, on the following grounds: that he has, over a period of eighteen months, conducted public dissections using human bodies obtained without lawful warrant from the city magistrate; that the bodies in question were provided by the city's executioner under a private arrangement that has no basis in the university's statutes or in the laws of the city; and that the Guild's repeated requests for an accounting of the bodies' provenance have been refused by Dr. van der Spiegel without explanation.*

> *The Guild does not question the educational value of public dissection. It questions the lawfulness of the means by which the bodies are obtained and the anatomist's refusal to submit those means to scrutiny.*

The Board's chairman, a regent named Van Hoorn, accepted the petition and asked Heurnius whether the guild had evidence beyond the petition's claims. Heurnius said the guild had compiled a list of fourteen dissections conducted in the past eighteen months, cross-referenced against the city magistrate's warrant records. Of the fourteen, only three had corresponding warrants on file. The remaining eleven had no documented authorisation.

Van Hoorn asked the guild to leave the list with the Board and to return in one week for the anatomist's response.

**E3 (resistance scene).** The same chamber, one week later. Dr. van der Spiegel stood before the Board in his university gown, the guild's petition and the warrant list on the table beside him. He had reviewed both documents. He did not dispute the numbers.

He told the Board that the arrangement with the city's executioner was longstanding and had been in place since before his appointment. The executioner provided the bodies of condemned criminals after execution, as was the practice at every university in the Republic. The arrangement did not require individual warrants because it operated under a general understanding between the university and the city. The three warrants that appeared in the magistrate's records were exceptions -- cases where the family of the condemned had contested the release and a specific warrant had been sought to resolve the dispute.

He said the guild's petition was not about lawfulness. It was about control. The guild had been attempting to assert authority over the university's anatomical programme for years -- demanding approval over dissection schedules, insisting on guild observers at public demonstrations, and requiring that the anatomist submit his lecture plans for review. The petition was the latest instrument of that campaign.

Van Hoorn asked van der Spiegel directly: would he consent to obtaining warrants for all future dissections, as the guild requested?

Van der Spiegel said he would not. The warrant process required application to the schout, review by the magistrate's clerk, and a waiting period of up to four weeks. The bodies of executed criminals could not be preserved for four weeks. A dissection delayed by a month was a dissection cancelled. The warrant requirement would effectively end the anatomical programme.

The guild's Heurnius spoke from the gallery. He said the anatomist's refusal to submit to lawful process was precisely the problem the petition described. He asked the Board to act.

Van Hoorn said the Board would deliberate and respond within the month.

**E4 (failed negotiation scene).** Van Hoorn's private chambers at the university, two weeks later. He had invited Heurnius and van der Spiegel separately to discuss a possible resolution before the Board issued its formal response.

He met Heurnius first, in the morning. He proposed a compromise: the anatomist would obtain warrants for all dissections, but the Board would negotiate with the schout for an expedited warrant process -- a seventy-two-hour turnaround instead of the standard four weeks -- applicable only to bodies provided by the executioner.

Heurnius said the guild would accept an expedited process provided the warrant required the guild's countersignature. Without that, the warrant was merely the university authorising itself.

Van Hoorn met van der Spiegel in the afternoon. He relayed the guild's condition. Van der Spiegel refused. He said the guild's countersignature gave the guild veto power over every dissection. If the guild could refuse to sign, the anatomist's programme was subject to the guild's approval. He would not accept that.

Van Hoorn reported the impasse to the full Board. The Board voted to defer the matter to the States of Holland, the provincial governing body, on the grounds that the dispute involved questions of criminal-body disposition that exceeded the university's authority.

**E5 (escalation scene).** The Binnenhof in The Hague, six weeks later, February. The States of Holland heard petitions on provincial matters every second week. The guild's petition and the university's referral had been placed on the agenda by the pensionary's office.

Heurnius spoke for the guild. He presented the warrant discrepancy, the anatomist's refusal to submit to oversight, and the guild's concern that bodies were being obtained through an informal arrangement that no civic authority had sanctioned or reviewed.

Van der Spiegel spoke for himself and, by extension, the university. He presented the history of anatomical instruction in Leiden, the practical requirements of dissection -- the body's perishability, the seasonal constraints, the educational calendar -- and the impossibility of conducting the programme under a warrant system designed for property disputes, not medical instruction.

The pensionary asked both parties the same question: would they accept a system in which warrants were issued, but through a new process specifically designed for anatomical use, administered by the university's Board of Curators rather than the city magistrate?

Heurnius said the guild would accept it only if the guild had a seat on the review body.

Van der Spiegel said he would accept it only if the guild did not.

The pensionary noted the positions and said the States would issue a resolution within the month.

**E6 (resolution scene).** The Board of Curators' chamber at Leiden, five weeks later, March. Van Hoorn read the resolution from the States of Holland aloud to the assembled Board, with Heurnius and van der Spiegel both present:

> *The States of Holland, having heard the petition of the Guild of Physicians of Leiden and the response of the University's anatomist, resolve as follows:*

> *First, that all public dissections conducted at the University of Leiden shall henceforth require a warrant issued under a new process administered by the Board of Curators, with a review period not to exceed seven days from application.*

> *Second, that the Guild of Physicians shall appoint one observer to the Board's review body, with the right to inspect the provenance documentation for each body but without the right of approval or refusal. The observer's objections, if any, shall be recorded in the university's minutes but shall not delay the issuance of the warrant.*

> *Third, that the anatomist's teaching licence is confirmed and shall not be revoked on the basis of dissections conducted prior to this resolution, the States finding that the prior arrangement, while informal, was not unlawful under the existing statutes.*

Van der Spiegel said the seven-day review period was manageable but that the guild observer's presence would have a chilling effect on the programme's independence.

Heurnius said the observer role without approval authority was meaningless -- a seat at the table with no voice.

Van Hoorn said the resolution was the resolution. The States of Holland did not negotiate amendments through the parties. Both men could submit appeals through the standard process if they wished.

Neither man indicated that he would appeal. Both left the chamber. The Board entered the resolution into the university's records and scheduled the first warranted dissection for the following week.

## C-arc-Hi2-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A Cherokee headman demands that a British Indian agent return a consignment of trade goods diverted from his town to a rival faction's settlement in exchange for the rival faction's agreement to a land cession. The agent refuses, arguing the diversion was authorised by the colonial governor. The headman threatens to close the trade path, and the agent is forced to negotiate directly. Setting: 1760s Carolina frontier.

> A = Attakullakulla (the Little Carpenter), headman of Chota; B = John Stuart, British Superintendent of Indian Affairs for the Southern District; O = a consignment of trade goods (gunpowder, cloth, and iron tools) diverted from Chota to the Overhill town of Great Tellico.

**E1 (demand scene).** The trading post at Fort Prince George, a morning in late October. The air carries the first cold of the season down from the mountains. Attakullakulla sits on the bench outside the storehouse with two of his warriors standing behind him. He has been waiting since dawn. Stuart arrives from the fort on horseback, accompanied by his interpreter, a man named Watts.

Attakullakulla does not stand. He speaks in Cherokee, and Watts translates.

"I sent fourteen horse-loads of deerskins to this fort in August. The skins were weighed and valued. The goods owed to Chota were packed and marked. I sent my nephews to collect them three weeks ago. The storehouse keeper told them the goods had been sent to Great Tellico instead."

Stuart dismounts and ties his horse. He sits on the opposite bench, facing the headman across the packed-dirt yard.

"The goods were redirected by order of the governor in Charles Town," Stuart says. "Great Tellico's headmen agreed to terms on the Long Canes boundary. The goods were part of that agreement."

"Chota's goods," Attakullakulla says. "Earned by Chota's hunters. Paid for with Chota's skins. You took what belonged to my town and gave it to men who sold land that was not theirs to sell."

Stuart says the governor's authority extends to the distribution of trade goods within the superintendency. The diversion was lawful.

"Lawful in Charles Town," Attakullakulla says. "Not lawful on this path. The trade agreement between Chota and this fort is between your king's agent and my town. The governor was not present when we made it. He did not smoke with us. He did not hear the terms."

He tells Stuart he wants Chota's goods replaced in full -- the same weight in gunpowder, cloth, and iron tools -- delivered to this fort within thirty days. He says this is not a request.

**E2 (resistance scene).** The same trading post, continuing. Stuart leans forward and speaks carefully, because he has dealt with Attakullakulla for seven years and knows that carelessness with this man costs more than it saves.

"I understand Chota's position," he says. "But I cannot replace the goods without the governor's authorisation. The superintendency's trade budget is set annually. The goods sent to Great Tellico came from the same allocation. There is no surplus to draw from."

"Then take them back from Great Tellico."

"That is not possible. The goods were distributed to Great Tellico's families two weeks ago. They cannot be recalled."

"Then find new goods. Your forts on the coast have storehouses full of powder and cloth. I have seen them."

"Those stores are military supplies. They belong to the garrison, not to the trade. I do not have the authority to requisition military stores for trade obligations."

Attakullakulla is quiet for a moment. Then he speaks again, and his voice is the same level tone he has used throughout, but Watts, the interpreter, shifts his weight as he translates.

"If Chota's goods are not replaced, Chota will close the trade path through the Overhill towns. No skins will come to this fort. No British traders will pass through Chota's territory. The path will be shut until the debt is honoured."

Stuart knows what this means. The Overhill trade path carries more than half the deerskin volume in the southern superintendency. Closing it does not just affect Chota -- it affects Charles Town's merchants, the London factors, and the revenue that pays Stuart's own salary. He also knows that Attakullakulla can do it. Chota controls the mountain passes. The path runs through the headman's jurisdiction as surely as a road through a toll gate.

Stuart says he needs time. He says he will write to the governor and request either replacement goods or authorisation to draw from the military stores. He asks for sixty days.

"Thirty," Attakullakulla says.

"Forty-five. The letter must reach Charles Town and the reply must return. Thirty is not enough for the distance."

**E3 (escalation scene).** Fort Prince George, six weeks later, early December. Stuart has received the governor's reply. It arrived by express rider two days ago. He reads it again in his quarters before the meeting.

The governor's letter authorises a partial replacement: half the original consignment's value in cloth and iron tools, but no gunpowder. The governor writes that powder allocations are fixed for the year and cannot be adjusted for trade purposes. He further writes that the Chota headman should be reminded that the trade relationship operates under the crown's terms and that threats to close the path are inconsistent with the friendship between the Cherokee and His Majesty's government.

Stuart folds the letter. He knows that delivering half the goods and a lecture about friendship will not satisfy Attakullakulla. He also knows that the headman's thirty-day deadline has already passed by two weeks, and that the trade path remains open only because Attakullakulla granted an extension when Stuart's initial letter explained the delay. The extension was not unlimited.

He sends Watts to Chota with a message: Stuart will meet Attakullakulla at the fort in three days, with the governor's response and a proposal of his own.

**E4 (resolution scene).** The trading post yard, three days later. Both men sit on the same benches. The December air is sharp. Attakullakulla has brought four warriors this time, not two, and a woman who sits apart from the group and does not speak -- his clan mother, whose presence signals that the meeting carries weight beyond the headman's personal authority.

Stuart presents the governor's offer: half the goods, no powder.

Attakullakulla listens to the translation. He does not respond to the governor's offer. Instead he asks Stuart a question: does Stuart wish to be the agent who lost the Overhill trade path?

Stuart says he does not.

"Then give me the full value," Attakullakulla says. "You have goods in this storehouse that are marked for spring trade with the Lower towns. I have seen the manifests -- your storehouse keeper is not as careful with his papers as he should be. Take Chota's replacement from the spring allocation and explain the shortfall to the governor when the time comes. By spring you will have Chota's winter skins to offset the loss."

Stuart sits with this for a moment. The proposal is irregular -- it requires him to reallocate goods from one district's budget to another without authorisation. But it is also practical. Chota's winter hunt will produce skins worth more than the replacement value. The books will balance by April if the hunt is good.

He asks whether Chota will guarantee the winter hunt's output to this fort as a condition.

"Chota's skins come to this fort because Chota's goods come from this fort," Attakullakulla says. "That is not a new condition. That is the trade."

Stuart looks at the storehouse. He looks at the headman. He looks at the clan mother, who meets his eyes steadily and says nothing.

"I will open the storehouse this afternoon," Stuart says. "Full replacement value. The spring allocation will be adjusted. I will inform the governor after the fact."

Attakullakulla nods. He stands, and for the first time in the meeting, he extends his hand. Stuart takes it. The warriors and the clan mother rise. They walk toward the horses without looking back.

Stuart watches them go, then crosses the yard to the storehouse and tells the keeper to begin weighing out the goods.
