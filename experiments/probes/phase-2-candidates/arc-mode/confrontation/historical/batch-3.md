## C-arc-Hi3-01

**Scenario.** Setting: Ottoman Istanbul, 1680s. A master bookseller demands that a court scribe return a rare manuscript loaned to the palace scribal workshop for copying. The scribe's superior — the chief calligrapher of the imperial scriptorium — refuses, claiming the manuscript has been designated as a palace acquisition and cannot be returned without the Grand Vizier's authorisation. The bookseller petitions the kadi.

> A = Bookseller Yusuf ibn Mehmed; B = Chief Calligrapher Hasan Efendi, on behalf of the imperial scriptorium; O = a single-volume commentary on Ibn Khaldun's Muqaddimah, annotated in the margins by a known Safavid scholar, loaned by Yusuf to the workshop for a forty-day copying engagement against a receipt bearing the workshop's seal

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (loan scene).** The receipt was written in Hasan Efendi's own hand, the workshop's tugra-seal pressed into the wax at the foot of the page. Forty days for the copying, the document said, against the return of the volume undamaged, with Yusuf's name in the margin as the lending party.

Yusuf had carried the book to the Topkapi himself and placed it on the scribal table. He had watched the senior copyist unwrap it and turn the first pages with the careful attention of a man who understood what he was holding. He had felt, walking back through the second court, the particular unease of a man who has handed a rare thing to another man's care and cannot quite remember why he trusted the arrangement.

He had the receipt in his satchel. He looked at it twice on the way home. It was a sound document.

**E2 (demand scene).** On the forty-second day Yusuf presented himself at the scribal workshop's antechamber and stated his purpose to the attendant. The attendant returned after some time and told him that Hasan Efendi would receive him.

Hasan Efendi was a man of fifty with ink-stained fingers and the air of someone who had not slept enough for twenty years. He received Yusuf standing and did not offer tea.

"I've come for the Muqaddimah commentary," Yusuf said. "The forty days have passed."

"The volume has been added to the palace register," Hasan Efendi said. "A formal acquisition was entered three days ago. You will need to direct any claim to the Grand Vizier's chancellery."

"I have the receipt," Yusuf said. "I lent the book. I did not sell it."

"The circumstances of its acquisition are a matter for the chancellery. The volume is now a palace holding."

"Hasan Efendi. Return my book, or tell me the price they're offering, because I was not offered a price."

"There is no price offered at this stage. The chancellery will be in contact."

Yusuf took the receipt from his satchel and placed it on the nearest table. "I want you to look at that document," he said, "and tell me it does not mean what it says."

Hasan Efendi did not look at it. "I have told you where to direct your claim," he said. "I have nothing further to add."

**E3 (first approach to chancellery scene).** The Grand Vizier's chancellery occupied a building near the second court and operated on a schedule that had no relationship to the needs of petitioners. Yusuf waited for three hours before a secretary received his complaint and took a copy of the receipt. The secretary's manner was neither hostile nor encouraging; it was the manner of a man who received complaints about palace acquisitions regularly and who had no personal investment in the outcome.

The secretary told Yusuf that the matter would be reviewed in due course and that he would receive a response. He could not say when. Yusuf asked whether there was an appeal mechanism. The secretary said the review was the appeal mechanism. Yusuf asked whether he could speak to someone with authority to issue an immediate return order. The secretary said he could not.

Yusuf left without the book and without a date. He went to the kadi's court the following morning.

**E4 (kadi petition scene).** The kadi who heard commercial disputes in that district was a man named Farrukh Beg, known for methodical reasoning and an impatience with vague testimony. He received Yusuf's petition on a Thursday, read the receipt, and asked three questions: whether the loan had been recorded, whether the period had been specified, and whether a return had been formally demanded and refused. Yusuf answered yes to all three.

Farrukh Beg issued a preliminary notice to the palace scribal workshop requiring Hasan Efendi to appear before the court in seven days with the relevant acquisition documentation. He noted in the notice that the court's jurisdiction extended to commercial disputes involving private parties regardless of whether one party's claims involved palace property, provided the original transaction predated the palace's interest.

A copy of the notice was sent to the Grand Vizier's chancellery as a courtesy.

**E5 (resolution scene).** Hasan Efendi appeared at the kadi's court on the appointed day, accompanied by a palace secretary carrying a document folder. The secretary presented the acquisition register entry. Farrukh Beg examined it.

The acquisition entry was dated three days after Yusuf's loan receipt. The kadi noted this. He asked the secretary whether the palace had purchased the volume or taken it under compulsory requisition. The secretary said it had been entered as a gift from an unspecified benefactor. The kadi asked who had authorised the entry. After some consultation with his document folder, the secretary named a junior palace official who was not present.

Farrukh Beg found for Yusuf. A loan receipt predating the acquisition entry established Yusuf's prior right. The palace could not acquire a property it did not yet possess. He ordered the volume returned within five days and noted that if the palace wished to purchase the volume, it was free to approach the owner with an offer.

The volume was returned on the fourth day. No purchase offer followed. Yusuf found, upon examination, that the copying had been completed; the workshop had made a clean transcription. He sold the original to a collector in Cairo six months later for a price that more than compensated for the inconvenience.

## C-arc-Hi3-02

**Scenario.** Setting: Colonial Massachusetts, 1730s. A Congregationalist minister demands that a printer retract a pamphlet the printer published criticising the minister's handling of a church disciplinary matter. The printer refuses, claiming the pamphlet is accurate and that the minister has no authority to compel a retraction. The town selectmen are asked to weigh in.

> A = Reverend Caleb Morse; B = Printer Ezra Hallett; O = a four-page pamphlet titled "A Plain Account of the Proceedings at First Parish, October Last," authored anonymously but widely attributed to a dismissed church member, printed and distributed by Hallett's press in a run of approximately eighty copies

4 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4

**Note on beat order.** Opens with the selectmen's proceeding (present), then moves back to reconstruct the pamphlet's publication and Morse's initial demand, then returns to the resolution.

**E3 (selectmen's proceeding — depicted first).** The selectmen's meeting was held in the town hall on a Tuesday evening in February, and half the town had come to watch or had sent someone to watch for them. There were eleven men on the selectmen's board and they sat at the front table with the look of men who wished this had come to them in writing instead.

Reverend Morse spoke first. He described the pamphlet as a libel against his character and an interference with the lawful discipline of the church. He asked the selectmen to order Hallett to withdraw the remaining copies from circulation and to publish a correction in the next issue of the county newspaper.

Hallett stood and said the account was accurate to the best of his knowledge, that he had exercised his judgment as a printer in accepting the manuscript, and that he was not aware of any civil authority the selectmen possessed to compel a retraction of printed matter that had not been found false in law.

One of the selectmen asked Hallett if he had verified the account before printing. Hallett said he had spoken to two of the witnesses named in the pamphlet and found their accounts consistent with what was written. He said he would not name the author.

The proceeding did not conclude that evening.

**E1 (pamphlet publication — reconstructed).** Hallett had received the manuscript in late November from a man he knew only as a regular customer of the print shop. The man had paid for the run in advance, in coin, and had provided a list of households to which copies should be sent. He had described the pamphlet as a factual account of a church proceeding and had asked Hallett to verify nothing — only to print. Hallett had read it himself before setting the type and had found nothing that struck him as obviously false, though he had noted that it presented one side of a matter about which there were evidently at least two sides.

He had printed eighty copies. He had distributed sixty-three.

**E2 (demand scene — reconstructed).** Morse had come to the print shop ten days after distribution. He had brought a copy of the pamphlet, folded open to the third page, and had placed it on Hallett's counter without a greeting.

"This account states that I dismissed Samuel Whitmore from the church rolls without conducting a proper hearing," he said. "That is false. A hearing was conducted."

"I printed what was given me," Hallett said.

"You printed a libel. I am asking you to retrieve the distributed copies and publish a correction stating that the account was in error."

"On whose finding that it is in error?" Hallett said. "Yours? You're the subject of the complaint."

"I am the minister of the First Parish and a gentleman, and I am telling you directly that this account is false. That ought to be sufficient for a printer of this town."

"With respect, Reverend, your word that the account of your own conduct is false is not sufficient for a printer of any town to destroy what he has printed. If you believe the account is libelous, the remedy is at law, not at my counter."

Morse took the pamphlet from the counter. "I will go to the selectmen," he said.

"That is your right," Hallett said.

**E4 (resolution scene).** The selectmen reconvened the following week with a written statement from Samuel Whitmore — the dismissed member whose case was described in the pamphlet — and a counter-statement from Morse setting out his account of the hearing proceedings. Three of the selectmen had also spoken privately to two deacons of the First Parish who had witnessed the proceedings.

The selectmen's chairman read their finding into the record. The board found that the pamphlet's account was substantially accurate in its description of the procedural steps taken and not taken. There was a genuine dispute about whether the steps taken constituted a "proper hearing" under the church's own rules, but that was an ecclesiastical question, not a civil one, and it was not the selectmen's role to adjudicate it.

The board found no civil authority to compel a retraction. They noted that if Reverend Morse believed he had been libeled, the courts of the colony were the appropriate venue. They declined to make any further order.

Morse stood and said the board had failed in its duty to uphold the peace and good order of the community. The chairman said the board had done its duty as it understood it, and that he hoped the minister and the printer could resolve their differences without further proceedings.

Morse left without speaking further to Hallett. Hallett thanked the board and retrieved his copy of the pamphlet from the table where it had been placed as evidence. The remaining seventeen copies stayed in his shop. He did not distribute them further, but he did not destroy them either.

## C-arc-Hi3-03

**Scenario.** Setting: Edo-period Japan, mid-1700s. A domain lord (daimyo) demands that a senior merchant house pay an extraordinary levy to fund a construction project. The head of the merchant house refuses, presenting accounts showing the house cannot meet the demand without ruin. The dispute is mediated by the domain's chief administrator (karō), who brokers a compromise under threat of the daimyo's full displeasure.

> A = Domain Lord Matsudaira Nobutaka; B = Merchant House Head Okaya Jirobei; O = an extraordinary levy of two hundred kan of silver, demanded to fund the reconstruction of the domain's secondary castle and road network, exceeding the merchant house's liquid holdings

6 beats, present tense, chronological

**E1 (preparation scene).** Jirobei receives the notice on a Tuesday and reads it four times before he shows it to his eldest son. Two hundred kan. The number sits in the room like a piece of furniture that arrived without being ordered.

He sends for the head clerk. Together they spread the account books across the low table and work through them for three hours. The liquid holdings are ninety-two kan. A forced sale of the northern warehouse and the Osaka inventory would yield perhaps another sixty, at loss. To reach two hundred would require calling in every outstanding debt and selling assets that the house needs to continue operating.

He does not send word to the domain office that evening. He sends instead for his brother-in-law, who served twelve years in the domain administration before his retirement, and asks him to come the next morning.

**E2 (consultation scene).** The brother-in-law, Toshida, sits with the account books and says what Jirobei already knows: the levy is ruinous, but direct refusal is dangerous. The domain lord has the power to revoke the house's trading licence, reassign their routes, and make any number of informal life difficult in ways that do not require explicit punishment.

The path, Toshida says, is not refusal. It is petition. Present the accounts honestly, request a revision, and make the petition through the karō, who has a reputation for pragmatism and who understands that a ruined merchant house pays no future levies.

Jirobei prepares the petition himself that afternoon. It is formal, respectful, precise. It presents the accounts in full and requests that the levy be reduced to a sum the house can meet, or alternatively spread over several years. He seals it and sends it to the karō's office by registered messenger.

**E3 (demand scene).** He does not receive a reply to the petition. Instead, three days later, he is summoned to the domain office and shown into a receiving room where the daimyo's chief secretary — not the karō — is waiting. The secretary reads from a document: the levy stands as stated, payment is required within thirty days, failure to pay will be treated as a failure of commercial duty to the domain, with consequences accordingly.

Jirobei asks to speak to the karō directly. The secretary says the karō is occupied with the construction survey and cannot be reached at present. He repeats the thirty-day deadline.

Jirobei says, with careful formality, that he wishes the domain to know that the house's accounts, submitted in full with the petition, demonstrate that the sum requested exceeds the house's means without causing the destruction of the trading operation that generates the domain's customs revenue. He asks whether the domain wishes him to confirm this assessment before the thirty days have elapsed.

The secretary writes something in his ledger. He says he will convey the message. He dismisses Jirobei without tea.

**E4 (karō intervention scene).** The karō, Yamashita Genzaemon, comes to the Okaya house himself two weeks later — an unusual step that signals his own discomfort with the situation. He arrives in the evening, without announcement, and accepts tea.

He tells Jirobei that the daimyo is committed to the construction project and that the levy is politically necessary. He tells him also, in the carefully indirect manner of a man who is giving information without saying he is giving information, that three other merchant houses have already paid partial amounts and received informal assurances of future favour. The daimyo wants the matter closed.

Jirobei listens. He asks, with equal care, what partial amount would be considered acceptable from the Okaya house, given that the house's accounts have been submitted and are presumably in the karō's possession.

Yamashita says he cannot speak for the daimyo. But he suggests that a first payment of sixty kan, delivered promptly, followed by a formal commitment to a multi-year remainder schedule, would demonstrate the house's good faith in a manner likely to satisfy.

**E5 (negotiation scene).** Jirobei convenes his senior household that night. The numbers are discussed. Sixty kan is within reach without forced sales. A multi-year remainder — say, twenty kan per year for seven years — is manageable if the trading routes remain intact and the domain does not impose additional levies in the interim. The question is whether the daimyo will honour an informal arrangement brokered by the karō.

His eldest son says: the karō came himself. That is not nothing.

Jirobei sends word to the karō's office the following morning: the Okaya house will deliver sixty kan within fifteen days and requests the opportunity to present a formal commitment schedule for the remainder. He asks whether this proposal will be received favourably.

The reply comes by noon: the karō will arrange an audience.

**E6 (resolution scene).** The audience is formal but brief. The daimyo is present in the main receiving hall, seated. The karō stands to his left. Jirobei presents the sixty kan — counted and sealed in lacquered boxes that were made for exactly this kind of occasion — and then presents a written commitment for twenty kan per year for the following seven years, signed with the house seal.

The daimyo accepts the first payment. He says, through the karō, that the house's good faith is noted and that the trading licence will be renewed for a further five years at the current terms.

He does not acknowledge that the original levy has been reduced. There is no formal revision. The payment schedule is entered in the domain records as a "supplementary contribution arrangement." The face of the demand is never openly revised; it is simply never collected in full.

Jirobei leaves the castle carrying nothing but a receipt. He considers this, walking back through the gate, roughly equal to a victory.

## C-arc-Hi3-04

**Scenario.** Setting: Cape Colony, South Africa, 1820s. A British colonial magistrate demands that a Khoikhoi headman turn over a young man of his community accused of cattle-theft from a settler farm. The headman refuses, insisting the man is innocent and that the accusation was made without evidence, and requests that the matter be heard by a circuit court with the accused present and able to speak. The settler, impatient with the delay, attempts to take the matter directly to the lieutenant-governor.

> A = Magistrate Pemberton; B = Headman Xhabe; O = Jakob, a young man of Xhabe's community, accused by farmer Gerrit Venter of stealing two oxen; the contested question is both the fact of the alleged theft and the appropriate procedural body for hearing the case

5 beats, free indirect style, chronological

**E1 (accusation scene).** Pemberton received Venter's complaint on a Monday and sent a written notice to Xhabe's settlement the same afternoon. The notice was standard: the accused was to be produced at the magistrate's office within three days for examination. Pemberton had handled fifty such notices in the two years since his appointment and had not had cause to expect difficulty with this one.

He was therefore surprised when, on the third day, Xhabe arrived at the office without Jakob and with an answer that had clearly been considered.

**E2 (demand scene).** Xhabe said that Jakob had not stolen the cattle. He said this without elaboration, which Pemberton noted was not the manner of a man who had been caught in something and was trying to talk around it. It was the manner of a man who had already decided what he was going to say.

Pemberton told him to produce the accused. He said the examination would be conducted fairly and that if the accusation lacked evidence, it would not stand.

Xhabe said he had no doubt of Pemberton's personal fairness, but that he had seen examinations conducted in similar cases and that the outcome often had more to do with who was telling the story than with the evidence. He asked whether Jakob could be examined before the circuit court, where the proceedings were recorded and where Jakob could speak through an interpreter who was not employed by either party.

Pemberton told him that the circuit court sat twice a year and was three months away. In the meantime, the accused was required to appear for preliminary examination. That was the law.

Xhabe said he understood the law. He said Jakob would not come before the local examination without a guarantee that he would not be committed to the goal before the circuit court sat to hear the case properly.

Pemberton could not give that guarantee, and both of them knew it.

**E3 (escalation scene).** Venter, hearing that the accused had not been produced, rode to the magistrate's office the following afternoon and told Pemberton that if he could not enforce a simple production order, Venter would write to the lieutenant-governor directly.

Pemberton told him to do as he saw fit, and that in the meantime the matter was proceeding by law. He was not, he said privately, afraid of Venter's letter, but he was aware that a complaint to the lieutenant-governor from a settler in good standing about a magistrate's failure to enforce a production order was not a comfortable thing to have on record.

He rode to Xhabe's settlement himself the following morning. He found Xhabe and four of the community's elder men waiting outside, which told him the visit had been anticipated.

He repeated the demand. He said he had no power to guarantee the circuit court outcome, but he gave his word that the preliminary examination would be conducted in writing, that Xhabe could be present throughout, and that he, Pemberton, would personally forward the examination record to the circuit court judge with a note recommending expedited hearing if the evidence appeared contested.

Xhabe looked at the four elder men. Something passed between them that Pemberton could read only approximately.

**E4 (preparation scene).** Jakob was brought to the magistrate's office the following morning. He was seventeen. He answered questions through an interpreter — not the regular court interpreter, whom Pemberton had replaced at Xhabe's request with a missionary who had no employment relationship with the magistracy — and his account was direct.

The cattle, Jakob said, had strayed onto the common land adjacent to the settlement after a section of Venter's fence gave way in the rain. He had found them and put them in the settlement's enclosure to keep them from the scrubland. He had sent a boy to Venter's farm to report the cattle's location. The boy had gone and come back and said no one had answered the farm gate.

Pemberton asked whether the boy could be questioned. Xhabe said yes.

The boy confirmed the account. He had knocked at the gate for some time and left when no one came. He could name the day and the weather and the number of knocks. He gave these details without being asked, which Pemberton noted in the record.

**E5 (resolution scene).** Pemberton did not commit Jakob to the gaol. He wrote to Venter informing him that the preliminary examination had produced evidence of an alternative explanation for the cattle's absence and that the matter would be forwarded to the circuit court for determination. He noted in the letter that if Venter chose to pursue the accusation at circuit, he would need to address the fence and the non-response to the boy's visit.

Venter did not write to the lieutenant-governor. He wrote an angry letter to Pemberton instead, which Pemberton filed without response.

Xhabe received a copy of the examination record two days later by messenger. He read it — or had it read to him; Pemberton was not sure which — and sent back a brief acknowledgement through the same messenger. The acknowledgement said nothing that could not have been said in passing at a market stall, which was to say that it said nothing at all, and for a man in Xhabe's position in the colony, Pemberton understood that this was all that could safely be said.

The circuit court heard the case in the autumn. The accusation was dismissed for want of evidence. Venter's fence was noted in the proceeding record as in need of repair.
