# Confrontation arc x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## C-arc-Hi1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A Venetian glassmaker on Murano demands that the Council of Ten revoke a license it granted to a rival workshop, arguing the license permits the rival to produce a type of glass whose technique is protected under the guild's secrecy statutes. The Council refuses to revoke the license, and the dispute escalates through the guild's internal tribunal before the Council imposes a binding compromise. Setting: 1480s Venice.

> A = Master Orsini, senior glassmaker of the Orsini furnace on Murano; B = the Council of Ten (represented by its secretary); O = the production license granted to the Baldini workshop, which Orsini claims authorises the use of a technique protected under Murano's guild secrecy statutes.

**E1 (demand scene).** The guild hall on Murano, a low-ceilinged room above the canal, June. The guild warden sat at the head of the table. Master Orsini stood before him. The other masters had gone -- the regular session had ended an hour ago, and Orsini had asked the warden to stay.

"I need the guild to petition the Council to revoke Baldini's license," Orsini said. "The license authorises him to produce lattimo glass. Lattimo is a protected technique. My family developed it. It's registered in the guild rolls under my grandfather's name, and the secrecy statute forbids any workshop from producing a registered technique without the originator's consent."

The warden had heard the complaint before -- not from Orsini, but from three other masters who had come to him separately over the past month. Baldini's new license had unsettled the island.

"The Council granted the license," the warden said. "They reviewed it against the guild statutes before issuing it. I was present at the hearing."

"Then the Council made an error. I've read the license. It says Baldini may produce 'white opaque glass of the milk variety.' That's lattimo. The secrecy statute is clear: any technique registered in the guild rolls cannot be practiced by another workshop without written consent from the registering family. I have given no consent."

"Baldini's advocate argued at the hearing that lattimo is no longer a secret. He said the technique has been described in two published treatises -- one Florentine, one German -- and that a technique known outside Murano cannot be protected by Murano's secrecy statute."

"Published descriptions are not working knowledge. Those treatises describe the colour. They don't describe the batch composition, the furnace temperature, or the timing. Baldini didn't learn lattimo from a book. He learned it from my former assistant, who left my workshop eight months ago and is now working Baldini's furnace."

The warden was quiet. Then he said the guild could not petition the Council on Orsini's behalf without a vote of the masters. He said he would put it on the agenda for the next session. Orsini said the next session was three weeks away and Baldini was already firing lattimo. The warden said three weeks was the soonest the statutes allowed.

Orsini left the hall and walked back along the canal to his furnace. He did not visit Baldini's workshop on the way, though he passed it.

**E2 (escalation scene).** Three weeks later, the guild hall. All fourteen masters present. The warden read Orsini's petition aloud: that the guild formally request the Council of Ten to revoke Baldini's production license on the grounds that it violated the secrecy statute.

Orsini spoke in support. He presented the guild registration -- his grandfather's entry, dated 1438, describing the lattimo technique in sufficient detail to establish priority. He presented the secrecy statute, which he read aloud. He named his former assistant and stated that the assistant had carried working knowledge of the technique to Baldini's furnace.

Baldini spoke in opposition. He said the lattimo technique was widely known and no longer protectable. He said his own experiments had produced white opaque glass before the assistant arrived. He said the secrecy statute was designed to protect techniques that were genuinely secret, not techniques that had been published in foreign treatises and practised -- poorly, he conceded -- by workshops in Florence and Bohemia. He said Orsini was using the statute to maintain a monopoly the market had already dissolved.

The warden called the vote. Nine masters voted in favour of the petition. Four voted against. One abstained. The petition passed.

The warden drafted the petition that evening and dispatched it to the Council of Ten's secretary the following morning, by the guild's bonded courier.

**E3 (resistance scene).** Five weeks later. A chamber in the Ducal Palace, Venice. The Council of Ten's secretary received Orsini and the guild warden in the antechamber before the session. He was direct.

"The Council has reviewed your petition," he said. "It declines to revoke the license."

Orsini had prepared for this. "On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that the license was issued after a hearing at which both parties were represented, and that the Council determined at that hearing that the lattimo technique does not meet the secrecy statute's threshold for protection. The technique has been described in published sources. The Council's position is that a technique known outside Murano's guild system is not a Murano secret."

"The published descriptions are incomplete. They describe the appearance, not the process. No one outside Murano can produce lattimo of commercial quality from those treatises alone."

"That may be true, Master Orsini, but the statute does not require that the technique be perfectly reproduced elsewhere. It requires that the technique be unknown outside the guild. The Council has determined that it is not unknown. The determination stands."

Orsini turned to the warden. "Can the guild appeal?"

The secretary answered before the warden could. "The guild may submit a formal appeal to the Council within thirty days. The appeal will be heard at the next quarterly session. But I will tell you, as a matter of courtesy, that the Council has not reversed a licensing determination on appeal in the past eleven years."

Orsini said he would file the appeal. The secretary said that was his right and showed them to the door.

**E4 (authority-involvement scene).** The quarterly session of the Council of Ten, three months later. The chamber was formal -- the ten councillors seated at the raised bench, the secretary standing to the side, Orsini and Baldini and their advocates seated below.

Orsini's advocate argued that the secrecy statute's language -- "unknown outside the guild" -- meant unknown in its operative, working form, not unknown as a described curiosity. He said the published treatises were evidence of awareness, not evidence of knowledge. He produced a sample of lattimo glass made by a Florentine workshop following the German treatise's instructions. The glass was cloudy, uneven, and visibly inferior to Orsini's production sample, which he placed beside it.

Baldini's advocate argued that the statute drew no distinction between partial and complete knowledge. Unknown meant unknown. If any description of the technique existed outside the guild's rolls, the technique was no longer protectable. He said the Florentine sample's inferiority was a matter of skill, not secrecy.

The senior councillor asked Orsini's advocate a question: if the Council upheld the secrecy protection and revoked Baldini's license, what would prevent Orsini from claiming secrecy protection over every technique his family had ever registered, regardless of how widely known it had become? Where was the limiting principle?

Orsini's advocate said the limiting principle was the guild registration itself. Only techniques formally registered in the guild rolls were protectable. The registration system was the filter.

The senior councillor asked Baldini's advocate a question: if the Council declined to protect any technique that had been described in any published source, however incompletely, what incentive did any glassmaker have to develop new techniques? If a foreign writer could destroy a Murano monopoly by publishing a partial account, the secrecy statute was worthless.

Baldini's advocate did not have an immediate answer.

The Council retired. When it returned, the senior councillor read the determination.

The Council would not revoke Baldini's license. But the Council ruled that the secrecy statute's threshold for protection was "operative knowledge," not mere description. A technique remained protectable as long as its working method -- batch composition, temperature regimen, and production sequence -- had not been published in sufficient detail to permit reproduction of commercial-quality product. The published treatises on lattimo did not meet this threshold. The technique remained protectable under the statute.

However, the Council found that Baldini had obtained operative knowledge through Orsini's former assistant, which constituted a transfer of protected knowledge outside the originating workshop. Baldini's license was not revoked, but was amended: Baldini could continue to produce lattimo glass, but would pay Orsini's workshop a licensing fee of eight percent of gross revenue from lattimo production for a period of ten years, after which the technique would pass into the guild's common knowledge.

Orsini's advocate asked whether the fee was negotiable. The senior councillor said it was not.

Baldini's advocate accepted the ruling. Orsini's advocate accepted the ruling, noting for the record that Orsini had sought revocation, not compensation, and that the compromise rewarded Baldini for acquiring a technique by improper means.

The senior councillor said the record would reflect the objection. He closed the session.

**E5 (aftermath scene).** The following week. Orsini's furnace on Murano, early morning. The furnace was hot -- the night crew had kept it burning through the small hours, and the morning light through the open doors caught the orange glow from the crucibles.

Orsini stood at his workbench reviewing the licensing agreement the Council's clerk had delivered the previous day. Eight percent of Baldini's lattimo revenue, paid quarterly, verified by the guild warden's audit. The first payment was due at the end of the quarter.

His foreman came in and asked whether they should increase the lattimo batch for the season. Orsini said no. He said Baldini would undercut them on price within a year -- Baldini's furnace was newer, his fuel costs lower, and the licensing fee would not erase the margin. The fee was a tax on knowledge that had already left the building.

He told the foreman to begin trials on a new formulation he had been sketching in his notebook for the past month -- an aventurine glass, gold-flecked, using copper filings in a reducing atmosphere. He said he wanted a working sample before the autumn fair.

The foreman asked whether they should register the technique in the guild rolls once it was developed. Orsini said they would. He said they would register it the day they produced the first satisfactory piece, and they would not publish a word about it to anyone.

## C-arc-Hi1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect**

**Scenario.** A widowed landowner in colonial Virginia demands that the county surveyor resurvey a boundary line that she believes was drawn incorrectly, cutting forty acres of her tobacco land into her neighbour's patent. The surveyor refuses, defending his original work. The county court hears the dispute and orders a resurvey by an independent party. Setting: 1720s Virginia.

> A = Margaret Ashby, widow and landowner; B = James Poole, county surveyor; O = the boundary line between Ashby's patent and her neighbour's patent -- specifically, the position of the western marker, which determines whether forty acres of cleared tobacco land belong to Ashby or to her neighbour, Colonel Hardin.

**E1 (demand scene).** The county courthouse in Charles City, a timber building at the crossroads, a Thursday morning in April. The court was not in session -- the next sitting was two weeks away -- but the surveyor kept an office in the back room, and Margaret Ashby had ridden twelve miles to use it.

She found Poole at his table with his chain and compass laid out for cleaning. He stood when she entered -- he knew her by reputation if not by close acquaintance, and the reputation was that she managed her late husband's plantation with more competence than her late husband had.

She did not sit. She placed a copy of her patent on his table, unfolded it, and pointed to the western boundary. She told him the boundary line he had surveyed the previous autumn -- the line now recorded in the county plat book -- was wrong. She said his line placed the western marker eighty rods too far east, cutting forty acres of cleared tobacco land out of her patent and into Colonel Hardin's. She said she had paced the line herself, with her overseer and two witnesses, using the bearing and distance described in the original patent, and had found that the marker Poole had set did not correspond to the patent's description.

Poole looked at the patent and then at his own survey notes, which he retrieved from the shelf behind him. He told her the survey was correct. He said he had run the line with a compass and chain, that Colonel Hardin's agent had been present, that the western marker was set at the point his instruments indicated, and that the bearing and distance in the patent were consistent with his measurement. He said the discrepancy she had found was likely the result of the patent's original description using magnetic north, which had shifted in the ninety years since the patent was granted. He said this was a common problem in old patents and that his survey accounted for the variation.

She said she was aware of magnetic variation and had accounted for it in her own measurement. She said the discrepancy was not a compass error. It was a surveying error. She asked him to resurvey the line.

He refused. He said a resurvey would call into question every boundary he had drawn in the county over the past six years. He said his instruments were sound, his method was sound, and the plat book was the official record. If she wished to dispute the record, she could bring the matter before the county court.

She said she would. She refolded her patent, thanked him for his time, and left.

**E2 (escalation scene).** Two weeks later, the county court. The four justices sat at the bench -- all planters themselves, all men, all acquainted with both parties. The clerk read Ashby's petition: that the court order a resurvey of the western boundary between her patent and Colonel Hardin's, on the grounds that the surveyor's line was in error.

Ashby presented her case. She had brought her overseer, who testified that he had walked the line with her and confirmed the discrepancy. She had brought the original patent, whose bearing and distance she read aloud. And she had brought a written opinion from a surveyor in the next county -- a man named Blount, who had reviewed the patent description and Poole's recorded line and concluded that the magnetic-variation correction Poole had applied was too large by a factor that would shift the marker approximately eighty rods eastward.

Poole presented his defence. He produced his field notes, his compass calibration record, and his chain-measurement tallies. He argued that Blount's opinion was based on a desk review, not a field survey, and that desk calculations could not account for local variation caused by iron deposits in the soil. He said the western boundary ran through a low ridge with known mineral content, and that his compass readings in the field had reflected that.

Colonel Hardin was present but had not filed a response. The senior justice asked Hardin whether he had any position on the matter. Hardin said he relied on the surveyor's plat and had no reason to question it, but that he would accept whatever the court determined.

The justices retired for less than an hour.

**E3 (authority-involvement scene).** The justices returned. The senior justice read the order. The court found that the discrepancy between the patent description and the recorded survey was sufficient to warrant a resurvey. The court did not find that Poole's original survey was in error -- it found that a legitimate dispute existed that could only be resolved in the field.

The court ordered a resurvey to be conducted by an independent surveyor -- not Poole and not Blount -- appointed by the court and paid from the county's surveying fund. The surveyor would run the western boundary line using the patent's original description, corrected for magnetic variation according to the standard published tables, and would set a new marker if the result differed from Poole's. Both parties -- Ashby and Hardin -- were entitled to have their own witnesses present. The resurvey was to be completed before the end of May, while the ground was dry.

Poole asked whether the resurvey would replace his recorded line in the plat book. The senior justice said it would, if the new marker differed from the old one. Poole said the replacement would undermine confidence in his other surveys. The senior justice said the court was ordering one resurvey of one line, not a review of Poole's entire career, and that the record would note the resurvey was ordered due to a legitimate patent-description dispute, not due to a finding of error.

Poole accepted the order. Ashby accepted the order. The clerk recorded both acceptances and set the resurvey date for the third week of May.

**E4 (aftermath scene).** A morning in late May, the western boundary. The court-appointed surveyor -- a man named Hatch, from Henrico County -- stood at the starting point with his chain crew. Ashby's overseer was present. Hardin had sent his steward. Poole had not come, though he was entitled to observe.

Hatch ran the line. He used the patent's bearing, applied the magnetic-variation correction from the published tables, and measured the distance with a new chain he had calibrated against the county's standard the previous week. When he reached the point where the patent's distance indicated the western marker should stand, he drove a stake into the ground. It was seventy-four rods west of Poole's marker.

Ashby's overseer measured the distance between the two markers and confirmed it. Hatch recorded the figure in his field book, noted the witnesses, and marked the new position on the plat he was preparing for the court.

The forty acres between the two markers were covered in tobacco plants, two months from harvest. The plants had been set by Ashby's labourers in March, before the survey dispute was filed but after Ashby had paced the line herself. The steward from Hardin's estate looked at the tobacco and said nothing.

Hatch filed his resurvey with the county clerk the following week. The clerk entered the new marker in the plat book and noted Poole's original marker as superseded. The forty acres returned to Ashby's patent. Hardin did not appeal.

## C-arc-Hi1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath/embedded document) -> E2 (demand) -> E3 (resistance) -> E4 (escalation) -> E5 (failed negotiation) -> E6 (resolution), embedded-document voice**

**Scenario.** The abbot of a Cistercian monastery demands that a wool merchant honour a contract to purchase the monastery's entire annual wool clip at the agreed price, after the merchant attempts to renegotiate the price downward citing a market collapse. The merchant refuses to pay the original price, and the dispute is resolved by the bishop's commercial court. Setting: 1340s England, on the eve of the wool crisis caused by Edward III's war financing.

> A = Abbot William of Fountleigh Abbey; B = Robert of Stamford, wool merchant; O = the annual wool contract -- the merchant's obligation to purchase the monastery's entire wool clip at the price agreed the previous autumn.

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. Chronological order: E2 (demand) -- E3 (resistance) -- E4 (escalation) -- E5 (failed negotiation) -- E6 (resolution) -- E1 (aftermath). The depicted order opens with a document written after the resolution.

**E1 (aftermath scene -- embedded document).** From the abbey's chronicle, in the cellarer's hand, entered after Michaelmas:

> *In the matter of the wool, which occupied the house from Candlemas to the feast of the Assumption, the following is set down for the guidance of future cellarers.*
>
> *The contract with Robert of Stamford was upheld by the bishop's commercial court in July, but the price was reduced by one-fifth, which the court judged fair in light of the market's collapse. The abbey received eleven marks per sack rather than the fourteen contracted. Robert paid under protest and took delivery of the full clip -- sixty-two sacks -- at Midsummer. The cellarer notes that at the time of delivery, wool at the Staple was trading at nine marks per sack, so eleven marks was more than the abbey could have obtained on the open market.*
>
> *The abbot instructed that future wool contracts include a clause permitting price adjustment in the event of market disruption exceeding one-quarter of the contracted price. The cellarer has drafted such a clause and it is appended to this entry.*
>
> *Robert of Stamford has indicated he will contract again next year, at a price to be agreed after shearing. The relationship is strained but not broken.*

**E2 (demand scene).** Fountleigh Abbey, February. The abbot's parlour, a small room off the cloister, heated by a single brazier. The cellarer stood by the door. Robert of Stamford sat across from the abbot with a cup of the abbey's beer untouched before him.

The abbot spoke first. He said the shearing was eight weeks away and the abbey expected Robert to fulfil the contract signed the previous October: sixty-two sacks of wool, at fourteen marks per sack, collected at the abbey gate by Robert's carters before Midsummer. He said the cellarer had prepared the storage and the lay brothers were ready. He asked Robert to confirm the arrangement.

Robert did not confirm it. He said the wool market had changed since October. The king's agents had been buying wool at the Staple at compulsory prices to finance the war in France, driving the open-market price below ten marks per sack. He said he could not pay fourteen marks for wool he would sell at nine. The contract would ruin him.

The abbot said the contract was the contract. He said the price had been agreed, the terms had been written, and the abbey had planned its year's expenses -- building repairs, tithes to the mother house, the infirmary's medicines -- against the contracted revenue. He said the market's misfortune was not the abbey's responsibility.

Robert said he was not asking to be released from the contract. He was asking to renegotiate the price. He proposed ten marks per sack -- above the current market, fair to both parties, and enough to keep the abbey solvent while allowing him to sell without a loss.

The abbot said ten marks was a reduction of four marks per sack on sixty-two sacks. That was two hundred and forty-eight marks the abbey would not receive. He said the abbey could not absorb that loss. He said the contracted price stood. He told Robert to honour it.

Robert said he could not pay fourteen marks. He said the abbey could take him to court, but a court judgment against an insolvent merchant produced no wool money -- only a ruling written on vellum.

The abbot told the cellarer to note the conversation and to begin preparing a formal complaint for the bishop's commercial court.

Robert left the parlour. The cellarer noted that he did not finish his beer.

**E3 (resistance scene).** Three weeks later. A letter delivered to the abbey by Robert's agent:

> *To the lord abbot of Fountleigh, with respect --*
>
> *I write to confirm that I will not take delivery of the wool clip at the contracted price of fourteen marks per sack. I remain willing to purchase the full clip at ten marks per sack, which reflects the present market and exceeds the price available at the Staple.*
>
> *I am aware that the abbey intends to bring the matter before the bishop's court. I will answer any claim. I note only that the court has, in three cases known to me, reduced contracted prices when the market has moved beyond what the parties could have foreseen at the time of agreement. The king's compulsory purchases were not foreseeable in October. They have changed the market for every merchant and every producer in the realm.*
>
> *I have no wish to harm the abbey. I have purchased Fountleigh wool for eleven years. I intend to continue. But I will not pay a price that requires me to sell at a loss.*
>
> *Robert of Stamford*

The abbot read the letter in the parlour and handed it to the cellarer. He said the abbey would proceed with the complaint.

**E4 (escalation scene).** April. The cellarer rode to the bishop's registry in the cathedral town, a journey of two days. He presented the formal complaint: that Robert of Stamford had refused to honour a signed wool contract at the agreed price, and that the abbey sought an order compelling performance at fourteen marks per sack.

The registrar reviewed the contract, the correspondence, and the cellarer's summary of the February meeting. He asked whether the abbey would accept a mediated settlement before the court hearing. The cellarer said the abbot had authorised him to listen to any proposal but had not authorised him to accept a reduction.

The registrar said the court's docket was full and the hearing would not be scheduled before June. He said the bishop's commercial court preferred mediated settlements to adjudicated ones, and that both parties might benefit from a meeting before a neutral assessor. He offered to appoint one.

The cellarer accepted the mediation on the abbot's behalf. The registrar sent a letter to Robert proposing a meeting in May.

**E5 (failed-negotiation scene).** May. The chapter house of the cathedral, used for commercial mediations when the bishop's court was not in session. The neutral assessor -- a Benedictine monk with experience in wool commerce, appointed by the registrar -- sat at the head of the table. The abbot had come in person. Robert sat across from him.

The assessor read the contract aloud and confirmed that both parties acknowledged its existence and its terms. He then asked each party to state their position.

The abbot stated his: the contracted price was fourteen marks. The abbey required full payment. The market's collapse was Robert's risk, not the abbey's.

Robert stated his: the market collapse was caused by the king's compulsory purchases, which neither party had foreseen. Ten marks per sack was fair. He would not pay more.

The assessor proposed a middle position: twelve marks per sack. A reduction of two marks, split equally between the parties.

The abbot refused. He said twelve marks still left the abbey short of its planned expenses by one hundred and twenty-four marks. He said the abbey had made commitments against the contracted revenue -- the infirmary roof, the tithes, the medicines -- and could not reduce them.

Robert said twelve marks was still above market and he would accept it, but only if the contract term was extended to three years at the same price, giving him certainty for future planning.

The abbot said a three-year commitment at twelve marks locked the abbey into a below-contract price for three years, and if the market recovered, the abbey would bear the loss again. He declined.

The assessor noted the failure and informed both parties that the matter would proceed to the bishop's commercial court in June.

**E6 (resolution scene).** June. The bishop's commercial court, a formal hearing in the great hall of the bishop's palace. The bishop's commercial judge -- a canon lawyer named Nigel de Thorpe -- presided. Both parties were represented by advocates.

The abbot's advocate argued that the contract was binding, the price was agreed, and the market's misfortune did not excuse performance. He cited two precedents from the bishop's court in which contracted prices had been upheld despite market movement.

Robert's advocate argued that the king's compulsory purchases constituted an extraordinary disruption not contemplated by either party. He cited the three cases Robert had referenced in his letter, in which the court had reduced contracted prices under comparable circumstances. He argued that compelling Robert to pay fourteen marks would drive him into insolvency, which served neither party.

The judge asked the abbot's advocate whether the abbey would prefer a reduced price with payment, or a full-price judgment against an insolvent merchant. The advocate said the abbey preferred the contracted price, paid.

The judge asked Robert's advocate what the highest price Robert could pay without insolvency. The advocate said eleven marks, and produced Robert's accounts to support the figure.

The judge retired for one hour. He returned and read his ruling.

The contract stood. Robert was obligated to purchase the full clip. But the court exercised its equitable jurisdiction to reduce the price from fourteen marks to eleven marks per sack -- the highest price Robert's accounts could support -- on the grounds that the king's compulsory purchases constituted an extraordinary market disruption that neither party foresaw and that made the original price unconscionable in its current application. The judge noted that the reduction was specific to this year's clip and did not amend the contract for future years.

Robert's advocate accepted the ruling. The abbot's advocate accepted it, noting for the record that the abbey had sought full performance and that the three-mark reduction represented a loss of one hundred and eighty-six marks against the abbey's planned revenue.

The judge acknowledged the objection and closed the case. Robert took delivery of the wool in July. He paid eleven marks per sack, by the cellarer's count, on the day of collection.

## C-arc-Hi1-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A master printer in Lyon demands that the city's book-trade guild readmit her late husband's workshop to the guild rolls, after the guild expelled the workshop on the grounds that a woman cannot hold a master's license. She argues that the guild's own statutes permit a widow to continue her husband's trade for the duration of her widowhood. The guild master refuses. The dispute is resolved by the royal provost. Setting: 1550s France.

> A = Isabelle Morel, widow and operator of the Morel printing workshop; B = the guild of printers and booksellers (represented by Guild Master Thibault); O = the master's license -- the right to operate a printing workshop under the guild's authority, to sell printed books at market, and to train apprentices.

**E1 (demand scene).** The guild hall in Lyon, a morning in March. The hall is on the second floor above a bookseller's shop, and the stairs creak under the weight of the seven men who serve as the guild's governing council. Isabelle Morel waits at the long table. She has been waiting for an hour. The guild master, Thibault, enters last and does not apologise for the delay.

She states her demand. Her husband, Pierre Morel, held a master's license in this guild for twenty-two years. He died six weeks ago. The workshop continues to operate -- the journeymen are at the presses, the apprentices are setting type, the current commission from the cathedral chapter is on schedule. She asks the guild to transfer the master's license to her name for the duration of her widowhood, as provided by the guild's statutes.

Thibault opens the guild's book of statutes. He reads Article Twelve aloud: a widow of a deceased master may continue to operate her husband's workshop, using his guild mark, for a period not exceeding two years, provided she employs at least one licensed journeyman and submits her work to the guild's quarterly quality inspection. After two years, or upon remarriage, the license lapses.

He closes the book. He says the council voted last week to deny the transfer. He says Article Twelve has not been invoked in Lyon in thirty years, and that the last time it was invoked -- by the widow of a bookbinder in 1523 -- the guild permitted it under duress and the quality of the workshop's output declined to the point that the guild revoked the license within a year. He says the council's position is that Article Twelve is an obsolete provision that the guild has effectively abandoned through disuse.

Isabelle says disuse does not repeal a statute. She says Article Twelve is in the book. It has not been amended. It has not been voted out. It applies. She asks Thibault whether the council is refusing to honour its own statutes.

Thibault says the council is exercising its judgment. He says the printing trade requires a master's hand, and that a master's hand is a man's hand -- this is not his prejudice, he says, it is the trade's custom, observed in every guild city in France. He says the council will help her find a buyer for the workshop and will ensure the sale is fair.

Isabelle says she does not want to sell. She says she has worked in the shop alongside her husband for fifteen years. She sets type. She operates the press. She reads proof. The journeymen report to her. The apprentices are bound to the workshop, not to the man who ran it. She says she will bring the matter before the royal provost if the guild does not reconsider.

Thibault says that is her right.

**E2 (escalation scene).** Two weeks later. The workshop on Rue Merciere, afternoon. Isabelle sits at the composing table with a man named Devaux, a solicitor who handles commercial disputes for the city's smaller tradespeople. He has reviewed the guild statutes, the council's denial, and the relevant royal ordinances.

Devaux tells her the guild's argument -- that Article Twelve is obsolete through disuse -- is weak. Royal ordinance requires that guild statutes be amended by a recorded vote of the membership, not by the council's informal practice. No amendment vote occurred. Article Twelve is in force.

He tells her the stronger problem is the royal provost. The provost adjudicates disputes between tradespeople and guilds, but the provost in Lyon -- a man named Duchamp -- is known to defer to guild councils on questions of internal governance. Duchamp will hear the case, but he may accept the guild's argument that the council has discretion to interpret its own statutes.

Devaux says the key is to make the case about the statute's plain language, not about Isabelle's competence. If the case becomes about whether a woman can run a press, the provost will have room to defer to guild custom. If the case remains about whether the guild can ignore its own written rule, the provost's options narrow.

Isabelle approves the strategy. Devaux drafts the petition that evening.

**E3 (positioning scene).** The following week. The guild hall, a called meeting of the full membership -- not the council, but all thirty-eight masters. Thibault has convened the meeting to formalise the council's denial.

He presents a motion: that the membership vote to amend Article Twelve, restricting it to cases where the widow operates the workshop for no more than six months, solely for the purpose of completing existing commissions and arranging a sale. He says this amendment preserves the widow's right while limiting the duration and scope.

A master named Bonnard objects. He says the amendment is being proposed specifically to undermine Isabelle Morel's pending petition before the provost. He says amending a statute while litigation is pending is improper and will look like manipulation to the provost's court.

Three other masters speak in favour of Bonnard's objection. Two speak in favour of the amendment. Thibault calls the vote. The amendment passes, twenty-two to sixteen. The amended Article Twelve is entered in the guild book.

Devaux, who is not present but hears the result from Isabelle that evening, adds the amendment and its timing to the petition.

**E4 (authority-involvement scene).** May. The royal provost's chamber in the Hotel de Ville. Provost Duchamp sits at the bench. Isabelle and Devaux stand on one side. Thibault and the guild's advocate stand on the other. The guild's book of statutes -- both the original and the amended version -- is on the bench between them.

Devaux presents the petition. He reads the original Article Twelve. He presents the timeline: Pierre Morel's death, Isabelle's request, the council's denial, and the membership vote to amend the article three weeks after the denial and nine days after the petition was filed. He says the amendment was enacted to defeat a pending legal claim, not to improve the guild's governance. He asks the provost to apply the original Article Twelve as it existed on the date of Pierre Morel's death.

The guild's advocate argues that the membership's right to amend its statutes is sovereign and cannot be second-guessed by the provost's court. He says the amendment passed by a clear majority. He says the original Article Twelve was obsolete and the amendment simply formalised what the guild had practiced for thirty years.

Duchamp asks the guild's advocate one question: is there any record in the guild's archives of the membership voting to suspend or limit Article Twelve at any point in the thirty years of alleged disuse? The advocate says there is not. Duchamp asks whether any widow applied under Article Twelve during those thirty years. The advocate says none did.

Duchamp says the absence of applications is not the same as the absence of a right.

He asks Isabelle one question: does the workshop currently employ a licensed journeyman, as Article Twelve requires? Isabelle says it employs two.

**E5 (resolution scene).** One week later. A written ruling, delivered to both parties by the provost's clerk:

Duchamp rules that the original Article Twelve, as it existed on the date of Pierre Morel's death, applies to Isabelle Morel's request. The membership's amendment, enacted after the petition was filed and in response to a pending claim, is not retroactive. The amendment applies to future cases, not to Isabelle's.

Under the original Article Twelve, Isabelle Morel is entitled to operate the Morel workshop under her late husband's guild mark for a period not exceeding two years, provided she employs at least one licensed journeyman and submits to the guild's quarterly quality inspection.

Duchamp adds a finding: the guild council's denial of the transfer was improper. The council does not have authority to override a statute the membership has not amended. The provost orders the guild to enter the transfer in its rolls within ten days.

He notes that the amendment passed by the membership is valid for future cases. Any widow seeking to invoke Article Twelve after the date of the amendment will be subject to the six-month limit. But Isabelle Morel's claim predates the amendment and is governed by the original text.

Thibault receives the ruling at the guild hall. He reads it to the council and instructs the guild clerk to prepare the transfer. The clerk enters Isabelle Morel's name in the guild rolls the following day, with the notation: *widow's license, Article Twelve, expiring two years from date of entry, subject to quarterly inspection.*

Isabelle returns to the workshop and tells the journeymen. The presses do not stop. The cathedral chapter's commission ships on time.
