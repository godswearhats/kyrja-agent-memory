## Re-arc-Co3-1

**Scenario.** A junior data analyst at a logistics firm discovers that her manager has been falsifying safety-compliance figures submitted to a federal regulator — figures that, if accurate, would have triggered an immediate fleet audit. When a whistleblower complaint she files internally is intercepted and suppressed, a senior compliance officer at the parent company steps in to route the disclosure directly to the regulator before the falsified submission can be certified.

> A = Miriam Paez, Group Compliance Officer, Hartwell Logistics Group; B = Dana Cho, junior analyst, Hartwell subsidiary; T = Director of Operations Kyle Renner, who has intercepted Dana's internal complaint and drafted a termination package to eliminate her before the submission certifies.

5 beats, chronological.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The open-plan floor of Hartwell's regional office, 7:40 a.m. Dana was at her workstation before the lights on her row had fully come up. She had run the reconciliation three times, changing nothing each time, because she kept hoping the output would change.

It didn't. The fleet utilisation figures in the draft submission were off by a factor that couldn't be rounding error: forty-one vehicles listed as compliant on the federal form, twenty-seven of them not cleared for highway operation since the Q3 brake inspection. She had the maintenance log. She had the submission draft. She had both open side by side on her second monitor, and the gap between them was not a clerical problem.

She flagged it to her manager through the internal ethics portal at 8:15, attaching the reconciliation. The portal confirmed receipt. By 9:30 the flag had been marked as reviewed and closed with a note: *Analyst error; figures verified by Operations.* Her access to the maintenance database was revoked at 10:00.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The conference room on the third floor, 11:15 a.m. Kyle Renner closed the door before Dana had fully sat down. He set a printed copy of her ethics filing on the table between them.

"You went to the portal," he said. "Not to me."

"The portal is the correct channel," she said.

"The portal routes to my office. Which means you went to my office without coming to my office first." He turned the printout face-down. "I've spent twelve years building this operation. I'm not going to have it reviewed by a federal examiner because a first-year analyst misread a maintenance log." He slid a single sheet across the table. "HR is processing your separation today. You'll have two weeks' severance and a standard reference. The NDA is a condition."

Dana looked at the sheet. She didn't pick it up.

"The submission certifies Friday," she said.

"Yes," he said. "It does."

**E3 (failed initial response).** The lobby of Hartwell's regional office, 12:45 p.m. Dana had called the regulator's public tip line from the stairwell and been placed on hold for twenty minutes before a recorded message instructed her to submit in writing. She had emailed from her personal account — attaching the reconciliation she had printed before her database access was cut — and received an auto-acknowledgement with a five-to-seven business-day response window.

Friday was three days away. She sat in the lobby with her temporary access badge already deactivated, her building card collecting in a manila envelope at the front desk, and tried to think of what else she had.

**E4 (preparation for intervention — S2 begins).** Miriam Paez's office at Hartwell Group headquarters, two hours later. The Group ran quarterly compliance sweeps of its subsidiaries, and Miriam had a standing alert on any ethics portal closure coded as analyst error — not because they were common, but because they were the category most likely to be something that wasn't analyst error.

She had pulled Dana's filing, the maintenance log, and the submission draft before lunch. She had also pulled Kyle Renner's personnel file and the submission history for the regional office. The previous three federal submissions had each been certified the same week as a Q-end performance review. Each one had passed without question.

She called the federal regulator's regional compliance desk directly — not the public line — and asked to speak to the examiner assigned to Hartwell's current submission cycle. She had his direct number from a prior audit. She told him she had a subsidiary submission that should not be certified before she had completed an internal review, and that she was sending him a hold request under the Group's regulatory liaison authority within the hour.

**E5 (outcome — S3).** The HR conference room of the regional office, 4:50 p.m. Miriam arrived with two people from the Group's legal team and asked to be shown to Kyle Renner's office. She did not go to HR first.

She met Dana in the lobby on her way out. Dana had the manila envelope with her badge and her printed reconciliation under one arm.

"I got your name from the portal close note," Miriam said. "I need you to leave me a number and not sign anything today."

"They have a separation agreement on my desk," Dana said.

"Don't sign it," Miriam said. "The submission has a hold on it as of two hours ago. The examiner will open a formal review Monday. You're a material witness in that process, which makes your NDA unenforceable as written." She handed Dana a card. "Call me tomorrow morning."

Dana looked at the card. Miriam was already walking toward the lift.

Kyle Renner's termination of access request for Dana's user accounts was still in the HR queue, unprocessed, when Miriam's legal team began their records hold at 5:15.

## Re-arc-Co3-2

**Scenario.** The founding CTO of a Series B startup is being pushed out by the board through a manufactured performance improvement plan — a process orchestrated by a new CEO who wants to replace him before a fundraising round. A board member who was not party to the plan discovers its procedural irregularities and moves to halt the termination before the CTO signs a severance agreement that would strip his equity.

> A = Sandra Voll, independent board member; B = Marcus Teel, co-founder and CTO; T = CEO Harrison Gault, who has filed a PIP with HR designed to vest cause for termination within thirty days and forfeit Marcus's founder equity under the vesting clawback clause.

4 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4.

**Note on beat order.** The sequence opens with Sandra's discovery and moves backward to establish the threat before returning to the intervention and its outcome.

**E3 (danger present — S1, depicted first).** Sandra's home office, a Sunday evening. She had been sent the board pack for the Tuesday call as a standard attachment. The PIP was listed under operational updates, two items below the updated cap table. She almost didn't read it.

The plan was twenty-two pages. The performance metrics were legitimate — response times, sprint velocity, architectural documentation. What was not legitimate was the dates. The metrics ran from February to April. Marcus had been on approved medical leave from January through March. Fourteen of the twenty-two cited incidents fell within a period when he had been formally absent with board-approved documentation, which meant the incidents either did not occur as described or had been attributed to a period when attribution was not possible.

She read the metrics twice. Then she went back to the cap table. Marcus held eighteen percent, fully vested except for the clawback clause, which activated on a cause termination. The round Harrison was raising was at a valuation that would put Marcus's stake at approximately four million dollars. Fully forfeited on cause.

She called Harrison. He did not answer. She called the board chair. He said he'd seen the PIP and it looked thorough.

**E1 (danger escalation, depicted second).** The HR director's office, the previous Thursday — four days before Sandra's discovery. Marcus had been handed the PIP in a meeting he had been told was a product roadmap review. Harrison was there with HR and outside counsel.

"This is a thirty-day plan," Harrison said. "The metrics are objective. Legal reviewed the clause. If the plan benchmarks aren't met, HR processes the cause termination and the clawback activates automatically under your employment agreement."

Marcus looked at the metrics. He had not seen most of the incidents referenced.

"Half of these are from Q1," he said. "I was on leave."

"The responsibility for architectural delivery doesn't pause for leave," Harrison said. "You had a designated deputy. The gaps in delivery are documented."

"I want to see the underlying incident logs."

"You'll have access to your own PIP documentation under the process guidelines. The logs are internal operational records and aren't covered under that access." Harrison set the signature page on the desk. "The plan begins Monday whether or not you sign. Signing acknowledges receipt, not agreement."

Marcus did not sign. He left the building and called a lawyer, who told him the clause was enforceable as written and that the thirty-day window had already started.

**E2 (preparation for intervention — S2 begins, depicted third).** The board chair's home, Monday morning. Sandra was there at eight, which was earlier than the chair had expected.

"The incidents in the February-to-April window are fabricated or misdated," she said. "Marcus was on leave with board approval. I have the leave documentation in the Q1 board pack. You approved it yourself."

The chair looked at the pages she had printed. "Harrison said legal reviewed it."

"Harrison's counsel reviewed it. Not the board's. And the procedural rules for a cause PIP require a board majority, not CEO discretion — it's in the governance charter, section nine." She set the charter excerpt on the table beside the leave documentation. "This PIP was filed without board vote. It's invalid under the documents that govern it."

"Harrison will push back."

"Harrison can push back at a board meeting. I want an emergency session called for Wednesday and the PIP suspended pending that meeting. I need your name on the notice or I'll call it myself under my board rights, and it'll look worse for everyone."

The chair picked up the charter excerpt. He read it for a long time. Then he picked up his phone.

**E4 (outcome — S3, depicted fourth).** The conference call, Wednesday morning. All five board members were on. Harrison was not on the board; he was present at the board's invitation.

Sandra presented the procedural grounds: the PIP had been filed without a board vote, as required under section nine of the governance charter for any cause process that triggered a founder equity clawback. The February-to-April incident dates fell within a board-approved medical leave. The PIP was procedurally defective and could not be enforced as filed.

Harrison said the CEO had authority to manage performance independently of the board.

"For standard performance management, yes," Sandra said. "Not for processes that activate a founder equity clawback. The charter is specific. You needed a majority vote. You didn't take one."

"Call a vote now," Harrison said.

"We will," Sandra said. "But not on a defective filing. If you want to re-initiate a cause process, you start over with valid documentation and a proper board vote." She addressed the chair. "I move to suspend the current PIP pending a formal review of its procedural validity."

The chair seconded. The vote was three to one, with Harrison's proxy board member dissenting. The PIP was suspended. Marcus's thirty-day clock stopped. His equity position was intact.

Harrison resigned six weeks later, before the fundraising round closed.

## Re-arc-Co3-3

**Scenario.** A senior accountant at a mid-size construction firm discovers that invoices are being forged under her name and submitted for payment — a fraud scheme run by the firm's controller that is about to be discovered in an external audit, at which point the paper trail will point entirely to her. The external auditor, recognising the inconsistency in the signature patterns, intervenes before the audit findings are filed.

> A = Priya Nkemdirim, senior partner at the external audit firm; B = Rosario Vega, senior accountant; T = Controller David Bast, who has been forging invoice approvals in Rosario's name and will direct blame to her once the audit findings surface.

6 beats, chronological, free indirect style.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** It was the second week of the external audit, and Rosario had been answering questions about the accounts payable cycle for three days without understanding exactly what was being asked. The auditors wanted originals of things she had already given them. They kept returning to the same vendor file — Meridian Subcontracting, Q2 and Q3 — and asking whether she had personally reviewed and approved the invoice set. She said she had, because the approval trail showed her login credentials and her e-signature on each one.

She believed this. She had no reason not to believe it. She had approved hundreds of Meridian invoices in the ordinary course of her work. The dates in the approval trail matched her working days. The amounts were within her authorization threshold. Everything looked like something she might have done.

She didn't notice, and had no means to notice, that Meridian Subcontracting had been dissolved eight months ago.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The controller's office. David Bast was reviewing the audit request list with a yellow highlighter when his assistant brought him the afternoon request batch. He read through it without expression, set the highlighter down, and asked his assistant to close the door.

The auditors had pulled the vendor registration records. They would find the dissolution date. They would match it against the invoice approvals. The invoice approvals had Rosario's credentials because he had cloned her access token six months ago when she had been on a two-week leave and he had needed to run disbursements without her involvement. He had kept using the clone because it was simpler. There were forty-seven invoices. All of them were in her name. None of the disbursements appeared in the official accounts payable ledger; they routed to a vendor subaccount he had set up and controlled.

He began drafting a memo to HR flagging Rosario for a disciplinary review related to the Meridian account discrepancies.

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2 begins).** Priya Nkemdirim had been doing external audits for nineteen years, and the thing that was wrong with the Meridian approvals was the typing cadence. She had pulled the metadata on the e-signature files during the evening batch — a habit she had developed on an insurance fraud case years before -- and the keystroke intervals in the approval fields were uniform in a way that human typing was not. Uniform to within twelve milliseconds across forty-seven separate approvals, as though each one had been produced by the same macro.

Human beings didn't type like that. Not even people with good habits and consistent practice. The approvals had been automated.

She pulled Rosario's other approvals for the same period -- legitimate ones, on vendor files that showed no irregularities -- and ran the same analysis. The cadence was different: varied, faster in some fields and slower in others, with the rhythm of a person working through a routine task with partial attention.

She went back to the Meridian set. Forty-seven approvals. Same macro. Someone had generated them in Rosario's name.

She set the analysis aside and began building a separate documentation file, kept outside the main audit record, before she decided what to do with it.

**E4 (failed initial response).** The audit team room, the following morning. Priya brought the cadence analysis to her audit manager, who looked at it for a long time and then said he thought they should surface the discrepancy in the draft findings and let the client respond. That was the standard process.

"The standard process routes the draft findings to the controller's office for management response," Priya said. "David Bast is the controller. If he's the one who generated these approvals, he'll use the management response window to prepare a position."

"We don't know it was him."

"We know it wasn't Rosario Vega. The analysis is conclusive. If we send the draft to the controller, we're giving the person who likely did this three weeks to construct a response that puts the blame on the person who didn't."

The manager said he needed to consult with the engagement partner before deviating from the standard process. He would do that by end of week.

**E5 (intervention — S2).** Priya contacted the firm's audit engagement partner directly that afternoon, citing independence grounds -- a finding that implicated the client's controller required escalation above the standard management response loop. The partner reviewed her analysis and authorised her to deliver the cadence finding and the vendor dissolution records directly to the company's audit committee chair, bypassing the controller's office entirely.

Priya met with the audit committee chair the following morning. She presented the metadata analysis, the Meridian vendor dissolution record, the disbursement routing to the subaccount, and a preliminary timeline that placed the first anomalous approval seven months earlier -- four weeks before Rosario had taken her leave and her access token had last been used from her own device's MAC address.

The audit committee chair asked her to hold the draft findings pending an emergency audit committee session. She agreed. She also asked, before she left, that Rosario Vega be placed on paid administrative leave rather than disciplinary hold, pending the committee's determination. The chair agreed to this as well.

**E6 (outcome — S3).** The audit committee room, three days later. David Bast did not attend. He had been escorted from the building the previous evening after the IT forensics team, engaged by the committee at Priya's recommendation, confirmed that the access token clone had been generated from his workstation.

The audit committee chair called Rosario directly. She was at home, having been told four days ago that she was under review for invoice fraud. The chair told her the IT findings had established that her access credentials had been cloned without her knowledge and that all forty-seven approvals had been generated without her involvement. The disciplinary hold was rescinded. She would be notified in writing by end of business.

Rosario sat with the phone in her hand for a moment after the call ended.

The audit findings, when finalised, listed the fraud under the controller's access credentials and noted that the firm's senior accountant had been identified as a victim of the scheme rather than a participant in it.

## Re-arc-Co3-4

**Scenario.** A product manager at a SaaS company is blamed for a data breach that leaked customer PII — a breach caused by a vendor integration she had flagged as insecure six months earlier, whose risks had been overruled by engineering leadership. When the incident response process begins routing accountability to her, the CISO intercepts the internal communications and moves to correct the record before the customer notification and regulatory filing misattribute responsibility.

> A = Delphine Okafor, Chief Information Security Officer; B = Iris Yuen, Product Manager; T = VP of Engineering Conrad Metz, who is directing the incident response narrative to position Iris's product specification as the root cause, shielding his own team's decision to override her security objections.

5 beats, present tense.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The incident command room is a glass-walled conference space on the fourth floor, and it has been occupied for thirty-six hours. The breach is contained; the scope is defined: forty-three thousand customer records, PII including email, name, and partial billing address, exfiltrated through a misconfigured API endpoint in the vendor integration stack.

The incident timeline on the whiteboard shows the integration going live in March, the first anomalous API call in October, and the confirmed exfiltration window running eight days. Conrad is presenting the root cause analysis to the executive team. His slide shows the product specification as the origin of the misconfiguration: the spec called for a broad data-sharing scope with the vendor API, the engineering team implemented to spec, and the scope was the vulnerability.

Iris is in the room. She looks at the slide. The spec on the whiteboard is version one, from February. Version two, submitted in April, had narrowed the data-sharing scope to the minimum required fields and included a note flagging the original scope as a security risk. Conrad's team had responded to version two by reverting to version one in the implementation, on the grounds that the narrow scope would require an additional development sprint.

The current slide shows version one.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The legal team's draft of the customer notification and regulatory filing arrives in Delphine's inbox that afternoon. She is copied as CISO for review. The root cause section attributes the breach to a product specification that called for unnecessarily broad vendor data-sharing access. The product team is identified as the specification owner.

She opens the incident folder and looks for the product spec. There is one version there: version one. She searches the document management system for Iris's name and the vendor integration project. There are twelve documents. Version two of the spec is the third one. It is dated April, two months before the integration went live. The security flag is in section four, clearly labelled.

She looks back at the filing draft. Version two is not cited.

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2).** Delphine pulls the engineering review thread from the project management system. It takes her forty minutes to locate because the thread has been archived. She finds Conrad's reply to Iris's version two spec: a one-line comment noting that the narrow data scope would require additional sprint capacity and that the team was proceeding with the original implementation to meet the launch deadline. Iris's response, dated the same day, reads: *I want to flag formally that this overrides my security recommendation. Please confirm this decision is approved at VP level.* Conrad's reply: *Confirmed. We'll manage the risk in the next sprint.*

The next sprint had been spent on a different feature. There was no subsequent security review of the vendor integration before the breach.

Delphine copies the engineering thread, version two of the spec, and the confirmation exchange into a separate document. She marks it as CISO review material and sets a calendar item for the regulatory filing review session, which is scheduled for tomorrow morning.

She also sends a message to the legal lead asking that the filing not be finalised before that session.

**E4 (confrontation — S2 active).** The regulatory filing review session. Conrad arrives with his lead engineer. Delphine puts the version two spec and the engineering thread on the table.

"The filing attributes root cause to the product specification," she says. "The product manager submitted a version two spec in April that narrowed the data scope and flagged the security risk. Engineering overrode it and implemented version one. I need that reflected in the root cause analysis before we file."

Conrad says the product spec is the governing document for the implementation. The engineering team implemented what the product team specified.

"They implemented version one after the product team submitted version two and explicitly flagged the scope as a risk," Delphine says. "Your reply is in writing. You confirmed the override at VP level."

"Scope decisions are engineering decisions. She specified a scope. We evaluated it and made a call."

"You made a call that was flagged as a security risk by the specification owner, in writing, before the integration went live. That is material to the root cause. If we file without it, we are misrepresenting the incident to the regulator." She turns to the legal lead. "I can't sign off on the CISO review with the filing as written."

The legal lead looks at both of them. "I need the CISO's sign-off to file," he says. "We'll hold until we resolve this."

Conrad's jaw tightens. He does not respond.

**E5 (outcome — S3).** The revised filing goes out the following afternoon. The root cause section now describes the breach as arising from an engineering implementation decision that overrode a security recommendation in the product specification, made under schedule pressure and without a subsequent security review. Iris Yuen is identified as the specification owner who had flagged the risk in writing prior to the integration launch.

Iris is notified of the change by the legal team. She is in a one-on-one with her manager when the email comes in. She reads it twice.

Her manager, who has been watching her, says: "Good."

She doesn't answer immediately. The version two spec has been in the document management system for eight months. No one had looked at it until yesterday.

"Yes," she says finally. "I suppose it is."
