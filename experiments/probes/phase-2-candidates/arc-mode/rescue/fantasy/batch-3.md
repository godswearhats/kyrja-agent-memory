## Re-arc-Fa3-1

**Scenario.** A young herbalist's apprentice has been accused of poisoning the lord's steward — a charge manufactured by the lord's chancellor, who needs a body to take blame for his own subterfuge. A travelling physician of the Healer's Compact, arriving at the manor to attend the steward's sickbed, recognises the true cause of the illness and intervenes before the apprentice can be tried and hanged.

> A = Physician Aldeth of the Healer's Compact; B = Tomas, herbalist's apprentice; T = Chancellor Orren Vaes, who has framed Tomas for a poisoning he committed himself using a compound no apprentice would have access to.

5 beats, chronological, free indirect style.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The holding cell beneath the manor's stable block was three paces wide and smelled of old straw, and Tomas had been in it for two days. He had been given water twice. He had been given no account of what he was accused of, precisely, except that the steward had fallen gravely ill after a meal at which Tomas had served the table, and that the herbalist's boy was the most convenient explanation.

He knew he hadn't done anything. He also knew that this fact had no particular weight in the chancellor's hall. The chancellor had looked at him during the brief examination with an expression of mild distaste, as though Tomas were an inconvenience that would be resolved shortly. The trial, such as it would be, was set for the morning of the following day. The lord was travelling; he would return in three days; the chancellor had authority to convene a manorial court in his absence.

Tomas was seventeen years old. He had been apprenticed to Mistress Elara for four years and had never prepared a compound stronger than a willow-bark fever draught. He sat in the straw and tried to think of what he might say, and found nothing that would matter.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The chancellor's chamber, the previous evening. Orren Vaes was reading the court schedule for the following morning with the satisfaction of a man who had arranged things correctly. The steward would recover -- the compound Vaes had administered was designed to incapacitate, not kill, which was a tedious constraint but a necessary one. A dead steward would require an inquest, and an inquest would require documentation that Vaes preferred not to generate. A gravely ill steward, attended by sympathy, was much more useful. The steward's illness would confirm the poisoning charge. The apprentice's execution would close the matter.

The boy had been at the table. His master's herbs were the only source of botanical compounds in the household. The logic was simple enough to be persuasive and simple enough to preclude detailed examination, which was the quality Vaes valued most in an explanation.

He set the schedule down and poured himself wine. The physician from the Compact had arrived that afternoon -- summoned by the steward's wife before the accusation was formulated, in that early window when the illness was still mysterious. Vaes had not anticipated a physician. He would need to manage the examination carefully.

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2 begins).** The steward's chamber, that same evening. Aldeth of the Healer's Compact had been a physician for thirty-one years, and the first thing she noticed about the steward's illness was the symmetry of it. The presenting signs were consistent with wortbane poisoning: the trembling, the contracted pupils, the elevated pulse, the specific quality of the sweating. All of it consistent, and all of it too consistent. Wortbane poisoning was variable. It depended on dose, body weight, time of ingestion, the form of preparation. It did not present identically in every particular across a six-hour window in a man of the steward's age and constitution.

She asked to see the compound the herbalist's apprentice had allegedly administered. She was told no compound had been recovered. She asked what the basis of the accusation was. She was told the apprentice had served at table. She asked whether any other guest had shown symptoms. She was told no.

She sat with this for a time, watching the steward's breathing. The sweating had stabilised in the last hour. The trembling had not worsened. The illness was not progressing.

She went to her satchel and drew out her casebook and began writing.

**E4 (confrontation — S2).** The chancellor's receiving room, the following morning, an hour before the court was to convene. Aldeth set her casebook on his desk open to the relevant pages.

"I will not certify that this illness is consistent with poisoning by a compound available to an herbalist's apprentice," she said. "The symptom presentation is consistent with drosswort tincture, which is not a botanical compound. It is a distillate. It requires equipment and knowledge your apprentice does not have access to and almost certainly does not possess."

"The Compact's physician is welcome to present her observations to the court," Vaes said. "The court will weigh them."

"The court you are convening without the lord's presence, on a schedule you set two days after the accusation. I am asking you to postpone until the lord returns."

"I have full authority to convene in the lord's absence. The court will proceed."

"Then I will appear before it and state what I have told you. And I will also note that drosswort is a compound found in distillers' stores and apothecary stock, which is to say in the kind of place a chancellor has access to and an apprentice does not." She closed the casebook. "You may prefer a delay."

Vaes looked at her for a long moment. The Healer's Compact held charters in four of the seven provinces. A Compact physician's testimony before a manorial court was admissible and, more to the point, was recorded in the Compact's own registers, which were maintained independently of any lord's archive.

"The court will convene at noon rather than the ninth bell," he said. "That will allow time for a fuller review of the evidence."

He did not meet her eyes when he said it.

**E5 (outcome — S3).** The court did not convene at noon. By the eleventh bell, a rider had been dispatched to recall the lord, and the chancellor had presented to the steward's wife a revised account of the morning's proceedings that attributed the delay to the physician's insistence on further examination. By midday the following day, when the lord arrived, the court schedule had been quietly removed from the hall's record.

The lord reviewed Aldeth's casebook. He called her before him in the great hall and asked her to state her conclusions plainly. She did: the illness was consistent with drosswort, not with anything an herbalist's apprentice could compound; the boy should be released pending a full inquiry into the sources of drosswort within the household.

The lord ordered Tomas freed that afternoon. He also ordered the chancellor's stores examined. What the examination found is recorded in the lord's own accounts, which are a different matter.

Aldeth spent one more night at the manor. When she left the following morning, she stopped at the stable block and told Tomas, who was cleaning tack, that his master would be expecting him. He thanked her. She said it was the Compact's function to produce accurate diagnoses.

She rode out without looking back.

## Re-arc-Fa3-2

**Scenario.** During a contested succession to the Warden's seat of a border fortification, a senior cartographer is targeted for assassination by the faction that fears his detailed maps of the fortress's hidden passages will undermine their candidate's control. A garrison sergeant who discovers the assassination plot moves to extract the cartographer from the fortress before the faction's agents can reach him.

> A = Sergeant Brynn Ash, garrison; B = Orvyn Chalk, senior cartographer; T = Faction agent Haval and his two companions, under orders from claimant Seren Doss to kill Chalk before the succession hearing.

4 beats, non-linear: E2 → E1 → E3 → E4.

**Note on beat order.** Opens with the sergeant discovering the plot, returns to establish the danger, then follows the extraction and its resolution.

**E2 (danger present — S1, depicted first).** Brynn heard it in the mess, which was where she heard most things that mattered. A supply carter had been drinking with one of Seren Doss's household men, and the household man had said something about the cartographer that he hadn't intended to say loudly but had. Brynn was at the far end of the table with her back to them, and she had nineteen years of listening to conversations she wasn't part of.

The cartographer was to be dealt with before the succession hearing. That was the phrase. *Dealt with*. She had heard it used to mean many things, but the household man's voice had had a specific quality -- the flatness of someone repeating an instruction rather than expressing an opinion -- that told her what it meant tonight.

She set down her cup and left the mess quietly. The cartographer's workroom was on the upper floor of the east tower, which was a long way from the garrison billet. She did not run, because running drew attention, but she moved quickly.

**E1 (danger escalation, depicted second).** The east tower stairwell was narrow and cold, and Brynn took it two steps at a time. She had her service knife on her belt and her crossbow over her shoulder, which was regulation for a sergeant on night watch, which she was not. She had come off watch at the eighth bell.

At the third landing she heard voices below her -- outside, through the arrow loop, in the inner courtyard. Three men, moving without lanterns. Moving in the direction of the east tower door.

She went up faster.

**E3 (intervention — S2).** The cartographer was at his table when she came in without knocking. He looked up from his work, startled -- a slight man in his fifties with ink on his forearms and a piece of measuring string between his fingers.

"You need to leave this room," she said. "Now. Take the maps you can carry and nothing else."

"I don't understand. Who are--"

"Sergeant Brynn Ash, garrison. There are three men crossing the courtyard. They have orders from the Doss faction. Take the maps."

He looked at her. Something in his face shifted -- not surprise, she thought, but recognition. As though this was a thing he had considered might happen and had not permitted himself to fully believe.

He took the maps. She had him through the workroom's secondary door -- a servant's passage she had learned the location of during her first month in the garrison, because learning secondary passages was the habit that had kept her alive this long -- before the sound of boots reached the landing outside.

The passage led to the north wall, and from the north wall to the postern gate, and from the postern gate to the road.

**E4 (outcome — S3).** The road was dark and the night was cold and Orvyn Chalk had an armful of map cases and no outdoor clothing, which was an inconvenience and not a crisis. Brynn had a garrison cloak she gave him and directions to the shrine keeper's house half a mile down the road, which was a place she knew and the faction's men did not.

"You'll be safe at the shrine until morning," she said. "I'll report the intrusion to the garrison captain. When the succession hearing convenes, the Warden-elect will need to know what happened tonight."

"Which claimant do you support?" he asked.

"None of them," she said. "I support the garrison and I support accurate maps. Both of those require you to be alive." She turned him toward the road. "Walk quickly."

She went back through the postern gate. In the east tower, she found the workroom door had been broken open and the table overturned. No maps remained on the table -- they were in Orvyn's arms on the road -- and the three men had found nothing worth taking. They were gone before she reached the garrison captain's office.

The succession hearing was held six days later. Orvyn Chalk attended, carrying his maps.

## Re-arc-Fa3-3

**Scenario.** A temple archivist has been drawn into a debt bond by a moneylender who has exploited a clerical error in the temple's own records to claim ownership of a property that has been in the archivist's family for three generations. When the moneylender moves to enforce the bond and seize the property, the temple's senior canonist uncovers the original error and files a corrective registration before the bond can be executed.

> A = Canonist Deva Sorn, Temple of the Recorded Word; B = Archivist Maret Finn; T = Moneylender Hol Thress, who holds a fraudulently obtained debt bond secured against Maret's family property and is moving to execute it through the municipal registrar.

5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The temple archivist's workroom was small and cluttered and smelled of old vellum, and Maret Finn was at her reading-desk when Deva Sorn knocked and came in without waiting.

"I need to see the Thress bond," Deva said. "The one you told me about last week."

Maret found it in the second drawer. She had looked at it many times. "The debt's not in dispute," she said. "He loaned me the money for my brother's surgery and I have the obligation in writing. The question is the security. He says the house is registered in his name as collateral."

"And you say it isn't."

"It isn't. It's been in my family since my grandmother's time. I have the grant deed."

"He has the municipal registration."

"Yes," Maret said. "He does. Which is the problem."

Deva sat down across from her and looked at the bond. "Walk me through what happened with the registration."

"There was a survey error six years ago. The municipal surveyor drew the ward boundaries wrong on the revised map and our property was accidentally absorbed into the adjacent parcel. My father reported it and was told it would be corrected. It was not corrected. Hol Thress purchased the adjacent parcel last year. When he made me the loan, I didn't know the error had never been fixed. He did."

Deva looked up from the bond. "He knew the property was under his registration when he made you the loan."

"I believe so. He's moving to execute next week. The registrar will honour the bond because it's properly executed. Nobody will question whether the registration underlying it is valid."

"Somebody will," Deva said, and stood up. "I need the grant deed and the survey error report your father filed. Do you have them here?"

"They're at home."

"Bring them tomorrow morning. Early."

**E2 (danger escalation).** The following morning, Maret arrived at the temple with a leather case. Deva was already at her own desk with the municipal registration ledger open in front of her. The ledger was not the sort of document the temple normally held -- the Temple of the Recorded Word maintained copies of registration filings as part of its archival function, which was why Deva had been able to pull it overnight.

"The survey error was filed and received by the registrar's office," Deva said. "It's in the ledger. Your father's complaint. Six years ago. With a notation that says 'referred for correction.' Below that, nothing. No correction was made and no record of why it wasn't made."

She turned the ledger to show Maret. The entry was two lines in the registrar's hand and then silence.

"He'll say the correction was never filed," Maret said.

"He'll say exactly that. Which is why we're going to file it today."

"Can we do that? After six years?"

"We can file a corrective registration on the basis of a prior error at any point while the original error is still in the record. It's in the temple's founding charter -- we're the authorised correction body for any registration derived from survey data." Deva picked up the grant deed and turned it over in her hands. "This predates the survey by forty years. There's no legitimate basis for the adjacent parcel to have absorbed it. The error is on its face. I can file the correction this morning."

"Thress is executing next week."

"We'll be at the registrar's office in an hour."

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2).** The registrar's outer office was busy. Deva had brought two temple clerks and a letter of authority from the temple's senior canon, which Maret had not known she was going to obtain but which, evidently, Deva had collected at dawn. The clerks carried a copy of the founding charter, the survey error complaint, the original grant deed, and the corrective registration form drafted in the temple's formal hand.

"This will take time," the registrar's assistant told Deva.

"It will take the time it takes," Deva said. "I am filing a corrective registration under the temple's charter authority. The filing is valid on presentation. Whether the registrar processes it immediately or not, it enters the record upon filing."

The assistant went to get the registrar.

**E4 (confrontation — S2 active).** Hol Thress arrived at the registrar's office twenty minutes later, which told Maret that someone had sent him word. He was a compact man with a quality of stillness that she had always found unpleasant.

"The bond is properly executed," he said. "Any challenge to the underlying registration is a separate proceeding."

"The corrective registration is already in the record," Deva said. "Filed eighteen minutes ago. The property is now correctly registered in Maret Finn's name on the basis of a prior survey error that was reported to this office six years ago and never addressed."

"The bond was executed in good faith under the existing registration."

"Under an erroneous registration that you had notice of," Deva said. "The survey error complaint is in the municipal ledger. Your purchase of the adjacent parcel was six months after that complaint was filed. You acquired property with a recorded dispute attached to it." She addressed the registrar directly. "The bond cannot be executed against property the bondholder did not legitimately hold as security. I am asking you to confirm that execution is suspended pending review of the corrective registration."

The registrar looked at the filing, the ledger entry, and the grant deed. He looked at Thress.

"The execution is suspended," the registrar said. "Pending review."

Thress's face did not change. He looked at Deva. He looked at Maret. He said: "I will take this to the civic court."

"You're welcome to do so," Deva said. "We'll bring the same documents."

**E5 (outcome — S3).** The civic court review was scheduled for three weeks later. It did not result in a hearing. The judge reviewing the filings prior to the hearing date determined that the corrective registration was valid on its face, that the bondholder had constructive notice of the underlying survey dispute at the time of the loan, and that executing a bond against disputed property without disclosure voided the security clause. The bond principal remained due, but the property was no longer security for it.

Maret received the ruling by messenger at the temple, where she was at her desk. She read it twice. Then she took it across the hall to Deva's office and set it on her desk.

"Thank you," she said.

Deva looked at the ruling. "Thank whoever filed your father's complaint six years ago," she said. "Without the ledger entry, we'd have had a much harder morning."

Maret thought about her father, who had spent two weeks on that complaint and had told her afterward that the city never fixed anything.

"He'd be annoyed that it took this long," she said.

"Most corrections do," Deva said, and went back to her work.

## Re-arc-Fa3-4

**Scenario.** A junior smith at a weapons guild has been given charge of the master smith's private forge — and of the master smith's most dangerous current work: an unfinished blade commissioned for a political assassination. When the guild master's enforcer arrives to confiscate the blade and silence the junior smith as a witness, the master smith returns early and confronts the enforcer before the violence can proceed.

> A = Master Smith Corath Veld; B = Pell, junior smith; T = Guild Enforcer Davan, acting on orders from Guild Master Hrest to confiscate the blade and remove the only witness who has seen its commission details.

4 beats, present tense.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** Pell is at the secondary bellows when Davan comes through the forge door. The forge is small — private, separated from the guild hall's main smithing floor by a passage and two locked doors, one of which Pell has been given a key to and one of which Davan has apparently opened without one. This is the first thing wrong.

The second thing wrong is the commission ledger. Davan looks at it before he looks at anything else, which means he knows it's there, which means someone told him. Master Veld doesn't share the private forge's contents with the guild administration. He is very specific about this. Pell has been apprenticed to him for three years and has heard the instruction enough times to feel it in his chest: the private forge's ledger is not guild property, is not subject to guild inspection, and is not discussed outside this room.

Davan says: "Put down the tools and step back from the blade."

The blade is in its final tempering stage on the form rack. It is unfinished. It is also, Pell now understands, the reason the guild master wants it before Master Veld returns.

He puts down the tools. He steps back. He does not look at the ledger. He looks at the knife on Davan's belt and thinks about the passage behind him and the two locked doors, one of which Davan can apparently open and one of which Pell still has the key to.

**E2 (danger escalation).** Davan takes the ledger first. He pages through it with the efficiency of someone looking for a specific thing rather than reading. He finds it: the commission entry. He does not read it aloud. He sets the ledger in the leather satchel he has brought and buckles it closed.

Then he looks at Pell.

"You've been here alone for three days," he says. "You've read the commission."

It is not a question. Pell has read it, because the ledger sits open on the workbench and he has worked around it every day. He knows who commissioned the blade, and who it is intended for, and when delivery was expected.

"The guild master's concern," Davan says, "is that certain knowledge, held by the wrong people, becomes a liability." He says it in the way that a man recites something he has been told to say, in the voice of the institution rather than the voice of a man who has himself decided anything. "Master Veld is not expected to return until tomorrow."

There is a cabinet on the east wall with a draw-bolt. Pell is thinking about it.

**E3 (intervention — S2).** The forge door opens behind Davan. Master Veld is in the doorway, which should not be possible for another day, which means he returned early, which means something has changed.

He takes in the room in less than a second: the closed satchel, Davan's position, Pell against the wall.

"Give me the satchel," he says.

"The guild master has--"

"I don't operate under the guild master's authority in my private forge. You know this. The ledger is mine. The commission is mine. Give me the satchel."

Davan's hand moves toward his belt. Not toward the knife: toward the letter he has tucked there. The guild master's written order. He holds it out.

Master Veld takes it. He reads it. He folds it once and sets it on the workbench beside the blade form.

"The guild master's written order applies to guild property," he says. "This forge is not guild property. It is my personal workshop, held under a private charter that predates my membership in the guild. If Hrest wants to contest that, he is welcome to bring it before the guild tribunal." He meets Davan's eyes. "Leave the satchel. Leave the forge. Tell Hrest I will attend on him in the morning."

There is a long pause. Davan looks at Pell, then at Master Veld, then at the blade on the form rack.

He sets the satchel on the workbench. He leaves.

**E4 (outcome — S3).** Master Veld closes the forge door and sets the bar. He picks up the satchel, checks the ledger, and places it in the cabinet on the east wall. Then he turns to Pell.

"How long was he here before I arrived?"

"Ten minutes. Perhaps twelve."

"Did he touch the blade?"

"No. He went for the ledger first."

Master Veld nods. He looks at the commission entry, which Pell has been looking at for three days. He closes the ledger.

"The commission is cancelled," he says. "As of this morning. I sent the client a return of payment by rider from the north road." His voice is even. "The guild master knew because I informed him of the cancellation this morning. Apparently he drew his own conclusions about what remained to be managed."

Pell thinks about this. About what *managed* means.

"I didn't know about the cancellation," he says.

"No. You were here." Master Veld looks at him directly. "You have not read the commission entry. You do not know the client's name. You have been attending to the tempering work, which you have done correctly, and that is the full account of your time in this forge. Is that understood?"

Pell looks at the cabinet where the ledger is now locked.

"Yes," he says. "That's understood."
