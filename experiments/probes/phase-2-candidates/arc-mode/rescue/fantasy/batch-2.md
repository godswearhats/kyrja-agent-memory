# Rescue arc × Fantasy — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## Re-arc-Fa2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A court herbalist discovers that the city's aqueduct has been poisoned by a rival kingdom's agent, and the poison will reach the lower districts' cisterns by nightfall. The city watch commander dismisses the warning. The herbalist must convince the guild-mistress of the water-keepers to close the sluice gates before the tainted water reaches the population.

> A = court herbalist (Maren); B = population of the lower districts (thirty thousand residents who draw from the cisterns); T = alkaloid poison introduced into the aqueduct by an agent of the Veldmark court, reaching the cisterns by nightfall.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The herbalist's workshop in the palace undercroft, late afternoon. Maren had been testing the weekly water sample from the aqueduct intake -- a routine she performed every seventh day at the seneschal's request -- when the reagent turned the wrong colour. Not slightly wrong. Completely wrong. The bright violet of nightward root alkaloid, a compound she had catalogued in her training and never expected to see outside a poisoner's kit.

She repeated the test with a fresh sample. The same result. She tested for concentration and worked the arithmetic twice on her slate. At the levels present in the intake sample, the alkaloid would not kill immediately. It would take eight to twelve hours after ingestion. The symptoms -- cramping, blindness, organ failure -- would begin overnight, in the dark, in homes without physicians, in a district whose residents could not afford an apothecary.

She corked the sample, wrapped it in cloth, and climbed the stairs to the watch commander's office.

**E2 (failed initial response scene).** The watch commander's office, the same hour. Commander Aldric sat behind a desk covered in patrol rosters and district reports. Maren set the sample on his desk and explained the findings: nightward root alkaloid in the aqueduct, concentration lethal within twelve hours, reaching the lower cisterns by nightfall.

"And you're certain of the compound?" Aldric said.

"I ran the test twice. The colour is unmistakable."

"The aqueduct draws from a mountain spring twelve leagues north. How does a poison enter a sealed channel?"

"Through the inspection hatches. There are six between the spring and the city wall. Any one of them would be sufficient."

Aldric leaned back. "I have forty men on watch tonight. Half of them are assigned to the harbour quarter because the merchant guild filed a complaint about warehouse theft. If I pull patrols to shut down the water system on the word of one test from one herbalist, and the test is wrong, the seneschal will have my commission."

"If the test is right and the cisterns are not closed, you will have thirty thousand people drinking poisoned water by morning."

"Bring me a second opinion. Another herbalist, the guild physician, someone with standing. I'll act on a confirmed result from two sources. I won't act on one."

Maren looked at the sample on his desk. She looked at the window. The sun was perhaps three hours from setting. The guild physician was across the city, and she did not know if he was in residence. She picked up the sample and left.

**E3 (preparation for intervention scene).** The street of the water-keepers' guild, one hour later. Maren had considered her options as she crossed the city. The guild physician might confirm the test, but the time required -- finding him, explaining, re-testing, returning to Aldric -- would consume the remaining daylight. The poison would reach the cisterns before the commander received his second opinion.

The water-keepers' guild controlled the sluice gates between the main aqueduct channel and the lower-district cisterns. The guild-mistress, Ulla, had the authority to close those gates for maintenance without the watch commander's approval. It was a guild prerogative, jealously guarded, older than the current city charter.

Maren had treated Ulla's daughter for a fever two winters ago. She did not know whether that constituted enough trust to demand that Ulla shut down the water supply to thirty thousand people on an herbalist's word. She knocked on the guild-hall door.

**E4 (intervention scene).** The guild-hall's testing chamber, a stone room built over the main sluice junction. Ulla had admitted her without delay and listened to the explanation in silence. When Maren finished, Ulla did not ask for a second opinion. She asked for the sample.

"Test it yourself," Maren said, handing her the corked bottle. "Your guild keeps reagent stock. I'll walk you through the procedure."

Ulla set the sample on the bench, took a vial of detection reagent from the locked cabinet, and followed Maren's instructions. When the liquid in the testing dish turned violet, Ulla stared at it for a long moment.

"How long until it reaches the lower cisterns?"

"Two hours. Perhaps less, depending on flow."

Ulla crossed the room to the sluice-gate mechanism -- a system of iron wheels and counterweights built into the wall. She turned to the two journeymen who had been watching from the doorway.

"Close the lower gates. All six. Crossbar and padlock each one. No water passes to the cisterns until I give the order."

The elder journeyman hesitated. "Mistress, thirty thousand people lose their water tonight. The district aldermen will be at the door by--"

"Then they will find me here. Close the gates."

The journeymen descended to the sluice level. Maren heard the iron wheels turning, one after another -- six heavy groans of metal on stone. Ulla stood at the top of the stairs and counted each one.

**E5 (outcome / cost of intervention scene).** The guild-hall's main room, the following morning. Maren arrived at dawn to find Ulla seated at the long table with the district aldermen already assembled. Three of them were shouting. The lower districts had been without water for fourteen hours. Residents had been queueing at the river since first light, drinking untested water from an open source that carried its own risks.

Ulla laid the testing dish on the table -- the violet stain still visible in the ceramic. She explained the compound, the concentration, and the timeline. She told them that if the cisterns had not been closed, the lower districts would have been drinking lethal water since midnight.

The seneschal arrived at midmorning with two of the palace's physicians and the guild physician Aldric had wanted. They tested the aqueduct water independently. Both confirmed the alkaloid. The seneschal ordered a full inspection of the aqueduct's hatches. By noon, the watch had found the injection point -- hatch four, three leagues north of the wall, the lock forced, a residue of nightward root paste on the channel's inner lip.

The cisterns remained closed for four days while the aqueduct was flushed. The lower districts drew river water under the guild physician's supervision. Seven people fell ill from river parasites. None died. The seneschal's report, filed with the court the following week, estimated the number of deaths that would have resulted from the alkaloid at the measured concentration had the cisterns remained open: between eight hundred and two thousand.

Ulla's name appeared in the report. Maren's did not. She read the report in the palace archive, noted the omission, and returned to her workshop.

## Re-arc-Fa2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A temple archivist discovers that the binding ward on a sealed crypt beneath the temple is failing, and the entity imprisoned within will break free by the next moonrise. The high priest is absent on pilgrimage. The archivist must locate and perform the renewal ritual -- a task normally reserved for the high priest alone -- before the ward collapses entirely.

> A = temple archivist (Brother Edrin); B = the temple community and surrounding village (four hundred people within the entity's reach); T = a bound malevolent spirit (the Pallor) whose imprisoning ward is degrading and will fail by moonrise.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The archive beneath the Temple of the Sixth Threshold, an hour before dawn. Brother Edrin had come down to reshelf the solstice liturgies when he noticed the light. It came from beneath the inner door -- the one that led to the sealed stairway, the one that had not been opened in his twenty years of service. A cold, grey luminescence, pulsing in a rhythm that was almost but not quite regular, like a heartbeat losing its cadence.

He pressed his hand against the door. The wood was warm. It should not have been warm. The crypt below was carved into bedrock, and the sealed stairway held the temperature of deep earth year-round. He had read about this in the archive's oldest binding record -- a manuscript in the founder's hand, seven centuries old, describing the signs of ward degradation. Warmth at the threshold. Visible light where there should be none. A rhythm in the emanation that grew less steady as the binding weakened.

The binding record stated that ward renewal was to be performed by the high priest and no other, using the renewal ritual transcribed on folios 14 through 17 of the same manuscript. The high priest had departed on pilgrimage to the mountain shrines eleven days ago and was not expected to return for another nine. Brother Edrin stood at the door and felt the warmth against his palm and thought about nine days.

He went to the archive's astronomical tables and calculated the next moonrise. It was seventeen hours away.

**E2 (danger escalation scene).** The archive, midmorning. Edrin had spent the intervening hours reading the binding record from beginning to end. The entity sealed beneath the temple -- referred to in the manuscript only as the Pallor -- had been imprisoned by the temple's founder after it had killed thirty-one people in the village over a period of three days. The founder's account was clinical: the Pallor fed on warmth. It drained heat from living bodies in its vicinity. The thirty-one had died of a cold that came from within, their organs failing as their core temperature dropped below what flesh could sustain. The founder had bound it in the crypt using a ward that drew on the temple's consecrated ground and the lunar cycle. The ward required renewal every fifty years. The high priest had renewed it twice in the current era, most recently three years ago.

But the manuscript also noted that the ward's integrity depended on the structural condition of the crypt. Edrin recalled the earthquake that had shaken the valley five months ago -- mild on the surface, but strong enough to crack the foundation stones of the temple's east wall. He had written a repair requisition at the time. He had not thought about what it might have done to the crypt.

He returned to the inner door. The light was brighter now. The pulsing had become visibly irregular. He could feel the cold beginning to seep through the gap beneath the door -- not the cold of stone, but something else. Something that made his fingers ache as though the warmth were being pulled from them.

Seventeen hours had become fourteen.

**E3 (preparation for intervention scene).** The archive, early afternoon. Edrin had made his decision. The high priest was nine days away. The ward would fail tonight. If the renewal was not performed, the Pallor would be free by moonrise, and four hundred people -- the temple community and the village -- were within the radius the founder had described.

He was not a priest. He was an archivist. He had never performed a ritual of any kind. But he had the manuscript, and the manuscript contained the renewal ritual in full -- the incantations, the gestures, the materials, the sequence. The founder had written it as a procedure, not as a mystery. Step followed step. The materials were common: consecrated salt from the temple stores, oil from the sanctuary lamp, chalk from the teaching rooms, and the renewal key -- a bronze disc kept in the high priest's study.

Edrin gathered the materials methodically. The salt and oil were in the sacristy. The chalk was in the teaching rooms, as expected. The renewal key was in a locked drawer in the high priest's study. Edrin did not have the key to the drawer. He used a chisel from the repair shed and accepted that the high priest would judge him for it later.

He laid out the materials on the archive table and read the ritual through three more times. The incantation was in the old liturgical language, which he could read but had never spoken aloud. He practised the pronunciation against the phonetic guide the founder had included -- a small mercy from a practical mind seven centuries dead.

**E4 (intervention / outcome scene).** The sealed stairway and the crypt, late evening. Edrin opened the inner door. The cold hit him like a wall. The grey light filled the stairway, pulsing in long, arrhythmic surges that made the stone walls appear to breathe. He descended with the materials in a satchel and the manuscript open in his hands.

The crypt was a circular chamber, ten paces across, with the binding circle carved into the floor. The circle's lines were cracked -- three fractures running through the carved channels where the original binding medium had been poured. The earthquake. The grey light emanated from the fractures, and the cold was concentrated there, intense enough that Edrin's breath crystallised and fell as fine ice before it had travelled a foot from his lips.

He began the renewal. The manuscript specified the order: salt in the channels first, then oil, then the incantation spoken while walking the circle's perimeter clockwise. The renewal key was to be placed at the circle's centre and turned once at the final word.

His hands shook from the cold. The salt spilled twice and he gathered it from the floor and continued. The oil froze in the channels as he poured it and he had to warm the flask against his body between each section. The incantation was forty-two lines. He spoke them from the manuscript, slowly, checking his pronunciation against the phonetic guide. The old language felt strange in his mouth. He stumbled on the seventeenth line and started it again.

At the thirty-ninth line, the light in the fractures flared. The cold intensified until his vision narrowed and his fingers went numb around the manuscript. He finished the fortieth line, the forty-first, the forty-second. He knelt at the centre of the circle, placed the bronze disc in the carved depression, and turned it.

The light died. The cold withdrew -- not gradually, but all at once, as though something had been pulled back behind a closed door. The crypt was dark. Edrin knelt on the stone floor, shaking, unable to feel his hands, the manuscript on the ground beside him. The binding circle's channels glowed faintly with the residue of the oil and salt -- a warm amber, steady, without pulse.

He stayed in the crypt until his hands recovered enough to hold the manuscript. Then he climbed the stairs, closed the inner door, and sat on the archive floor until dawn. When the sub-prior found him there the next morning, he was asleep against the shelving with the renewal key still in his coat.

## Re-arc-Fa2-3

**6 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A trade-caravan's outrider spots a flash flood bearing down on a canyon where the caravan's wagons are camped for the night. The caravan master insists the canyon walls provide shelter from the storm. The outrider must convince enough of the teamsters to move the wagons to high ground before the water arrives.

> A = outrider (Seska); B = caravan (forty-three people, twelve wagons); T = flash flood descending through the canyon, arriving in approximately one hour.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The canyon rim, sunset. Seska sits her horse on the eastern ridge, a quarter league above the canyon floor where the caravan has circled for the night. She has ridden ahead to check the trail for the next day's march, and on her way back she stopped at the ridge to watch the sky.

The sky to the north is wrong. A wall of cloud, black at the base and lit green at the edges, sits across the upper valley like a dam. She can see the rain falling from it -- not ordinary rain but a solid grey curtain that erases the mountains behind it. The upper valley feeds three tributaries that converge six leagues above this canyon. The canyon is narrow, steep-walled, and dry tonight. It will not be dry for long.

She has seen this once before, four years ago, on a different route. That time the water came through a similar canyon in the dark and killed eight mules and a teamster who had been sleeping beside his wagon. The caravan master on that journey had camped on high ground. The water passed beneath them.

She turns her horse and rides for the canyon floor.

**E2 (failed initial response scene).** The canyon floor, dusk. The wagons are circled, the mules unhitched and picketed, cook-fires burning. Seska finds the caravan master, Yoren, sitting on a supply crate reviewing the manifest for tomorrow's delivery.

"There's a storm breaking in the upper valley," she says. "Heavy rain across the full headwater. The water will funnel through this canyon."

Yoren looks at the sky above the canyon walls. It is clear. Stars are beginning to show.

"The sky's clear here," he says.

"The storm is six leagues north. The tributaries converge above us. We won't see the water until it's in the canyon."

"We're sheltered here. The walls block wind, the floor is dry, and I've got forty-three people who've been walking since dawn. I'm not moving twelve wagons in the dark because of a storm you saw on the horizon."

"It's not on the horizon. It's in the headwater basin. I've seen this before --"

"And I've been running caravans through this pass for eleven years. This canyon floods in spring when the snowmelt comes. It doesn't flood from summer storms. We stay."

Seska looks at the cook-fires. She looks at the canyon walls, forty feet high, smooth stone, no ledges, no handholds, no way out once the water fills the floor. She looks at Yoren. He has returned to his manifest.

**E3 (danger escalation scene).** The canyon floor, one hour after dusk. Seska walks the perimeter of the camp. The air has changed. It carries a smell she recognises -- wet stone and river silt, though the nearest river is two leagues east. The mules are restless. Three of them are pulling at their picket stakes, ears flat, nostrils wide. Mules know water. Mules have always known water.

She kneels and puts her hand flat on the canyon floor. The rock is vibrating. Not from the wind, not from the mules. From upstream. A low, continuous tremor transmitted through the stone. She stands and walks to the narrowest point of the canyon, fifty paces north of the camp, and listens. She can hear it now -- a distant, sustained roar, like a crowd or a landslide, coming from the direction of the upper valley.

She estimates the sound. Four leagues away, perhaps less. The canyon narrows further two leagues upstream. The water will accelerate when it hits the narrows. She has perhaps one hour. Perhaps less.

**E4 (preparation for intervention scene).** The wagon circle, minutes later. Seska does not return to Yoren. She goes to the lead teamster, Pol, a man she has worked with on three previous journeys. She tells him what she heard. She tells him about the mules. She asks him to put his hand on the ground.

Pol kneels, touches the stone, and stands up without saying anything. He looks north, toward the narrows. Then he starts walking along the wagon line, stopping at each teamster's fire.

Seska hears the conversations -- fragments, carried on the wind. Some of the teamsters argue. Some look north. One of them, a woman named Dalla, stands immediately and begins hitching her mules. Two others follow Dalla. The momentum shifts when Pol reaches the seventh wagon and the teamster there -- an old man who has been running this route longer than Yoren -- puts his hand on the ground, stands, and says one word to his sons. They begin hitching.

Within twenty minutes, eight of the twelve wagons are hitching. Yoren emerges from his wagon to find half his caravan preparing to move. He shouts for Pol. Pol tells him to put his hand on the ground. Yoren does. His expression changes.

**E5 (intervention scene).** The trail to the eastern ridge, less than an hour before the flood. The caravan moves in the dark by lantern light. The trail climbs steeply from the canyon floor to a shelf of high ground fifty feet above the canyon rim -- a broad, flat area where another caravan has clearly camped before, its fire rings still visible in the rock.

Seska rides ahead of the column, checking the trail for obstacles. Pol drives the lead wagon. The mules are moving fast, faster than they should on a steep trail in the dark -- they need no encouragement. The roar from upstream is audible to everyone now. It is no longer a distant sound. It fills the canyon.

The last four wagons are the problem. Three of them belong to teamsters who hesitated longest, and the fourth is Yoren's own supply wagon, the heaviest in the caravan. The trail narrows at the halfway point and the supply wagon's axle catches on a rock outcrop. Yoren and two teamsters work the axle free while Seska holds a lantern. The roar is louder. She can feel the air pressure changing in the canyon below them -- a cold wind pushing ahead of the water.

The axle clears. The wagon lurches forward. The last four climb the final section of trail and reach the shelf.

**E6 (outcome scene).** The shelf above the canyon, the same night. The caravan is on high ground. Forty-three people, twelve wagons, all mules accounted for. Seska dismounts at the eastern edge of the shelf and looks down into the canyon.

The water arrives eleven minutes after the last wagon reaches the shelf. It does not rise gradually. It comes around the upstream bend as a wall -- brown, churning, carrying uprooted trees and rock debris. It fills the canyon floor in seconds. The fire rings where they had camped disappear. The picket stakes disappear. The water reaches a height of nine feet against the canyon walls, measured later by the silt line, and it sustains that height for over an hour.

The caravan watches from the shelf. No one speaks for a long time. The old teamster from the seventh wagon sits on the tongue of his wagon and watches the water and says nothing. Dalla's mules stand calmly, ears forward, as though they had known all along.

In the morning, when the water has receded, Seska rides down to the canyon floor. The silt is three inches deep. The camp site is unrecognisable. She finds one of the picket stakes, bent flat, half-buried in mud, four hundred paces downstream from where it had been driven.

She rides back up to the shelf. Yoren is waiting. He does not mention the previous evening's argument. He asks her to ride ahead and check whether the trail south is passable. She does.

## Re-arc-Fa2-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath/embedded document) -> E2 (danger present) -> E3 (danger escalation) -> E4 (intervention) -> E5 (outcome/cost), free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A mining-guild surveyor discovers that a gallery in the deep mines is about to collapse, trapping a crew of sixteen dwarven miners behind a failing rock face. The guild master has already sealed the gallery's upper entrance to protect the rest of the mine. The surveyor must find an alternate route and shore the failing section long enough to bring the crew through.

> A = guild surveyor (Torvin); B = sixteen miners trapped in the deep gallery; T = imminent gallery collapse (unstable hanging wall, propagating fracture system in the roof stone).

**E1 (aftermath -- embedded document, depicted first).** The guild-mistress's chamber, ten days later. The guild clerk reads the following from the incident ledger:

> *On the seventh of the month, Guild Surveyor Torvin reported an imminent roof collapse in Gallery Twelve of the deep workings. The guild master sealed the gallery's upper entrance to contain the failure, isolating sixteen miners beyond the collapse zone. Surveyor Torvin located the abandoned ventilation shaft (Survey Reference 9-East) and descended to a point below the gallery floor, then cut a lateral passage to reach the isolated crew. He shored the lateral passage with salvaged timber from the abandoned workings. All sixteen miners were brought through the lateral passage before the gallery roof failed. The roof collapse occurred fourteen hours after Torvin's initial report. The gallery is now impassable. Two of the sixteen miners sustained injuries during the extraction: one broken arm, one laceration requiring twelve stitches. Surveyor Torvin sustained frostbite to both hands from prolonged work in the deep-level temperature zone without warming intervals. The guild physician reports that full recovery of hand function is uncertain.*

The guild-mistress asks whether the survey records for the ventilation shaft were in the current archive. The clerk says they were not. They were in the decommissioned section, unfiled, in a crate that had not been opened in thirty years. The guild-mistress notes this for the record and instructs the clerk to integrate all decommissioned survey records into the active archive within the month.

**E2 (danger present scene).** Gallery Twelve, the seventh of the month, early shift. Torvin had been conducting a routine wall survey when he heard the first crack -- not the ordinary settling sounds that accompanied deep mining, but a sharp, tearing report that travelled the length of the gallery ceiling like a whip. He looked up. A fracture had opened in the hanging wall, running from the gallery's midpoint toward the far end. Fine dust sifted down from the crack in a continuous curtain.

He checked his survey instruments. The tilt gauge on the roof showed a deflection of two degrees toward the gallery's lower side -- a movement that had not been present when he set the gauge that morning. The fracture was propagating. The hanging wall was separating from the roof stone.

At the far end of the gallery, beyond the fracture zone, sixteen miners were working the new face. They were three hundred paces from where Torvin stood, past the section of roof that was coming apart. He could hear their hammers. They could not hear the crack. Sound in a mine travels poorly against the rhythm of working tools.

**E3 (danger escalation scene).** The gallery's upper entrance, thirty minutes later. Torvin had pulled back to the entrance and sent a runner to the guild master, Brenn. Brenn arrived with two of his wardens. Torvin showed them the fracture, the deflection reading, and the dust fall. Brenn looked at the ceiling for a long time.

Brenn's decision was immediate. He ordered the upper entrance sealed -- heavy timber barricade, braced against the gallery walls, designed to contain the debris if the roof came down. The seal would protect the rest of the mine. It would also close the only known route to the sixteen miners on the far side.

Torvin asked about the crew. Brenn said the collapse, when it came, would likely be confined to the gallery's midsection. The far end, where the crew was working, had different roof stone -- harder, better supported. They might survive the collapse itself. But they would be sealed behind it, with no way out through the rubble, and no other exit on the survey maps.

Torvin said he needed to check the decommissioned survey records. Brenn asked why. Torvin said the current maps showed no alternate route, but Gallery Twelve was cut adjacent to older workings that had been abandoned before the current survey system was established. There might be a connection that was never recorded on the active maps. Brenn told him he had until the roof came down.

**E4 (intervention scene).** The decommissioned archive and the deep workings, hours later. Torvin found the crate in the back of the archive -- thirty years of dust on the lid, survey sheets rolled and tied with cord that crumbled when he touched it. He spread seven sheets on the floor before he found what he needed: Survey Reference 9-East, an abandoned ventilation shaft that descended from the third level to a point twelve feet below the floor of Gallery Twelve, with a lateral drift marked "exploratory -- not developed" that ran roughly parallel to the gallery's far end.

The ventilation shaft was on the opposite side of the mine from the gallery entrance. Torvin descended it alone -- the shaft was too narrow for more than one person with timber. The temperature dropped sharply below the third level. His breath steamed and then froze on his beard. The decommissioned record had noted the cold but had not recorded a temperature. Torvin estimated it was well below freezing.

The lateral drift existed. It was four feet high, three feet wide, and ended in a rock face after sixty paces. The gallery floor was above him and to the right. He could hear the miners' hammers through the stone -- faintly, but present. The distance through the rock was perhaps ten feet.

He had brought tools and salvaged timber from the upper workings. He began cutting through the rock face, shoring each foot of new passage as he went. The work was slow. The cold stripped the heat from his hands faster than exertion replaced it. He worked without gloves because the chisel required grip precision. After the first two hours, he could no longer feel his fingers. He continued by watching his hands and correcting their position visually.

The passage broke through to the gallery floor after four hours. A miner's pick struck through from the other side -- the crew had heard his hammering and worked toward the sound. The hole widened. Torvin shored the junction with the last of his timber and began passing the miners through, one at a time, into the lateral drift and down to the ventilation shaft.

**E5 (outcome / cost scene).** The ventilation shaft's upper opening, third level. The last miner emerged from the shaft fourteen hours after Torvin's initial report. Behind them, somewhere in the deep workings, the sound of Gallery Twelve's roof finally giving way reached them as a sustained, low-frequency vibration that made the lanterns sway on their hooks.

Torvin sat against the shaft housing. His hands were white to the wrists, the skin hard and waxy. He could not open his fingers. The guild physician, who had been waiting at the shaft head, wrapped his hands in warmed cloth and began the slow work of restoring circulation. The pain, when it came, was considerable. Torvin did not speak during the treatment. The physician told Brenn, quietly, that the damage to the tendons was extensive and that the surveyor's ability to hold instruments -- to do the fine work that surveying required -- was uncertain.

The sixteen miners sat along the third-level corridor, drinking broth from the kitchen. Two of them had injuries from the passage -- the broken arm and the laceration noted in the incident record. The rest were intact. One of them, a woman named Ketta who had been working the far face when the hammering began, walked to where Torvin sat and placed her hand on his shoulder. She did not say anything. She did not need to.

Torvin looked at his wrapped hands and thought about the survey instruments he might not be able to hold. Then he closed his eyes and listened to the sixteen miners breathing in the corridor, and he thought about that instead.
