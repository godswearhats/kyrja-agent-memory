## Re-arc-Hi3-1

**Scenario.** Ottoman Constantinople, 1453 — the final days of the siege. A Greek scribe employed by the city's Latin trading community has been identified by a Venetian merchant captain as disposable, his position about to be used to deliver false intelligence to the Genoese garrison, which will get him killed when the deception is discovered. A Genoese interpreter who overhears the plan moves to extract the scribe through the sea walls before the captain can deliver him to the garrison.

> A = Luca Ferrante, Genoese interpreter and merchant factor; B = Nikolaos Phrygios, Greek scribe; T = Venetian merchant captain Bartolomeo Sozzi, who plans to use Nikolaos as an unwitting courier of false intelligence to the Genoese defenders, a deception that will mark Nikolaos as a Venetian spy when Sozzi's fabrication is discovered.

5 beats, chronological.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The Venetian quarter of Galata, the twenty-sixth of May. The city across the water was still holding, which meant the siege had entered its terminal phase, which meant the Venetians were calculating what their position would be once it fell. Luca had been interpreting for the Genoese factor's house for eleven years. He had no affection for the Venetians, which was not unusual, and no particular feeling about their calculations, which were their own business.

What gave him pause was the conversation he overheard between Sozzi and his factor in the warehouse yard at the fifth hour. He had not been invited into it. He had come to collect a letter and the warehouse door was ajar and Sozzi spoke too loudly when he believed himself unheard. The scribe's name was mentioned twice. The phrase Sozzi used was: *he will deliver it and will not know what he carries, and when the Genoese discover it, the question of who sent him will answer itself.* He said it with the satisfaction of a man solving a problem by creating a worse problem for someone else.

Luca collected his letter. He did not let the warehouse door move as he left.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The Genoese factor's house, that afternoon. Luca spent two hours considering whether this was something he should involve himself in. He was not friends with Nikolaos. He knew him by reputation and by occasional professional crossing -- the scribe translated commercial documents for several of the Latin houses, Venetian and Genoese alike, and had the slightly distracted quality of a man who spent most of his time with paper. He had no quarrel with anyone that Luca knew of. He was the kind of man who acquired enemies only by being available to be used.

The delivery was planned for the following morning, before the day's assault began. Luca knew this because Sozzi's factor had mentioned a time. The intelligence was false: the Genoese would be told that the sea walls in the Neorion section were undefended, when in fact they were not, which was a piece of misinformation that served Venetian positioning well and Genoese survival not at all. When the deception was found out -- within hours, probably -- the question of who had sent the scribe with the information would be settled in the simplest way available. There would be no opportunity to explain.

Luca decided, in the way that practical men made decisions in bad circumstances, that this was not actually a calculation.

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2).** The evening of the twenty-sixth. Luca had a pilot's pass for the sea walls: a document he had obtained for legitimate commercial purposes and never surrendered. He also had contacts among the Genoese garrison's quartermaster corps, which was not the fighting arm but had certain functional autonomy about who moved within the wall sections. He had not used these contacts for any improper purpose before. He was aware that this was a condition that would shortly change.

He sent a message to Nikolaos through the Greek quarter's letter-carrier, which was the normal channel for professional correspondence, asking to meet on a matter of translation work. He proposed the harbour-side church of Hagia Eirene at the seventh hour. It was a neutral location; it was also thirty yards from the section of sea wall his pass covered.

He brought the pass, a small purse that represented most of his portable savings, and two letters of introduction to Genoese merchants in Caffa that he had been meaning to send for months.

**E4 (intervention — S2 / confrontation).** Nikolaos arrived at the church on time, with his satchel of working documents, clearly expecting to discuss a commission. Luca told him immediately what he had overheard.

The scribe was quiet for a moment. "You may have misunderstood," he said.

"I understood it correctly. Sozzi intends to use you as a carrier. You won't know the content until the Genoese interrogate you, and by then the content will be indistinguishable from your testimony."

"I should go to Sozzi. Demand an explanation."

"Sozzi will deny it and the morning appointment still comes. Or he'll move the timing to prevent you from refusing." Luca set the pass, the purse, and the two letters on the ledge between them. "The sea wall pass covers the Neorion section until tomorrow dawn. There are Genoese ships at anchor with Caffa destinations. The letters will get you to the counting house in Caffa and some weeks of shelter while you determine what comes next."

Nikolaos looked at the documents. "This is everything you have."

"The pass is a document. The letters are paper. The purse is replaceable." Luca considered. "Less replaceable than a scribe, in a city that is about to stop requiring them."

Nikolaos picked up the pass. He held it by the edge, as though still deciding.

"You don't know me well," he said.

"I know Sozzi well enough to be confident about this."

Nikolaos put the pass in his coat. He left the ledge money where it was for a moment, then took that too.

**E5 (outcome — S3).** Luca escorted Nikolaos to the Neorion wall section before the eighth hour and spoke to the quartermaster's man he knew, who saw the pass and asked no questions. Nikolaos went through and down to the harbour staging area. A Genoese merchant vessel with a Caffa charter was loading by lamplight; Luca had ascertained its departure schedule that afternoon.

Sozzi sent for Nikolaos the following morning. He was told the scribe had left the city. The morning delivery did not occur. The intelligence that Sozzi had manufactured was not delivered.

Constantinople fell two days later. Luca was not on the walls. He spent those two days settling what accounts could be settled and burning what paper could not be carried. When the city fell, the records of his pilot's pass were among the things that did not survive.

Nikolaos reached Caffa in June. He sent one letter, which arrived after the city had changed hands and found no one at the address.

## Re-arc-Hi3-2

**Scenario.** London, 1666 — the week of the Great Fire. A journeyman printer whose master has been arrested on a fabricated charge of seditious publication is about to have his master's press and type confiscated under a warrant obtained by a rival stationer. A senior member of the Stationers' Company, recognising the warrant as procedurally invalid, acts to halt the confiscation before the press is destroyed.

> A = Edmund Crane, senior warden, Stationers' Company; B = Thomas Gale, journeyman printer; T = Stationer Abel Pritch, who has obtained a warrant for confiscation of the press belonging to master printer James Oates (currently imprisoned), intending to break the type and acquire the press for below-market price in the confusion of the Fire.

4 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4.

**Note on beat order.** Opens at the moment of the confiscation's discovery, establishes the danger in retrospect, follows the intervention, then resolves.

**E3 (danger present — S1, depicted first).** Thomas Gale was pulling type when he heard the cart in the alley and the men at the door. He was not surprised -- the city was still burning three streets east and the week had been nothing but crowds and carts and the smell of smoke -- but the men at the door were not moving furniture and they were not fleeing, and the warrant one of them held up through the window was the kind of document that meant the press was going to leave the premises on that cart.

He went to the door. The warrant was in the name of the Stationers' Company, signed by one warden and countersigned by the company registrar. It authorised seizure of all printing materials belonging to James Oates, currently detained on a charge of seditious publication, pending resolution of proceedings.

James was in Newgate. Thomas was not James. The press was not Thomas's. But the type in his hands and the press on the floor and the stacked broadsheets by the west wall were all going onto the cart unless Thomas could produce something that made the warrant stop being operative, and he was a journeyman printer who had been at his master's side for four years and had not the faintest idea how a Stationers' Company warrant worked except that it apparently came with a cart.

He asked them to wait and went to find Edmund Crane.

**E1 (danger escalation, depicted second).** Crane's house was two streets away. Thomas had met him twice, at company dinners where his master had introduced them. He was not confident Crane would remember him.

Crane remembered him. He listened to Thomas's account without interrupting. Then he asked a question Thomas had not anticipated.

"Who are the two men on the warrant?"

Thomas described them. The warden's name had been on the document; he had read it before his hands started shaking.

"Pritch's man," Crane said. "That's Abel Pritch's personal warden proxy." He stood up. "The warrant is issued under standing company authority, but a seizure in the name of the company requires a quorum endorsement when the accused is not present at the proceeding. James is in Newgate. Pritch cannot execute a unilateral seizure under a proxy signature. The procedure is wrong."

"Can you stop it?"

"I am a senior warden. Yes." He was already at his coat.

**E2 (preparation for intervention — S2, depicted third).** On the walk back to Oates's print house, Crane explained the procedural ground to Thomas in the clipped way of a man thinking through an argument while he speaks.

The Stationers' Company could seize materials of a member accused of a prohibited publication, but only on a quorum warrant -- three wardens or the master and one warden. A proxy signature from a single warden, even a legitimate proxy, was insufficient for a seizure proceeding where the principal was absent and unable to respond. Pritch had rushed the warrant through the day before, using the confusion of the Fire to avoid the standard quorum review. The registrar had countersigned without checking, which was either negligence or participation.

"Can Pritch contest it?" Thomas asked.

"He can contest it before the full court. That will take weeks. By which time James will either be released or formally charged, and either way the company will have to follow its own procedure." Crane stopped at the corner and looked at the print house. The cart was still there. The men were carrying type. "We need to move quickly."

**E4 (outcome — S3, depicted fourth).** Crane did not raise his voice. He showed the lead man the section of the company's standing orders that covered seizure procedure, which he had memorised and now cited from memory because he did not have a copy on his person but he did not need one. He told the man that the warrant's procedural defect was actionable, that Crane's own standing as senior warden gave him authority to suspend a defective seizure pending full court review, and that any material removed from the premises on the basis of the defective warrant would be subject to return and the men executing the seizure would be personally liable for damage.

The lead man looked at his warrant. He looked at Crane. He asked if Crane could put his objection in writing.

"I'll have a written notice at the company house before noon," Crane said. "The seizure is suspended as of this moment."

The men put the type cases down. They conferred. They did not leave immediately -- there was a quality of waiting to see if someone would blink -- but they did not load anything else, and when it became clear that Crane was not going to move from the doorway, the lead man told his companion to leave the cart and they walked away.

Thomas stood in the print house doorway with a case of long primer type in his arms and looked at the cart and then at Crane.

"Will James be released?" he asked.

"That is a separate matter," Crane said. "The charge will need to be answered. But the press will be here when he is." He straightened his coat. "Put the type back. I have a notice to write before noon."

## Re-arc-Hi3-3

**Scenario.** Virginia, 1831 — the weeks following Nat Turner's rebellion. A free Black carpenter in Southampton County is about to be arrested on false witness testimony linking him to the conspiracy, a charge that would result in summary execution under the emergency measures in force. The county clerk, whose own family has employed the carpenter for years and who knows the testimony to be fabricated, uses his official position to interpose a formal hearing requirement before the arrest can proceed.

> A = Walter Briggs, Southampton County Clerk; B = Joseph Carver, free Black carpenter; T = Deputy Samuel Hews, acting on testimony from neighbour Amos Pell (who bears a property dispute grudge) that places Carver at a planning meeting for the rebellion.

6 beats, chronological, embedded document.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The county clerk's office, the first week of September 1831. The Southampton courthouse had been in continuous session for eleven days. Walter Briggs had not been home for three of them. He had been recording depositions, processing warrants, and attempting to maintain some procedural form in proceedings that were moving faster than the law, strictly speaking, allowed.

The deposition of Amos Pell arrived on the morning of the seventh, among a batch of six. Briggs read through them in order. Five of them were deponents identifying men they had personally seen on the Turner property or armed on the night of the twenty-first. The sixth was Pell's.

Pell stated that he had seen Joseph Carver at a gathering at the Turner farm in July, in conversation with Nat Turner and two other named men. He described the gathering as a planning meeting. He described Carver's participation as active. He named two witnesses to Carver's presence.

Briggs set the deposition down. He had known Joseph Carver for sixteen years. Carver had built the addition to his father's house in 1821 and had done the joinery work on the courthouse vestibule in 1828. More to the point: on the dates Pell described, Carver had been in Norfolk completing a contracted commission for a merchant named Aldridge. Briggs knew this because Carver had told him about the commission in June and had asked Briggs to witness the contract, which Briggs had done.

He looked at the warrant request attached to Pell's deposition, which Deputy Hews had already initialled for processing. Then he opened his desk drawer and found the contract copy he had retained as required by his witnessing function.

**E2 (danger escalation).** The courthouse anteroom, that afternoon. Briggs found Hews reviewing the day's warrant batch before submitting them to the emergency magistrate.

"The Pell deposition," Briggs said. "The one naming Carver."

"Signed and initialled," Hews said. "I'll take the arrest batch to Magistrate Dodd at four."

"I'm holding the Carver warrant."

Hews looked up. "On what grounds?"

"The deposition places Carver at a meeting in July. I have a witnessed contract establishing that Carver was in Norfolk on those dates. The deposition is contradicted by a document in county records."

"Pell signed under oath."

"Pell may have been mistaken. Or he may have reason to remember things incorrectly. There's a fence dispute between his property and Carver's that's been in the county records since 1828." Briggs kept his voice level. "A warrant on a disputed deposition, in a capital proceeding, requires a formal hearing before execution. That's the applicable procedure."

"The magistrate's been waiving the hearing requirement for the emergency batch."

"He can waive it for cases where the testimony is uncontroverted. This one is controverted. I will not process the warrant without a hearing date."

Hews looked at him for a moment. "You're the clerk," he said. He said it in the way of a man accepting a procedural fact rather than agreeing with its application. He took the Carver warrant out of the batch.

**E3 (embedded document — S1 confirmed, context deepened).** From the Southampton County Clerk's Record, September 1831, in Briggs's hand:

> *In the matter of the deposition of Amos Pell against Joseph Carver, free man, carpenter: I certify that on the 12th of June 1831 I witnessed the execution of a contract of commission between said Joseph Carver and one Marcus Aldridge, merchant of Norfolk, for joinery work at Aldridge's warehouse, with a specified period of execution from the 28th of June through the 19th of July. The contract bears my signature as county witness. The contract is recorded in the county instrument book at page 214. The dates of the alleged meeting described in Pell's deposition fall within the period of Carver's documented presence in Norfolk. I am placing this certificate in the warrant record and am requiring a formal hearing before the deposition is acted upon.*
> 
> *W. Briggs, County Clerk*

**E4 (preparation for intervention — S2).** The morning of September 9th. Briggs sent a rider to Norfolk with a letter to Marcus Aldridge requesting a sworn affidavit confirming the dates of Carver's commission and his presence at the Norfolk warehouse. He sent a second rider to Carver, through Carver's wife, advising him to remain indoors and not to travel until further notice. He did not explain further in that note.

He then drafted a formal hearing request and placed it on Magistrate Dodd's schedule. Dodd was handling twenty proceedings a day and would not be pleased. Briggs prepared a one-page summary of the documentary contradiction, which was designed to be read in two minutes.

**E5 (confrontation — S2).** Magistrate Dodd's chamber, the following morning. Dodd had the warrant request and Briggs's certificate in front of him. Hews was there. Pell had been summoned.

"The clerk's objection is procedural," Hews said. "The deposition is sworn."

"The deposition is contradicted by a county instrument," Briggs said. "A witnessed contract, in my own hand, placing Carver in Norfolk on the dates at issue. That contradiction is on its face. Proceeding on a contradicted deposition in a capital matter is not a procedural formality — it is the difference between a legitimate proceeding and an error."

Dodd looked at Pell. "You swear to what you saw?"

"I saw him there," Pell said. He did not look at Briggs.

"And yet there is a witnessed contract placing him elsewhere on those dates," Dodd said. He turned the contract copy over. "I will require the Norfolk affidavit before proceeding. Briggs, when do you expect it?"

"Within the week, if Aldridge responds promptly."

Dodd looked at Hews. "Hold the warrant pending the affidavit. If it confirms the contract dates, the deposition is insufficient and the warrant is voided." He looked at Pell. "If the affidavit confirms the contract, you will be asked to account for the discrepancy in your deposition."

Pell did not respond. He left the chamber before Hews.

**E6 (outcome — S3).** Aldridge's affidavit arrived on the fourteenth of September. It confirmed that Carver had been resident at his Norfolk warehouse premises from the twenty-eighth of June through the twenty-first of July, completing the contracted joinery work, and had been present on the premises daily throughout that period. Aldridge offered to appear in person if required.

Dodd voided the warrant on the fifteenth. The record noted the documentary contradiction and the affidavit confirmation. Pell was not called to answer, because Dodd had thirteen other proceedings scheduled and no appetite for a side proceeding against a white deponent in the current climate. Briggs noted this. He noted it in the way that county clerks noted things: in a separate personal ledger he kept at home, which was not a county record but which he considered a record of a different kind.

Carver came to the courthouse a week later to thank Briggs in person. He stood in the office doorway with his hat in his hands and said that he had heard what Briggs had done and wanted him to know.

Briggs said he had followed procedure. Carver said he was grateful for it. They shook hands. It was the last time Briggs saw him in Southampton County; Carver moved his family to Philadelphia in the spring.

## Re-arc-Hi3-4

**Scenario.** Edo-period Japan, 1701 — a provincial domain. A merchant's factor has been accused of adulterating sake by a rival merchant who has bribed the domain inspector. The charge carries severe punishment and the factor's employer cannot afford the political cost of contesting it publicly. The domain's senior physician, whose medicines require pure sake as a solvent and who has himself tested the accused batch, provides testimony that reframes the accusation and forces a second inspection.

> A = Physician Haruki Nishida, employed under domain authority; B = Kenjiro Moto, factor for merchant house Daichi; T = Domain Inspector Tanaka, acting under Merchant Takuro's inducement, who has certified the batch as adulterated without genuine examination.

5 beats, chronological, present tense.

**E1 (danger present — S1).** The domain inspector's office is hot in the afternoon light, and the written certification sits on the magistrate's desk between Kenjiro and the official from the magistrate's house. The certification is three lines in Inspector Tanaka's formal hand: that the sake batch presented by factor Kenjiro Moto on behalf of house Daichi was found upon examination to contain a prohibited adulterant; that the batch is therefore condemned; that the factor is responsible for the condemned goods and subject to the domain's standard penalty.

The standard penalty, as Kenjiro knows and as everyone in the room knows, is not a fine. It is a category that involves the magistrate's discretion and the domain lord's approval, which means it is also a category that involves the domain lord's mood and the political standing of everyone involved. House Daichi's standing is middling. Merchant Takuro's standing is better.

Kenjiro bows. He accepts the certification. He goes back to the warehouse and sits with the batch for a long time before he decides what to do.

**E2 (danger escalation).** He goes to Tanaka first, because going to the physician first feels like an accusation and going to the magistrate without evidence feels pointless. Tanaka receives him at his gate and listens with an expression of patient finality.

"The certification is issued," Tanaka says. "The procedure is complete."

"I request a description of the adulterant found," Kenjiro says. "The certification does not specify it."

"The certification is in standard form. It is not required to specify."

"Merchant Takuro submitted a complaint about our batch to your office three days before the inspection was conducted," Kenjiro says. "That timeline suggests prior knowledge of what the inspection would find."

"The timeline suggests the complaint led to a timely inspection," Tanaka says. "The certification stands." He closes the gate.

Kenjiro walks back through the domain town. He is thinking about Physician Nishida, who collects the Daichi house sake by personal arrangement, because the quality of pharmaceutical-grade sake is not a matter Nishida will compromise on.

**E3 (preparation for intervention — S2).** Nishida's dispensary is behind the domain lord's residence, in the medical quarter. Kenjiro brings a flask from the condemned batch. Nishida already has one; he collected his regular order last week.

"I have been using this batch for two weeks," Nishida says. "If it contains a prohibited adulterant of any standard type, my patients would present symptoms. None of them have."

He tests the flask Kenjiro brought and the flask from his own stock side by side, using the precipitate method he learned during his training. Both samples produce the same result. He records it.

"I will go before the magistrate," he says. It is not a question, and it is not offered as something Kenjiro can accept or decline. It is a statement of intention by a man who has been examining batches of sake for pharmaceutical quality for thirty years and whose findings carry the authority of the domain lord's medical household.

**E4 (confrontation — S2).** The magistrate's receiving hall. The magistrate is Okamoto, a cautious man who does not like Tanaka and does not like Takuro and does not like situations in which domain physicians appear before him holding chemical apparatus.

Nishida presents his findings: two samples from the Daichi batch, tested by precipitate analysis, showing no trace of any prohibited adulterant. He presents his patient records for the past two weeks, noting that six patients received medicinal preparations compounded with sake from the condemned batch, all of whom have shown normal recovery curves with no anomalous symptoms.

Tanaka, present at the magistrate's invitation, says that the domain inspection uses a different testing method.

"Name the method," Nishida says. "And name the adulterant found."

Tanaka does not name the method. He says the certification is issued under his authority and is not subject to review by the medical household.

"I am the senior physician of this domain," Nishida says. "My authority over matters touching the purity of pharmaceutical materials in this domain is established by the lord's own medical charter. A condemned batch of sake, if containing the adulterant category required for condemnation, would have produced observable effects in patients who received it as a medicinal solvent. No such effects are recorded." He sets the patient records on the magistrate's desk. "I request a second inspection by a qualified assessor, under my oversight, with the domain lord's authority."

Okamoto looks at the records. He looks at Tanaka. "The request is granted," he says. "Inspector Tanaka will provide his method and the identified adulterant in writing before the second inspection."

Tanaka's method was never provided in writing. The second inspection, conducted by the domain's secondary inspector under Nishida's presence, found no adulterant.

**E5 (outcome — S3).** The certification is voided by Okamoto's written order, issued eight days after the second inspection. The condemned batch is released from hold. Kenjiro receives the order at the warehouse and reads it twice.

The order does not address Inspector Tanaka's conduct. It does not address Merchant Takuro. It addresses only the factual finding: that the batch does not contain the adulterant required for condemnation under domain regulation, as established by two successive inspections. Domain politics are conducted in the space between findings, not in the findings themselves.

Kenjiro takes the order to house Daichi's master, who reads it and sets it down and says nothing for a long time. Then he says Kenjiro should go to the medical quarter and leave an appropriate gift for Physician Nishida.

Kenjiro goes. Nishida accepts the gift, sets it aside, and tells Kenjiro that purity of materials is not a virtue he practices as a favour. It is a requirement of his function. The gift, he says, can go to the dispensary's maintenance account.

Kenjiro bows. He leaves. The batch sells at full price before the end of the month.
