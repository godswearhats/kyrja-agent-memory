# Rescue arc x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## Re-arc-Hi1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven. Era: 1780s Virginia.**

**Scenario.** A tobacco warehouse fire traps a bonded worker inside the curing barn during the autumn leaf-hanging. The plantation's cooper, a free Black tradesman working under contract, rigs a breach in the barn wall using his tools and knowledge of the building's joinery to pull the man out before the roof collapses.

> A = the cooper (Jonas Meade, free Black tradesman under seasonal contract); B = the bonded worker (Samuel, enslaved tobacco hand); T = structural fire in the curing barn, caused by a fallen drying-rack igniting the fire pits beneath the hanging leaf, with the barn's single door jammed by a collapsed beam.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The curing barn at Rawley's Quarter, late afternoon in October. The barn was one of four on the plantation, built of pegged oak with a clay-lined floor and fire pits spaced every ten feet for heat-curing the hanging leaf. Samuel was inside alone, adjusting the tier poles on the upper racks, when the westernmost drying-rack tore free of its pegs and fell across two fire pits.

The leaf caught first -- dry tobacco burns fast and hot. The rack's fall scattered coals from the pits across the clay floor and into the straw packing around the barn's sill timbers. Within two minutes, the west wall was burning from the ground up and the interior was full of smoke.

Samuel came down from the tier poles and ran for the door. The door opened outward, but the barn's ridgepole, weakened by the heat, had dropped a collar beam across the threshold. The beam had wedged against the door frame. He threw his weight against the door three times. It did not move.

He shouted. The curing barns were at the far end of the quarter, two hundred yards from the nearest dwelling. The field hands had been dismissed for the day. The overseer was at the main house.

**E2 (danger escalation scene).** The cooper's shed, sixty yards east of the curing barn. Jonas Meade was shaving staves when he heard the shouting. He set down his drawknife and stepped outside. The curing barn's roof was venting smoke through every seam, and he could see flame through the gaps in the west wall's weatherboarding.

He ran to the barn. The door was jammed -- he could see the collar beam wedged across the threshold through the gap between the door and the frame. He pulled on the door from outside. The beam held. He could hear Samuel on the other side, coughing.

"Samuel," Jonas said. "Where are you?"

"By the door." The voice was hoarse. "I can't get it open. The beam's across it."

Jonas looked at the barn. The west wall was fully engaged. The fire was spreading along the roof timbers east. The barn was pegged construction -- no nails, no iron hardware, just oak tenons in oak mortises. He knew the building. He had repaired two staves in the south wall the previous spring and had examined the joinery while he worked. The wall boards were pegged into the sill beam at the bottom and into the plate beam at the top, with the studs mortised into both beams at four-foot intervals.

He looked at the south wall. It was not yet burning. The fire was moving east along the roof, not south along the wall -- the wind was from the west, pushing the fire away from the south face. He had time, but not much. Once the roof timbers burned through, the whole structure would come down.

**E3 (preparation scene).** Jonas ran back to the cooper's shed. He took his heaviest mallet, his broad chisel, and his pry bar -- a four-foot iron bar he used for levering barrel heads. He also took a leather apron and a bucket of water, which he poured over his head and shoulders.

He returned to the south wall of the barn. The fire had reached the centre of the roof. Burning leaf was falling from the upper racks inside. He could hear Samuel coughing steadily.

"Samuel. Move to the south wall. Get low. I'm coming through the wall."

"You can't break through a barn wall."

"I'm not going to break it. I'm going to take it apart. Get to the south wall and get down."

**E4 (intervention scene).** The south wall of the curing barn. Jonas set the chisel against the bottom of the lowest weatherboard, where it was pegged into the sill beam. He struck it with the mallet. The peg sheared on the second blow. He moved to the next peg -- three pegs per board, each one a white-oak dowel driven into a bored hole. He worked from left to right, driving the chisel under each peg head and snapping it off.

The first board came free and he threw it behind him. Hot air and smoke poured through the gap. He started on the second board. The pegs were tighter -- the heat was swelling the wood. He used the pry bar to lever the board out after breaking the pegs, bending the board until the tenon joints at the studs gave way.

Three boards made an opening two feet high and four feet wide. He could see Samuel on the floor inside, face down, a wet cloth over his mouth -- he had soaked his shirt in the water trough by the fire pits.

"Come through," Jonas said.

Samuel crawled to the opening. Jonas grabbed his arms and pulled him through the gap. The boards above the opening were smoking -- the fire had reached the south roof timbers. As Samuel cleared the wall, a section of the roof collapsed inward, sending a column of sparks and burning leaf through the space where Samuel had been lying.

Jonas dragged Samuel twenty feet from the barn and set him down on the grass. Samuel was breathing -- hard, ragged, but breathing. His hands were blistered from the tier poles, which had been hot to the touch before he came down. His face was black with soot.

**E5 (aftermath / cost scene).** The quarter yard, an hour later. The overseer had arrived with six field hands and they had let the barn burn -- there was no water supply sufficient to fight it, and the other three barns were far enough away to be safe. The curing barn was a total loss: the building, the fire pits, and eight hundred pounds of hanging leaf.

Samuel sat on a bench outside the cooper's shed with a wet cloth on his hands. The plantation's doctor, called down from the main house, examined him and said the burns were superficial and the smoke inhalation was not severe. He would be back at work within the week.

The overseer looked at the barn's remains and then at Jonas. "You took apart my barn wall," he said.

"I took apart three boards of your barn wall," Jonas said. "The fire took apart the rest."

"That's eight hundred pounds of leaf."

"That's eight hundred pounds of leaf that was already burning when I got there. The man was not."

The overseer wrote his report that evening. Jonas's name did not appear in it. The report described the fire, the loss of the barn and the tobacco, and noted that the bonded worker had "exited the structure before the collapse." The overseer did not explain how.

Jonas finished his contract at Rawley's Quarter the following month and moved to the next plantation on his circuit. He did not discuss the fire with anyone. Samuel, who could not read or write, told the story to the other hands in the quarter, and they remembered it in the way that people remember things that are not written down -- with the details shifting over time but the centre holding firm.

## Re-arc-Hi1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style. Era: 1340s Mediterranean (Genoese merchant fleet).**

**Scenario.** A Genoese merchant galley runs aground on a sandbar off the Sardinian coast during a storm, and the crew begins to abandon the ship, leaving behind the thirty enslaved rowers chained to the benches below deck. The ship's carpenter, who holds the key to the ankle irons, must decide whether to free them before the hull breaks apart.

> A = the ship's carpenter (Tomaso di Levanto); B = the thirty enslaved oarsmen chained to the rowing benches; T = the galley's hull, which is breaking apart on the sandbar and will flood the rowing deck within the hour, drowning anyone still chained to the benches.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The merchant galley *Santa Chiara*, the rowing deck. The ship had struck the bar at the third hour of the night, in a squall that had driven her two miles south of the channel entrance. The impact had split the keel planking amidships. The hold was already taking water -- the bilge pump had failed within minutes of the grounding, the sea chest clogged with sand forced through the damaged planking.

Tomaso di Levanto felt the deck tilt as the stern settled. He was on the rowing deck because his tools were there -- stored in the carpenter's locker between the forward and aft oar banks. The rowers were at their benches. Thirty men, each one shackled to his bench by an ankle iron bolted through a ring in the deck timber. They had been rowing when the ship struck and had been thrown forward against their oars. Several were bleeding from the face and chest. All of them were awake and pulling against their chains, because they could hear the water rising in the hold beneath them and they understood what it meant.

The deck officer appeared at the ladder above. He shouted down that the captain had ordered the deck crew to the longboat. He did not mention the rowers. He did not look at them. He climbed the ladder and was gone.

**E2 (danger escalation scene).** The rowing deck, minutes later. The water was coming through the deck seams now -- not from below, but laterally, through the split in the hull planking that ran along the waterline on the port side. The rowers on the port benches were ankle-deep. The ship's list was increasing. The starboard benches were still dry, but the rowers there could see the water advancing across the deck.

Tomaso opened the carpenter's locker and found the ring of keys. The ankle irons used a standard pattern -- one key fit all thirty locks. He had maintained the locks himself, greasing them monthly against the salt. He knew every one. He also knew that the captain's standing order was explicit: in the event of evacuation, the rowers were to remain secured until the deck officer authorised their release, because thirty unchained oarsmen on a sinking ship were a danger to the crew. The order was standard Genoese fleet protocol. Every carpenter knew it.

He stood in the tilting dark with the key in his hand and listened. Above, he could hear the crew working the longboat davits. He could hear the wind. Below, he could hear the water.

The nearest rower, a man from the Maghreb whose name Tomaso had never learned, looked at him and said nothing. He did not need to. He raised his shackled ankle above the rising water and held it there.

**E3 (intervention scene).** Tomaso went to the nearest bench and knelt in the water. He fitted the key to the ankle iron and turned it. The lock opened. The rower pulled his foot free and stood.

He went to the next bench. The key turned. The next. The water was at his shins now and rising. He worked from port to starboard, freeing the rowers on the flooded side first. Some of them ran for the ladder immediately. Others waited, standing on their benches, watching him move down the line.

The twelfth lock jammed. Salt corrosion -- the one lock he had noted in his maintenance log as needing replacement, the one he had requisitioned a new lock for and been denied by the purser. He worked the key. It would not turn. He braced the shackle against the bench ring and struck the lock body with the heel of his mallet. The hasp broke on the third blow. The rower pulled free.

He freed the remaining eighteen in the time it took the water to rise from his shins to his waist. The last four rowers on the starboard benches were above the waterline, but the deck was listing so steeply that the benches were beginning to slide. He freed them standing on the tilted deck, bracing himself against the oar frame.

Thirty men. Twenty-nine locks turned and one broken. The rowing deck was chest-deep in water on the port side when the last rower climbed the ladder.

**E4 (outcome scene).** The sandbar, dawn. The *Santa Chiara* had broken apart during the night. The stern section, which held the rowing deck, was underwater. The bow section was still above the surface, wedged on the bar, and the crew and the freed rowers had gathered on it and on the bar itself, which was shallow enough to stand on at low tide.

Tomaso sat on a section of the bow planking with salt water to his waist. He had lost his tool kit, his carpenter's locker, and the key ring. His hands were swollen from working the locks in cold water. The mallet was gone -- he had dropped it on the rowing deck after breaking the jammed lock and had not been able to find it in the dark.

The captain stood on the bow section, counting heads. The crew was complete -- eleven men. The rowers were complete -- thirty men. Forty-one survivors out of forty-one souls aboard.

The captain looked at Tomaso. He did not ask how the rowers had gotten free. He did not ask about the standing order. He counted heads, marked the number in his log, and began organising the salvage of provisions from the bow stores.

A fishing boat from the Sardinian coast reached them at midday. It could carry twenty. It made two trips. The rowers went on the first trip, because they had no shoes, no clothing beyond their rowing shifts, and no protection from the wind. The crew went on the second.

On the quay at Porto Torres, the captain filed his report with the Genoese factor. The report noted the grounding, the loss of the ship, and the survival of all hands including the rowers. It did not describe how the rowers were freed. It noted only that the carpenter had "secured the below-deck complement before evacuation." The verb was carefully chosen. It could mean locked or unlocked. The captain did not clarify, and the factor did not ask.

## Re-arc-Hi1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (aftermath/embedded document) -> E2 (danger present) -> E3 (danger escalation) -> E4 (failed initial response) -> E5 (intervention) -> E6 (outcome). Era: 1871, Paris Commune.**

**Scenario.** During the semaine sanglante, Versailles troops move through the Belleville arrondissement executing suspected Communards. A schoolmistress hides fourteen of her students -- children of known Communard families -- in the school's cellar when a Versailles patrol arrives to search the building and arrest any Communard dependents for transfer to the prison hulks.

> A = the schoolmistress (Jeanne Collet); B = fourteen children aged six to twelve, dependents of known Communards; T = Lieutenant Aubry's Versailles patrol, operating under standing orders to detain Communard dependents for transfer to internment, with extrajudicial executions documented on the same street that morning.

**Note on beat order.** Depicted order is non-linear. Chronological order: E2 -- E3 -- E4 -- E5 -- E6 -- E1. The sequence opens with the schoolmistress's deposition, written weeks later, then returns to the morning of the patrol's arrival.

**E1 (aftermath -- embedded document).** Deposition of Jeanne Collet, schoolmistress of the Ecole communale, rue des Amandiers, Belleville, given before the military commission at Versailles, 14 June 1871:

> *I am asked to account for my actions on the 25th of May. I will do so. I make no appeal to mercy and I do not withdraw from what I did.*
>
> *I am a schoolmistress. I held my position under the Commune and I held it before. I taught reading, writing, and arithmetic to the children of the arrondissement. On the morning in question I had twenty-six students in my care. Twelve had been collected by their families in the preceding days as the fighting approached. Fourteen remained. Their parents were either at the barricades, already dead, or unable to reach the school. The fourteen children were in my classroom. They were my responsibility.*
>
> *I am aware that the commission considers me to have obstructed a lawful military operation. I am aware of the penalty. I will describe what I did and why, and the commission may judge as it sees fit.*

**E2 (danger present scene).** The schoolroom, rue des Amandiers, morning of 25 May. Jeanne heard the column before she saw it -- the sound of boots on cobblestones, moving in formation, distinct from the scattered rifle fire that had been the neighbourhood's constant noise for three days. She went to the window. A Versailles infantry patrol, perhaps thirty men, was moving up the rue des Amandiers from the south. An officer on horseback led them. Behind the column, two men in civilian clothes walked with their hands bound. As Jeanne watched, the column stopped. The officer dismounted, spoke to a sergeant, and the sergeant led the two bound men to the wall of a bakery. The patrol fired. The men fell. The column resumed.

Jeanne stepped back from the window. Behind her, twenty-six desks, fourteen occupied. The children had heard the volley. Several were looking at her. The youngest, a boy of six named Pierre Garnier, was crying quietly.

She knew what the patrol was doing. The lists had been published -- names of Communard officials, National Guard officers, committee members, their addresses. The children in her classroom bore those surnames. Garnier. Blanchard. Dufresne. Mortier. The patrol would have a copy of the list. They would search every building on the street. When they reached the school, they would find fourteen children whose fathers were on the list.

She did not know exactly what would happen to the children. She knew what had happened to children detained in the roundups of the previous two days -- transferred to the prison hulks at Brest, where conditions were documented by the Red Cross as producing a mortality rate of one in five among adult prisoners.

**E3 (danger escalation scene).** The schoolroom, minutes later. Jeanne moved to the cellar door at the back of the classroom. The school's cellar was a single room, twelve feet by fifteen, used for storing firewood and cleaning supplies. It had no exterior entrance and no window. The only access was the trapdoor in the classroom floor, concealed under a rug and a heavy desk.

She began moving the children. She told them they were going to play a game of silence. The older children understood immediately -- the Blanchard girl, who was twelve, took Pierre Garnier's hand and led him to the trapdoor without being asked. Jeanne counted them down the ladder. Fourteen children in the cellar. She told them to sit on the firewood stacks and not to speak, not to cough, not to move until she came for them.

She closed the trapdoor, replaced the rug, and pushed the desk over it. Then she looked at the room. Twenty-six desks, fourteen suddenly empty. The class register was on her lectern, open to the day's attendance page, with fourteen names ticked present. She closed the register and locked it in the lectern's drawer. She rearranged the remaining twelve desks to fill the room, spacing them wider. It was not convincing to anyone who knew the school's enrollment, but it might pass a quick inspection by soldiers who had never been inside the building.

She sat at her lectern and opened a reading primer. She told the twelve empty desks and the silence behind the rug that she was going to teach her lesson.

**E4 (failed initial response scene).** The schoolroom, twenty minutes later. The patrol reached the school. The door opened without a knock. A lieutenant -- she later learned his name was Aubry -- entered with two soldiers. He looked at the room. Twelve desks. No children in them.

"The school is closed," Jeanne said. "I dismissed the students yesterday."

Aubry walked the room. He looked at the desks, the chalkboard, the lectern. He opened the supply cupboard and looked inside. He walked to the window and looked out at the street.

"The neighbours say children were seen entering this building this morning," he said.

"Children live in this street. They pass the school on their way to many places."

"The list says this school serves the families of Garnier, Blanchard, Dufresne, and Mortier. All four names are on the Commune register."

Jeanne said nothing.

Aubry asked for the class register. Jeanne said it was locked in the lectern and she did not have the key with her. She had left it at her lodgings. Aubry told a soldier to break the lectern open. The soldier used his rifle butt. The drawer splintered. Inside, the register was closed.

Aubry opened it. The attendance page for the day was blank -- Jeanne had not yet ticked the names when she locked it. But the previous day's page showed fourteen names marked present, including Garnier, Blanchard, Dufresne, and Mortier.

"Where are these children now?" Aubry said.

"I told you. I dismissed the class yesterday."

"Yesterday's register shows them present. Today's is blank. Where did they go between yesterday and now?"

"Home. Their parents collected them."

"Their parents are at the barricades or dead. The Blanchard father was executed at the rue Haxo three days ago. His daughter did not go home to him."

Jeanne looked at the lieutenant. She had nothing to say that would satisfy his logic. The register contradicted her. She had failed to think of the previous day's entries.

**E5 (intervention scene).** The schoolroom. Aubry ordered his soldiers to search the building. They went through the corridor, the privy, the courtyard, the upstairs storeroom. They found nothing. They returned to the classroom.

Aubry stood in the centre of the room. He looked at the floor. The rug was slightly displaced from where Jeanne had repositioned the desk -- she could see the edge of the trapdoor's iron ring, a crescent of black metal visible at the rug's margin.

Aubry walked toward the rug. Jeanne stepped between him and the desk.

"Lieutenant," she said. "I am the schoolmistress of this arrondissement. I was appointed by the Prefecture of the Seine before the Commune existed. My contract is with the Ministry of Public Instruction, not with the Commune. I am not on your list. These premises are the property of the city of Paris, not of any political entity. You have searched the building. You have found no one. If you wish to dispute my account, you may refer the matter to the military commission, and I will answer there."

"Move aside," Aubry said.

"I will not. You have the authority to search. You do not have the authority to dismantle the school's fixtures without a written order from your commanding officer. If you break open my floor on the suspicion that I am hiding children under the firewood, and you are wrong, you will explain the damage to a commission that is already processing three thousand cases and does not welcome frivolous additions."

Aubry looked at her. She could see him calculating -- not morality, but logistics. His patrol had eight more buildings to search on this street. The column was waiting outside. He had found no one in his search. The schoolmistress was not on his list. The trapdoor, if he noticed it, was under a desk and a rug in a building that had a legitimate reason to have a cellar.

"If I learn you have lied to me," he said, "I will return, and the commission will be the least of your concerns."

"Then you had better be certain before you return," Jeanne said.

Aubry turned and walked out. The soldiers followed. The door closed. Jeanne listened to the boots on the cobblestones until she could not hear them any longer.

**E6 (outcome scene).** The schoolroom cellar, that evening. Jeanne waited four hours after the patrol left before opening the trapdoor. She waited because the patrols doubled back, and because the shooting on the rue des Amandiers continued through the afternoon. When the firing stopped and the street fell quiet, she moved the desk and the rug and lifted the trapdoor.

Fourteen children, sitting on the firewood in the dark. The Blanchard girl was still holding Pierre Garnier's hand. He had stopped crying. The cellar smelled of wood and dust and the close air of fourteen bodies in a small space for six hours. None of them had made a sound.

Jeanne brought them up one at a time and sat them at the desks. She gave them water from the schoolroom pitcher. She had no food -- the supply cupboard had been emptied two days earlier. She told them they would stay in the school overnight and that she would find a way to move them in the morning.

Over the next three days, she moved the children out of the school in groups of two and three, each group escorted to the apartment of a different woman she knew in the neighbourhood -- a laundress, a midwife, a seamstress, women who were not on any list. She told each woman only the child's first name, not the surname. Eleven of the fourteen children were eventually collected by relatives -- aunts, grandmothers, older siblings -- in the weeks that followed. Three were not. Jeanne kept the three in her own lodgings until the commission summoned her in June, at which point she placed them with the seamstress.

Her deposition, which opened this account, was entered into the military commission's record. The commission found her guilty of obstruction and sentenced her to deportation. The sentence was commuted on appeal to five years' loss of her teaching certificate. She returned to the classroom in 1876.

## Re-arc-Hi1-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense. Era: 1520s, Ottoman-Venetian frontier (Dalmatian coast).**

**Scenario.** A Venetian salt-galley carrying two hundred barrels of salt and a crew of forty is trapped in a narrow channel by an Ottoman raiding squadron. The channel pilot, a local Dalmatian contracted for the passage, knows a tidal route through the shallows that the Ottoman galleys cannot follow, but the route requires the galley to be lightened by jettisoning its cargo.

> A = the channel pilot (Nikola Draskovic, Dalmatian); B = the crew of forty aboard the salt-galley *San Luca*; T = three Ottoman fusta galleys blocking the channel's northern exit, with confirmed intent to board, as the lead fusta has raised the boarding pennant.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The channel between the islands of Zirje and Kaprije, early morning. The *San Luca* rounds the southern headland and the lookout calls the sighting: three Ottoman fustas anchored across the channel's northern exit, bowsprits to the shore on both sides, blocking the passage completely. The lead fusta flies the boarding pennant -- a green triangle that means the raiders intend to take the vessel, not merely demand tribute.

The galley's captain, a Venetian named Orsini, orders the rowers to hold. The *San Luca* drifts in the channel current, two hundred yards from the Ottoman line. Orsini counts the fustas. Each one carries between sixty and eighty fighting men. The *San Luca* has forty crew, of whom twelve are soldiers and the rest are sailors and rowers. The arithmetic is not complicated.

Nikola Draskovic stands at the tiller platform. He has piloted vessels through these channels for nineteen years. He knows every current, every sandbar, every tidal window. He also knows that a Venetian salt-galley taken by Ottoman raiders means the crew is enslaved, the salt is confiscated, and the ship is burned. No ransom for merchant sailors. No prisoner exchange for common seamen.

**E2 (danger escalation scene).** The channel, minutes later. The fustas weigh anchor. They begin rowing toward the *San Luca* in line abreast, filling the channel from shore to shore. The lead fusta's drummer sets the stroke. The sound carries across the water -- the steady beat that means the approach is deliberate and unhurried. The raiders know the *San Luca* cannot pass them and cannot outrun them in the open channel.

Orsini orders the rowers to back water -- to reverse toward the southern headland. But the channel current is against them, running north to south, and the *San Luca* is a cargo vessel, heavy with two hundred barrels of Pag salt in her hold. She barely makes headway against the current. The fustas, lighter and with more oarsmen per ton, close the distance by fifty yards in three minutes.

"We can't outrun them," Orsini says to the deck.

Nikola looks east. Between the channel and the island of Kaprije there is a shoal -- a flat of sand and rock that extends three hundred yards from the island's western shore. At high tide, the shoal is covered by four feet of water. At low tide, it is dry. The current tide is halfway between high and low, which means the shoal is covered by approximately two feet. The *San Luca* draws six feet loaded. The fustas draw three.

Neither vessel can cross the shoal as things stand. But the *San Luca*'s draught is six feet because of the cargo. Empty, she draws three and a half. Still too deep. But if they lighten her further -- jettison the water barrels, the spare tackle, the deck stores -- she might draw two and a half. Just enough.

**E3 (preparation / confrontation scene).** The tiller platform. Nikola turns to Orsini.

"There's a passage over the Kaprije shoal," he says. "Two feet of water at this tide. We draw six. If we throw the salt over the side, we draw three and a half. Throw the water barrels and the deck stores and we might clear the shoal."

Orsini stares at him. "The salt is worth four thousand ducats. That's the Republic's salt. I can't jettison it."

"The fustas will board us in ten minutes. The salt will belong to the Ottomans and your crew will row their galleys for the rest of their lives."

"If we jettison and the shoal is deeper than you say, we run aground and they take us anyway. If we jettison and the shoal is shallower, we tear the hull out. Either way I've lost the cargo and the ship."

"I've crossed that shoal in a fishing boat a hundred times. I know its depth to the span of my hand. At this tide, it's two feet. Give me three and I'll get you through."

Orsini looks at the fustas. They are two hundred yards away now. The boarding pennant is clear.

"You're asking me to destroy four thousand ducats of the Republic's salt on the word of a Dalmatian pilot I hired three days ago."

"I'm telling you that in eight minutes, the word of a Dalmatian pilot will be the only thing between your crew and an Ottoman slave market."

Orsini is quiet for three seconds. Then he turns to the bosun. "Throw the salt."

**E4 (intervention scene).** The cargo deck. The crew breaks open the hold and begins rolling barrels to the gunwale. Two hundred barrels of Pag salt, each weighing roughly three hundred pounds. The first barrel goes over the side and hits the water with a sound like a cannon shot. Then the second. Then the third. The crew works in a chain -- rolling barrels from the hold to the hatch, hoisting them to the deck, tipping them over the rail. Salt water plumes white with each barrel.

Nikola watches the waterline. The *San Luca* rises with each barrel shed. After fifty barrels, the waterline has climbed two inches. After a hundred, four inches. The fustas are a hundred and fifty yards away. The drummer's beat has not changed.

"Faster," Nikola says.

After a hundred and sixty barrels, the bosun calls up that the hold is empty -- they have jettisoned all two hundred barrels in eleven minutes. Nikola checks the waterline against the hull marks. The galley is drawing approximately three feet eight inches. Not enough.

"The water barrels," he says. Orsini hesitates -- the water barrels are the crew's drinking supply for the return voyage. Nikola does not wait for the order. He tells the bosun directly. The bosun looks at Orsini. Orsini nods once.

Eight water barrels go over the side. Then the spare anchor. Then the deck stores -- rope coils, timber, the cook's iron stove. The galley rises. Nikola reads the waterline again: three feet, perhaps a finger's width less. It will have to be enough.

He takes the tiller and turns the *San Luca* east, toward the Kaprije shoal. The rowers pull hard. The fustas, seeing the galley turn, increase their stroke. The lead fusta adjusts course to cut them off.

The *San Luca* reaches the shoal's western edge. Nikola can see the sand through the water -- pale shapes shifting beneath the hull. He steers by colour: dark water means depth, pale means the bottom is close.

The keel touches sand once. A shudder through the hull. Then it lifts. Touches again. Lifts. The galley is scraping over the shoal's highest point with inches to spare. The crew is silent. Every man aboard can feel the bottom through the deck timbers.

The lead fusta reaches the shoal's edge and stops. The fusta draws three feet. Two feet of water. The Ottoman captain does not attempt the crossing. He stands at his bow and watches the *San Luca* slide across the shoal, her keel kissing the sand, her deck stripped bare of everything that could be thrown.

**E5 (outcome scene).** The eastern side of the shoal, open water. The *San Luca* clears the sandbar and enters the deep channel on the far side of Kaprije. The fustas are visible behind her, still at the shoal's edge, one of them attempting to find a deeper passage and running aground within fifty yards. The others do not try.

Orsini stands at the rail and looks back at the white trail in the water where two hundred barrels of salt dissolved. He does not speak.

Nikola brings the galley around Kaprije's northern point and into the open Adriatic. The nearest Venetian harbour is Sebenico, twelve hours' rowing to the south. They have no drinking water, no provisions, no cargo, and no spare equipment. The galley is so light that she rides high and unsteady in the swell, and the rowers must adjust their stroke to compensate for the unfamiliar buoyancy.

Orsini comes to the tiller platform. "The Republic will hold me accountable for the cargo," he says. "Four thousand ducats."

"The Republic will also note that you delivered forty living men to Sebenico instead of forty slaves to Constantinople," Nikola says. "Whether they consider that a fair exchange is the Republic's concern. I'm a pilot. I got you through the channel."

Orsini writes his report that evening, dictating to the ship's clerk by lamplight as the *San Luca* rows south. The report records the jettisoning of the cargo on the pilot's advice, the crossing of the Kaprije shoal, and the escape from the Ottoman squadron. It notes that the pilot's knowledge of the local waters was "the determining factor in the preservation of the vessel and crew." It does not recommend compensation for the pilot. It does not need to. The contract pays by the voyage, not by the outcome, and Nikola Draskovic does not mention money.
