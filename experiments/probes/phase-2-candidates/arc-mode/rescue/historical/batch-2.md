# Rescue arc × Historical — Batch 2 (Tier B)

## Re-arc-Hi2-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven. Era: 1840s American South (Mississippi River).**

**Scenario.** A steamboat pilot on the Mississippi discovers that the boiler of a passing packet boat is about to explode while sixty passengers are aboard. The packet's captain, racing to beat a rival, refuses to stop for repairs. The pilot must get a federal inspector to exercise his authority to halt the vessel before the boiler fails.

> A = steamboat pilot (James Scofield); B = sixty passengers aboard the packet boat *Atalanta*; T = over-pressured boiler on the *Atalanta*, already showing plate distortion, set to fail within hours at current operating pressure.

**E1 (danger present scene).** The pilothouse of the *Corinne*, three miles above Natchez, late afternoon. Scofield had the wheel and was watching the *Atalanta* pull alongside on the port side. She was running hard -- black smoke pouring from her stacks, her paddle churning a wake that rocked the *Corinne's* hull. The *Atalanta* was trying to pass, and her captain, Mercer, was pushing her to do it.

Scofield could see the *Atalanta's* boiler deck through the gap between the two vessels. The safety valve was tied down -- a practice he had seen before and despised every time. But it was the starboard boiler plate that held his eye. The plate was bulging outward between the rivets, a distortion visible even at forty yards. He had seen a plate like that once before, on the *Belle Creole*, two years ago. The *Belle Creole's* boiler had let go thirty minutes after he'd noticed the same bulge. Fourteen people had died.

He handed the wheel to his cub pilot and went to the rail with his glass. Through the telescope he could see the plate clearly: the bulge was three inches at least, the rivets along the seam were weeping steam, and the safety valve's lever was lashed to a stanchion with wire. The *Atalanta* carried sixty passengers, most of them on the boiler deck, sitting within twenty feet of a boiler that was going to come apart.

He shouted across the water. The wind and the engine noise took his voice.

**E2 (failed initial response scene).** The Natchez waterfront, one hour later. Both boats had put in at Natchez -- the *Corinne* to discharge freight, the *Atalanta* to take on wood for the next leg to Vicksburg. Scofield crossed the levee to the *Atalanta's* stage plank and found Captain Mercer on the boiler deck supervising the wooding.

"Your starboard boiler plate is bulging," Scofield said. "I could see it from my pilothouse. The rivets are weeping and your safety valve is wired shut."

Mercer looked at him. "Who are you?"

"James Scofield. Pilot on the *Corinne*."

"Then go pilot the *Corinne*, Mr. Scofield. My engineer inspected the boilers at Baton Rouge and passed them. I'm not interested in a diagnosis from a pilot on another boat."

"Your engineer passed a boiler with the safety valve tied down. That plate is going to let go."

"The *Madison* is four hours ahead of me. If I stop for every riverman who wants to tell me how to run my boat, I'll be four days behind her. I have sixty passengers who paid for speed and I intend to deliver it."

"Those sixty passengers are sitting on top of a boiler that's about to open up."

"Good evening, Mr. Scofield."

Scofield stood on the stage plank and watched Mercer walk back to the wooding crew. The *Atalanta* would be under way again within the hour.

**E3 (preparation for intervention scene).** The Natchez waterfront, thirty minutes later. Scofield knew that a pilot's warning carried no legal force. A captain was sovereign on his own vessel, and Mercer had refused. But the Steamboat Inspection Act of 1838 gave federal inspectors the authority to detain any vessel with defective boilers, and Scofield knew there was an inspector stationed at Natchez. He had met the man once -- a former engineer named Avery who had a reputation for thoroughness and no particular love for captains who tied down safety valves.

Scofield walked the six blocks from the levee to the inspector's office on Commerce Street. The office was closed, but the boarding-house keeper next door told him Avery was taking his supper at the Eagle Hotel on Main Street. Scofield went to the Eagle and found Avery at a corner table with a plate of catfish.

"I need you to come to the waterfront," Scofield said.

Avery looked at him. "It's suppertime, Mr. --"

"Scofield. Pilot on the *Corinne*. There's a packet called the *Atalanta* taking on wood right now. Her starboard boiler plate is bulging three inches between the rivets, the seam is weeping steam, and the safety valve is wired to a stanchion. She's carrying sixty passengers and her captain won't stop."

Avery set down his fork. "You saw the plate yourself?"

"Through a glass, at forty yards, and again from her own boiler deck. I've seen a plate like that before. It failed in thirty minutes."

Avery stood, left money on the table, and picked up his hat.

**E4 (intervention scene).** The *Atalanta's* boiler deck, Natchez waterfront. Avery came aboard without asking permission. He carried his inspector's commission in his coat and a pressure gauge in his bag. Scofield followed him up the stage plank.

Mercer met them at the top. "Inspector Avery. I wasn't expecting a visit."

"Captain Mercer, I'm conducting an emergency inspection of your boiler plant under Section 9 of the Steamboat Inspection Act. I'll need your engineer and access to the boiler deck."

"On whose complaint?"

"On mine. Stand aside, please."

Mercer did not stand aside. "This vessel was inspected at Baton Rouge ten days ago. I have the certificate in my cabin. An emergency inspection requires probable cause, and I don't accept the word of a rival pilot as probable cause."

Avery reached into his bag, took out his commission, and held it open. "Section 9 gives me authority to inspect any vessel at any time when I have reason to believe the boiler plant presents a danger to passengers. I'm looking at a bulging plate from where I stand. That's my probable cause. You can cooperate or I can have the harbourmaster chain your vessel to the wharf. Your choice, Captain."

Mercer looked at the commission. He looked at Scofield. Then he called for his engineer.

The inspection took twenty minutes. Avery measured the plate distortion at three and a half inches. He tested the boiler pressure at forty pounds above the rated maximum. He cut the wire on the safety valve himself and noted the act in his book. He turned to Mercer.

"I'm detaining this vessel. No departure until the starboard boiler is cooled, the plate is replaced, and a licensed boilermaker certifies the repair. Your passengers can disembark at Natchez or wait, but this boat does not move."

**E5 (outcome scene).** The Natchez waterfront, the following morning. The *Atalanta* sat at the levee with her fires drawn and her starboard boiler cooling. A boilermaker from the Natchez yard had examined the plate the previous evening and confirmed the distortion. He told Avery the plate had been within hours of failure -- possibly less, given the pressure Mercer had been running.

The sixty passengers had been moved to the *Corinne* and the *Three Sisters*, which were both departing for Vicksburg that morning. Some of them had complained about the delay. Most of them had not, once the boilermaker's assessment circulated through the waterfront.

Scofield stood on the *Corinne's* hurricane deck as the boat backed out from the levee. The *Atalanta* was still at her mooring, cold and quiet, Mercer's racing flag taken down from her jackstaff. Avery stood on the levee with his book in his hand, writing.

Scofield turned to the river and took the wheel from his cub. The *Madison*, the boat Mercer had been chasing, had passed Vicksburg during the night. The race was over. The sixty passengers were distributed across two boats, travelling at ordinary speed, with inspected boilers and safety valves that moved freely on their levers. Scofield watched the river and said nothing about it to anyone.

## Re-arc-Hi2-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style. Era: 1580s Japan (late Sengoku period).**

**Scenario.** A tea master in the household of a provincial lord discovers that assassins have poisoned the water supply for a diplomatic banquet, intending to kill the visiting delegation and provoke a war. The master of ceremonies has already approved the preparations. The tea master must find a way to prevent the poisoned water from being served without alerting the assassins, who are embedded in the household staff.

> A = tea master (Jiro); B = visiting diplomatic delegation (twelve emissaries and their retainers); T = poisoned water supply for the banquet (arsenic introduced into the kitchen cistern by agents embedded in the household staff).

**E1 (danger present scene).** The tea house adjacent to Lord Matsuda's reception hall, the morning of the banquet. Jiro had risen before dawn to begin the water preparation for the evening's formal tea ceremony -- the ceremony that would follow the banquet and seal the diplomatic agreement between Lord Matsuda and the delegation from the Mori domain. The water was the foundation. Everything depended on the water.

He drew the first bucket from the kitchen cistern and carried it to the tea house. He lit the brazier, set the kettle, and waited for the water to heat. When it reached the correct temperature, he ladled a measure into a testing bowl -- a practice his own master had taught him, not for poison but for mineral content and taste. He brought the bowl to his lips and held the water in his mouth without swallowing.

The taste was wrong. Not wrong in the way of mineral variation or seasonal change. Wrong in the way that made the roof of his mouth tingle and his tongue go faintly numb. He spat the water into the waste basin and rinsed his mouth with water from his personal flask -- water he had drawn from the garden well the previous evening and kept sealed.

He sat very still for a moment. Then he drew a second bucket from the cistern and tested it. The same taste. He tested a third. The same.

The kitchen cistern supplied the water for the entire banquet -- the cooking, the drinking cups, the tea ceremony. Twelve emissaries and their retainers would eat and drink from that water tonight. He knew what the taste meant. His master had taught him to recognise it, not because a tea master expected to encounter it, but because a tea master who served a lord should know the taste of every substance that might find its way into water.

**E2 (danger escalation scene).** The kitchen passage, midmorning. Jiro had spent the intervening hours thinking. The cistern served the entire kitchen. If he reported the contamination to the master of ceremonies, the preparation would be halted, the household would be searched, and the assassins -- whoever they were -- would know they had been discovered. If they were embedded in the household staff, as the method of introduction suggested, they would also be in a position to act by other means. A halted banquet and a panicked household would give them confusion to operate in.

He walked the kitchen passage and observed the staff. The preparations were well advanced. The fish had been dressed. The rice was washed. The vegetables were cut and arranged. All of it had been rinsed or cooked with cistern water. Jiro counted the dishes that involved water: the soup, the rice, the tea, the drinking water set at each place, the rinsing water for the fruit course. Every course but the grilled fish and the pickled radish used cistern water directly.

He returned to the tea house and considered what he could control. He could not purify the cistern. He could not halt the preparation without alerting the household. He could replace the water -- but only if he had an alternate source large enough to supply the entire banquet, and only if he could substitute it without the kitchen staff noticing.

The garden well. It was fifty paces from the kitchen, behind the tea house, enclosed by a low wall. It was not connected to the cistern. He had drawn from it the previous evening and the water was clean. But the garden well supplied four buckets per hour at best, and the banquet required twenty. The banquet was nine hours away.

**E3 (preparation for intervention scene).** The tea house and the garden, afternoon. Jiro worked methodically. He could not carry twenty buckets from the garden well to the kitchen without being seen. But he could enlist the one person in the household whose authority over the kitchen was absolute and whose loyalty was not in question: Lady Matsuda, the lord's wife, who managed the household and had the power to redirect the kitchen's operations without explanation.

He requested an audience. Lady Matsuda received him in the inner garden. He told her the water was contaminated. He did not say "poison" -- he said the water had turned and was unfit for the ceremony. He asked her to order the kitchen to draw all water for the banquet from the garden well instead of the cistern, and to frame the order as a matter of ceremonial purity. A visiting delegation of this importance required garden-well water, drawn fresh. The kitchen staff would not question it. It was the kind of instruction Lady Matsuda gave regularly.

Lady Matsuda studied his face. She was not a woman who missed the distance between what was said and what was meant. She asked him whether the contamination was natural. He said he did not believe so. She asked him whether the household was at risk. He said only if the cistern water was consumed.

She gave the order within the hour. The kitchen staff received it without surprise -- Lady Matsuda's standards for diplomatic occasions were well known. The cistern water was set aside. Fresh water from the garden well was drawn in relays throughout the afternoon.

**E4 (intervention / outcome scene).** The reception hall and the tea house, evening. The banquet proceeded. Jiro watched from the tea house as the courses were served -- each one prepared with garden-well water, each one clean. The delegation ate. Lord Matsuda ate. The household staff served and cleared. The soup was praised. The rice was praised. The tea, which Jiro prepared himself with water he had drawn and carried and heated with his own hands, was praised most of all.

After the final course, Jiro excused himself and walked to the kitchen. The cistern water -- eight buckets that had been drawn before Lady Matsuda's order and set aside -- stood in a row along the back wall. He carried each bucket to the garden drain and emptied it into the ground. Then he sealed the cistern's lid and tied it with cord.

The following morning, before the household woke, he drew a sample from the cistern and brought it to the household physician. The physician tested it with the methods available to him and confirmed what Jiro already knew. Lord Matsuda was informed privately. The investigation that followed was conducted by the lord's own intelligence, not by the household staff. Three members of the kitchen crew were identified and removed. The delegation departed safely two days later, the agreement sealed.

Jiro's role in the matter was recorded nowhere. Lady Matsuda, when asked by her husband how she had known to order the water changed, said only that her tea master had advised her on a question of ceremonial practice.

## Re-arc-Hi2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 (embedded document/aftermath) -> E2 (danger present) -> E3 (failed initial response) -> E4 (danger escalation) -> E5 (intervention) -> E6 (outcome). Era: 1793 France (Revolutionary period).**

**Scenario.** A printer's apprentice in Paris discovers that his master's name has been added to a denunciation list that will be read at the tribunal the following morning. The denunciation is false -- fabricated by a rival printer who wants the shop. The apprentice must find a way to get the denunciation withdrawn or discredited before the tribunal convenes.

> A = printer's apprentice (Lucien Morel); B = master printer (Guillaume Daubenton); T = false denunciation filed with the Revolutionary Tribunal, scheduled to be read the following morning, accusing Daubenton of corresponding with emigres.

**E1 (aftermath -- embedded document, depicted first).** From the records of the Section des Gravilliers, dated the 14th of Germinal, Year II:

> *The denunciation of Citizen Daubenton, master printer, by Citizen Farge, on the charge of correspondence with emigres, was withdrawn before the tribunal this morning by Citizen Farge himself, who stated before the president of the section that his accusation was made in error and without foundation. The apprentice Morel presented to the section committee a receipt from the postal bureau showing that the letter cited in the denunciation was addressed not to the emigre Peltier in London but to the paper merchant Pelletier in Rouen, and that the contents concerned an order for rag-stock paper, not political correspondence. Citizen Farge, questioned further, acknowledged that he had not inspected the letter himself and had relied on a report from his journeyman, who had misread the address. The committee accepted the withdrawal. Citizen Daubenton is released from suspicion. The committee notes that Citizen Farge's rival interest in Daubenton's printing contract with the section is a matter of record and recommends that the section president consider this in any future denunciation originating from the same source.*

**E2 (danger present scene).** The printshop on the Rue des Gravilliers, late evening. Lucien had stayed past closing to set type for the next morning's broadsheet when the door opened and a man he recognised -- Tessier, a clerk at the section committee -- came in without knocking. Tessier was pale. He set a folded paper on the composing table and told Lucien to read it.

It was a copy of a denunciation filed that afternoon by Citizen Farge, the printer whose shop was two streets away. The denunciation accused Guillaume Daubenton of corresponding with the emigre journalist Peltier in London, a man on the Committee of General Security's list. The evidence cited was a letter, allegedly seen by Farge's journeyman, bearing an address in London and Daubenton's hand on the envelope. The denunciation was scheduled to be read at the tribunal the following morning.

Lucien knew what a denunciation meant in Germinal of Year II. The tribunal was not a court that weighed evidence carefully. A denunciation from a fellow citizen, naming a specific emigre, with a witness, was enough to issue a warrant. Daubenton could be arrested by midday. What followed arrest was not certain, but the uncertainty was itself the danger.

Tessier said he had copied the denunciation because he owed Daubenton a debt -- Daubenton had printed Tessier's son's apprenticeship certificate without charge the previous year. He said he could do nothing else. He left.

**E3 (failed initial response scene).** Daubenton's rooms above the printshop, the same evening. Lucien woke his master and showed him the denunciation. Daubenton read it and sat on the edge of his bed for a long time. He was a careful man, a methodical man, and the first thing he said was that the letter Farge's journeyman had seen was not addressed to Peltier in London. It was addressed to Pelletier in Rouen -- the paper merchant from whom Daubenton ordered his rag-stock. The names were similar. The address was in France, not England. The contents were a purchase order.

Lucien asked where the letter was now. Daubenton said he had posted it at the postal bureau on the Rue Saint-Martin three days ago. He did not have a copy. He did not have a receipt.

Lucien asked whether anyone at the section committee would listen to an explanation. Daubenton said the section president, Citizen Moulin, was a fair man but would not dismiss a sworn denunciation without evidence. An explanation from the accused was not evidence. The denunciation named a specific emigre and a specific letter. Without proof that the letter was addressed to Pelletier in Rouen and not Peltier in London, Daubenton's word was simply denial.

Daubenton told Lucien there was nothing to be done. He said he would go to the tribunal in the morning and state his case. Lucien looked at the denunciation and thought about the tribunal and thought about the men he had seen taken from their shops in the past month, and he put on his coat.

**E4 (danger escalation scene).** The streets of the Gravilliers section, near midnight. Lucien went first to the postal bureau on the Rue Saint-Martin. It was closed and locked. The night porter told him the bureau would open at seven. The tribunal convened at eight. One hour to obtain proof, cross the section, and present it to the committee.

He then went to Farge's shop and stood across the street in the dark. The shop was closed but a light burned in the upper rooms. Lucien had worked beside Farge's journeyman, a man named Renard, at a section printing project the previous autumn. Renard was not a malicious man, but he was not a careful one, and Lucien understood now how the misreading had happened -- or been arranged. Farge had wanted Daubenton's printing contract with the section for months. A denunciation that removed Daubenton would leave the contract open.

Lucien could not confront Farge. A confrontation between an apprentice and a master printer, at midnight, over a sworn denunciation, would accomplish nothing and might produce a second denunciation. He returned to the postal bureau and sat on the step to wait for morning.

**E5 (intervention scene).** The postal bureau, Rue Saint-Martin, 7:03 a.m. Lucien was the first person through the door. He asked the clerk for a receipt confirming the address on a letter posted three days ago by Citizen Daubenton, master printer. The clerk said he could not issue receipts for letters already dispatched. Lucien asked whether the bureau kept a log of posted items. The clerk said they kept a dispatch ledger that recorded the sender, the destination city, and the recipient for every letter.

Lucien asked to see the entry. The clerk hesitated -- the ledger was not a public document. Lucien told him the entry was needed for a matter before the tribunal that morning, that the citizen's liberty depended on it, and that the section committee would request it formally if necessary, but that there was not time for a formal request.

The clerk opened the ledger and found the entry: Daubenton, Guillaume. Destination: Rouen. Recipient: Pelletier, Jean-Claude. Paper merchant. The clerk, at Lucien's request, wrote out a certified extract from the ledger, signed it, and stamped it with the bureau's seal. The time was 7:14.

Lucien ran. The section committee met in the former church of the Gravilliers, eight blocks south. He arrived at 7:41, breathing hard, and asked the door-keeper for the section president. Moulin was already in the committee room, reviewing the morning's docket. Lucien presented the postal receipt and explained the discrepancy: Pelletier in Rouen, not Peltier in London. A paper merchant, not an emigre. A purchase order, not political correspondence.

Moulin read the receipt. He sent a guard to bring Farge from his shop. Farge arrived at 7:55, five minutes before the tribunal was scheduled to convene.

**E6 (outcome scene).** The committee room, 8:00 a.m. Moulin set the postal receipt on the table in front of Farge and asked him to explain the discrepancy between his denunciation and the bureau's record. Farge looked at the receipt. He said his journeyman had seen the letter and reported the address as London. He said he had not inspected the letter himself.

Moulin asked whether Farge was aware that Daubenton's paper supplier in Rouen was named Pelletier. Farge said he was not. Moulin asked whether Farge wished to maintain his denunciation or withdraw it. Farge looked at the receipt again, at Moulin, and at the two committee members who flanked him. He withdrew the denunciation.

Daubenton, who had arrived at the committee room at 7:50 prepared to stand before the tribunal, stood instead in the corridor and listened as the clerk read the withdrawal into the record. Lucien stood beside him. When the reading was finished, Daubenton put his hand on Lucien's shoulder -- briefly, without words -- and they walked back to the printshop together.

The broadsheet still needed to be printed. Lucien set the remaining type while Daubenton inked the press. They did not discuss the morning's events. The first copies came off the press at half past nine, and Lucien carried them to the section's distribution point before the market opened.

## Re-arc-Hi2-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense. Era: 1665 London (Great Plague).**

**Scenario.** A parish sexton discovers that a family of six has been wrongly sealed inside a plague house -- the red cross painted on their door, the watchman posted -- despite the fact that the reported plague death occurred next door, not in their house. The parish clerk who filed the report refuses to correct it. The sexton must get the order reversed before the family starves or contracts the plague from the neighbouring house through the shared wall.

> A = parish sexton (Thomas Crabb); B = the Pennyman family (six people, including three children, sealed inside a house wrongly marked as infected); T = wrongful quarantine (sealed in a plague house with no provisions, adjacent to an actively infected dwelling, with exposure risk increasing daily).

**E1 (danger present scene).** Bladder Street, parish of St. Botolph-without-Bishopsgate, a Thursday morning in August. Thomas Crabb walks his rounds. The street is half-empty -- many of the wealthier residents left the city in June, and those who remain keep their doors closed and their windows shuttered. The air carries the sweetness of vinegar and the sharper smell of burning sulphur, both used against the contagion.

He stops at number eleven. The red cross is fresh on the door, painted yesterday, and the words LORD HAVE MERCY ON US are still tacky. A watchman sits on a stool beside the door, hired by the parish to enforce the quarantine. From inside, Crabb hears a child crying and a woman's voice, tight with fear, calling through the door.

"We are not sick," the woman says. "The death was at number twelve. Not here. We are six in this house and none of us have the distemper. The clerk has made an error."

Crabb knows the Pennyman family. He has known them for four years. Richard Pennyman is a glover; his wife, Agnes, keeps the household; the three children are under ten. The sixth person is Agnes's mother, who came to stay when the plague reached the parish in June.

He asks the watchman for the sealing order. The watchman produces it: a note from the parish clerk, Edmund Voss, dated yesterday, certifying that a death from plague has occurred at number eleven Bladder Street and ordering the household sealed for forty days. But Crabb buried the body yesterday. The body came from number twelve -- the house next door -- where a journeyman dyer named Harkin died of plague tokens on Tuesday evening. Number eleven and number twelve share a party wall, but they are different households.

**E2 (failed initial response scene).** The parish office, St. Botolph-without-Bishopsgate, the same morning. Crabb finds Voss at his desk, the parish rolls open before him. Crabb tells him the sealing order is wrong. The death was at number twelve, not number eleven. The Pennyman family is healthy. They need to be released.

Voss looks at the sealing order and then at his own notes. He says his information came from the searcher of the dead -- the woman appointed to examine bodies and report the cause of death. The searcher's report lists the address as number eleven.

"The searcher is wrong," Crabb says. "I carried the body out of number twelve myself. I know the house. The door faces the tallow shop."

"The searcher is the legally appointed authority. Her report is entered in the parish roll. I cannot alter a roll entry on the sexton's word against the searcher's."

"Then send the searcher back to look at the houses. She'll see her own error."

"The searcher examines bodies, not houses. And she has forty examinations to conduct today. I cannot pull her off her rounds to revisit a report because you disagree with the address."

"There are three children in that house, Edmund. If they stay sealed in there for forty days next to an infected dwelling, with no provisions, no physician, and no way out, they will either starve or catch the plague through the wall."

"Bring the matter to the alderman. He has authority to reverse a sealing order. I do not."

Crabb asks where the alderman is. Voss says the alderman left the parish in July and is at his country house in Hertfordshire. He will not return until the bills of mortality drop below a hundred per week. The current week's bill is over a thousand.

**E3 (danger escalation scene).** Bladder Street, later that morning. Crabb returns to number eleven. Through the door, Agnes Pennyman tells him the household has food for two days. The water jug was half-full when they were sealed in. The children are frightened. Her mother, who is sixty-three and suffers from a chest complaint, is having difficulty breathing in the shuttered house.

From number twelve, through the party wall, Crabb can hear the sounds of a second household still occupied -- Harkin's widow and her daughter, who are now under their own quarantine. He cannot hear whether they are sick. The plague does not announce itself through walls. It passes through them, carried by the air that moves through gaps in the plaster, through the floorboards, through the shared chimney stack that serves both houses.

Every hour the Pennymans remain sealed beside the infected house increases the chance that the contagion will reach them. The forty-day quarantine is designed to contain plague within a household where it already exists. For a household where it does not exist, sealed against a house where it does, the quarantine is not containment. It is exposure.

**E4 (preparation / intervention scene).** The vestry of St. Botolph-without-Bishopsgate, early afternoon. With the alderman gone, the only authority that can reverse a sealing order in his absence is the vestry committee -- the parish's governing body of householders. Crabb knows the protocol. Three vestrymen constitute a quorum. A quorum can review and reverse a parish officer's order.

He spends two hours locating vestrymen. Most have left the city. He finds the first, a mercer named Eliot, at his shop on Threadneedle Street, still open because his apprentices have nowhere else to go. He finds the second, a chandler named Woodfall, at his house near the churchyard, where he has stayed to look after his property. The third, a widow named Prescott who sits on the vestry in her late husband's place, is at the almshouse on Houndsditch distributing bread.

He brings all three to the vestry room. He presents his case: the burial record in his own hand, showing the body removed from number twelve; the sealing order citing number eleven; the discrepancy. He tells them about the three children, the two days of food, the shared wall, the absent alderman.

Eliot asks whether the parish clerk will accept the vestry's reversal. Crabb says the clerk defers to the searcher's report. Woodfall says the vestry outranks the clerk. Prescott says to write the order and she will sign it first.

Crabb drafts the reversal: the sealing order for number eleven Bladder Street is rescinded; the household is to be released immediately; the watchman is to be reassigned to number twelve, where the actual death occurred. All three vestrymen sign. Crabb blots the signatures and folds the paper.

**E5 (outcome scene).** Bladder Street, late afternoon. Crabb presents the vestry's order to the watchman. The watchman reads it, stands, and moves his stool to number twelve. Crabb removes the crossbar from number eleven's door.

Agnes Pennyman opens the door. The children push past her into the street -- not running, but standing in the light, blinking. The youngest sits down on the cobblestones and does not move. Agnes's mother leans against the doorframe and breathes the open air. Richard Pennyman comes last, carrying the empty water jug.

Crabb tells them to leave Bladder Street. The infection next door is active, and the shared wall is a continuing risk. Richard says his wife's sister lives in Aldgate, three streets past the pump. Crabb tells them to go there and to avoid the main roads where the carts pass.

The Pennymans gather what they can carry and leave within the hour. Crabb watches them walk down Bladder Street -- six people, three of them children, moving quickly, the youngest carried on Richard's shoulders. They turn the corner and are gone.

Crabb returns to number twelve. He checks the quarantine seal. It is intact. From inside, Harkin's widow asks him through the door whether anyone is coming with provisions. He tells her he will speak to the parish about it. He marks the address in his book and continues his rounds.
