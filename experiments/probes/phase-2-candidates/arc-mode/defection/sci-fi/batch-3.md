# Defection arc × Sci-fi — Batch 3 (Tier C)

## D-arc-Sf3-01

**Scenario.** On a deep-space research station, a sensor systems engineer accepts a data-exclusivity clause when joining a competitive survey mission: all readings taken by the mission's instruments belong to the mission collective, are not to be transmitted off-station, and require collective vote before any publication. Over eight months the engineer streams high-resolution gravitational anomaly data to an outside academic institution in exchange for a faculty appointment, using the station's maintenance bandwidth channels which are unmonitored. The mission's data integrity officer discovers the transmissions during a routine bandwidth audit.

> A = the mission collective (represented by the data integrity officer, Dr. Yuen); B = sensor engineer Petra Holst; P = data-exclusivity clause in mission participation agreement (all sensor data collected during the mission term belongs to the collective; no transmission, disclosure, or publication of mission data to any external party without collective vote; all outgoing data transmissions must be logged in the mission data register); V = streaming gravitational anomaly data to the Kepler Institute over eight months via maintenance bandwidth channels, bypassing the mission data register, in exchange for a conditional faculty appointment.

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** The mission briefing room on Station Arkanis, two days before the survey array went live. Dr. Yuen was not the one running the briefing -- that was the mission director -- but she sat in the back and watched the engineers sign their participation agreements page by page.

Holst reached the data-exclusivity clause and read it twice before she looked up. "What counts as a transmission? If I run analysis on the raw readings and send the methodology to a colleague for peer review --"

"The data itself," the mission director said. "Not methodology, not analytical frameworks. The actual readings, any subset of the readings, or any derivative product that could be used to reconstruct the readings. The collective votes before any of that leaves the station."

Holst said she understood. She signed. Yuen noted the signature in the participation log and moved on to the next page.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Three months in, the gravitational survey caught an anomaly in sector nine that Holst had flagged in her preliminary analysis. She brought it to the collective's weekly review, presented her interpretation with full data provenance, and proposed a supplemental observation window to resolve whether the signature was instrumental noise or genuine. The collective voted to extend the window. Her analysis drove the decision.

The mission director filed a commendation in the mission log noting that Holst's anomaly identification had potentially doubled the scientific value of the sector-nine dataset. Yuen noted the commendation in the participation records. The collective discussed putting Holst's name on the primary author slot for the sector-nine paper when the mission concluded.

**E3 (violation scene).** Six weeks after the sector-nine commendation. The faculty appointment offer came through her personal message queue on a Tuesday -- the Kepler Institute, a named chair in gravitational survey systems, contingent on bringing a "substantial and original dataset" to the appointment. She read the offer three times.

She had the dataset. The sector-nine anomaly alone would be sufficient for two years of original research. The collective would publish eventually -- missions like this moved slowly through internal review, collective negotiation, and the long queue of co-authorship sign-offs. The Kepler appointment would be gone before the collective's paper cleared.

She mapped the maintenance bandwidth channels on her station schematic. They were used for equipment telemetry and were not routed through the data register's monitoring layer. She modified her analysis software to package the gravitational readings as telemetry-format payloads and began streaming them that weekend. She set the transfer rate low enough to stay within the maintenance channel's expected variance.

Over the following eight months she transmitted eleven datasets to the Kepler Institute's receiving address. The sector-nine anomaly data was the third transfer.

**E4 (first-signal and discovery scene).** Yuen's bandwidth audit was quarterly and routine. She ran it on a Wednesday morning, pulling the maintenance channel logs alongside the mission data register to reconcile outgoing traffic. The maintenance channels normally showed instrument telemetry: low-variance, patterned, timestamped against the maintenance schedule.

Three of the maintenance channels showed outgoing payloads that did not match any instrument in the maintenance schedule. The payloads were periodic -- roughly three-week intervals -- and their data volume was consistent with compressed scientific datasets rather than telemetry. She ran the destination addresses and found they resolved to a receiving node at the Kepler Institute.

She cross-referenced the transmission dates against the mission data register. None of the transmissions were logged. She ran the payload signatures against the mission's sensor output catalogue. The signature patterns matched the gravitational survey array.

She saved the audit to a secured personal archive and wrote a formal anomaly report to the mission director, flagging the transmissions and noting that the data signatures appeared to match Holst's sector-nine instrument string.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The mission director called Holst to a meeting the following morning. Yuen attended with the audit and the payload analysis on her tablet. The mission director placed the anomaly report on the table.

"Eleven outgoing transmissions from maintenance channels over eight months," the mission director said. "Destination: Kepler Institute. Payload signatures matching your instrument string. None of them logged in the data register. I need an explanation."

Holst said the Kepler Institute had made her a faculty offer conditional on bringing original data. She said the collective's publication timeline meant the sector-nine findings would not be in the public record for at least two years, by which time the appointment would have gone to someone else.

"The participation agreement doesn't include a publication-timeline exception," Yuen said. "The data belonged to the collective from the moment your instruments collected it. You transmitted it to an external institution without a collective vote."

Holst said the collective would eventually publish the same data and that no commercial interest was involved. She said the Kepler appointment would bring the station's work more visibility than a journal paper on a two-year delay.

"That's a case you could have made to the collective," the mission director said. "You didn't make it. You transmitted." He turned to Yuen. "What's the collective's position?"

Yuen said the collective's agreement was clear and the violation was documented. She said the question was whether Holst would provide a full account of the transmissions and cooperate with a retrieval request to the Kepler Institute. If the Institute returned the data and agreed not to publish, the collective could decide through a vote whether to proceed with a formal finding or to resolve it internally. If Holst refused cooperation or the Institute declined retrieval, the collective would file a formal complaint with the mission oversight body.

The mission director asked Holst directly whether she would cooperate.

Holst said she would contact the Kepler Institute. She said the appointment was already conditional and the Institute would not want a formal complaint on their receiving end. She said she did not think retrieval would be refused.

"You'll do that today," the mission director said. "You'll copy Yuen on the communication. And you're suspended from the collective's data systems pending the Institute's response and the collective's vote." He took her station access card from the table where she had set it at the start of the meeting. "We'll reconvene when we hear from Kepler."

Holst left without contesting the suspension.

## D-arc-Sf3-02

**Scenario.** On a terraforming project moon, a hydrology supervisor accepts a site safety protocol requiring her to report any subsurface instability readings above a threshold value to the project's engineering council within six hours of detection. Over four months she fails to report eleven separate threshold exceedances, judging them to be minor and self-resolving, while privately logging them in her own notes. A crew member is injured during a foundation placement when a subsurface void she had detected two weeks prior collapses. The project's safety auditor traces the unreported readings through the supervisor's personal log, which is recovered after the accident.

> A = the project engineering council (represented by the safety auditor, Renata Solis); B = hydrology supervisor Dessa Kwon; P = site safety protocol (the hydrology supervisor must report any subsurface instability reading at or above the defined threshold to the engineering council via the project safety system within six hours of detection; all readings must be logged in the project safety record, not in personal systems); V = failing to report eleven threshold-level subsurface instability readings to the engineering council over four months, logging them instead in a personal notes file, including the reading that identified the subsurface void which collapsed during the foundation placement.

6 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6

**Note on beat order.** Opens after the accident with the auditor's investigation, then traces the protocol commitment, a trust-display, the specific pre-accident violation, the confrontation, and closes with the council's finding.

**E4 (first-signal / discovery scene — depicted first).** Renata Solis is in the project safety office three days after the foundation placement accident, working backward from the incident report. The crew member's injuries are documented. The collapse site is mapped. The question the engineering council needs answered is whether the subsurface void was detectable before the placement.

She pulls Kwon's hydrology logs from the project safety system. The project safety record shows no threshold exceedances in the sector for the ninety days before the accident. She pulls the raw sensor feeds from the hydrology instruments and runs a comparison. The sensor data shows three readings above threshold in the relevant sector over the past forty days. None of them are in the project safety record.

She requests access to Kwon's personal data archive from the project's information systems officer. The personal notes file is unlocked and intact. She opens it and finds eleven entries spanning four months, each recording a threshold exceedance with Kwon's personal assessment of severity. The entry from nineteen days before the accident reads: *Sector 7 subsurface, depth 3.2m, cavity signal consistent with partial void, threshold plus 12%. Self-monitoring. No escalation.*

The void had been there for nineteen days. The placement crew had not been told.

**E1 (commitment scene — depicted second).** Fourteen months earlier, the project safety orientation. Kwon had signed the site safety protocol as part of her site qualification. Solis had run the session -- a standard briefing, four slides on the reporting system, one on the threshold values, one on the six-hour window.

The threshold values were set conservatively, Solis had explained, because the project's timeline meant foundation placements were scheduled weeks in advance and the engineering council needed lead time to assess risks and adjust scheduling. A threshold reading that resolved in twenty-four hours was still a reportable event, because the council's ability to protect crews depended on knowing what had been detected, not just what had persisted.

Kwon had said she understood. She was the best-qualified hydrology hire the project had made in two site cycles. She signed on the fifth line of the acknowledgement sheet.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene — depicted third).** Six months into Kwon's tenure, she had detected a subsurface water channel in sector three that posed a significant risk to the eastern foundation corridor. She reported it within two hours of detection, provided a full written assessment to the engineering council, and proposed a modified foundation route that preserved the project timeline while avoiding the channel entirely. The council adopted her proposal.

The project director sent her a formal note of recognition in the project log, copied to the engineering council. Solis had read the note at the time and filed it. She had considered Kwon one of the most reliable site specialists on the project.

**E3 (violation scene — depicted fourth).** The subsurface reading in sector seven, nineteen days before the accident. Kwon is in the field survey cabin with the instrument feed running. The reading exceeds threshold by twelve percent. The signature is consistent with a partial void -- not a collapse risk on its own, but a structural uncertainty in a sector scheduled for foundation placement in three weeks.

She reviews her previous eleven personal-log entries. Ten of them had resolved -- the readings had dropped below threshold within seventy-two hours without any crew exposure. She makes the same judgment call: the sector-seven reading is minor, the void is shallow, she will monitor it. If it persists above threshold in seven days she will report it.

She enters the reading in her personal notes file. She does not open the project safety system. The six-hour window closes without a report.

Seventeen days later, the void does not collapse on its own. It collapses under the weight of the foundation placement equipment.

**E5 (confrontation scene — depicted fifth).** The project safety office, five days after the accident. Kwon arrives for the formal safety interview. Solis has the personal notes file, the project safety record, and the raw sensor comparison on the table.

"The project safety record shows no threshold readings in sector seven for the ninety days before the placement," Solis says. "Your personal notes show eleven threshold exceedances over four months, including a sector-seven void detection nineteen days before the accident. The safety protocol requires you to report threshold readings to the engineering council within six hours. None of these eleven were reported."

Kwon says ten of the eleven had resolved below threshold within seventy-two hours. She says the sector-seven reading was monitored and she intended to report it if it did not resolve.

"The protocol's reporting window is six hours from detection," Solis says. "It doesn't have a resolution exception. The engineering council is required to assess every threshold event because the council makes crew deployment decisions, not the field supervisor. You withheld eleven assessments that the council was entitled to make."

Kwon says she made reasonable professional judgments. She says a threshold exceedance every two weeks would have generated constant council interruptions for readings that were minor and self-resolving.

"The threshold is calibrated to the project's safety requirements," Solis says. "If you believed the threshold was set incorrectly, the protocol for raising that is a formal petition to the engineering council, not personal discretion in the field." She sets the notes file on the table. "The council is meeting tomorrow to determine the safety finding and the question of your continued site qualification. I need you to confirm for the formal record whether the entries in the personal notes file are accurate."

Kwon says they are accurate. Solis notes the confirmation in the interview record and closes the session.

**E6 (council finding scene — depicted sixth).** The engineering council meeting room, the following morning. Solis presents the full audit to the seven-member council: the eleven unreported readings, the personal notes file, the comparison with the project safety record, and the timeline connecting the sector-seven detection to the accident.

The council votes on four questions. The first: was the safety protocol violated? Unanimous. The second: was the unreported sector-seven reading causally linked to the accident? Five to two, yes. The third: should Kwon's site qualification be suspended pending full review? Unanimous. The fourth: should the matter be referred to the project oversight authority for a formal safety finding that would follow her professional record? Four to three, yes.

The council chair dictates the finding into the project log. Kwon's site qualification is suspended. The project oversight authority is notified. The crew member's compensation claim is noted as a consequential obligation.

Solis seals the audit report and files it with the project safety record. The personal notes file is transferred to the official archive. The sector-seven void is mapped and the foundation corridor in that sector is redesignated pending full subsurface survey.

## D-arc-Sf3-03

**Scenario.** On an orbital habitat serving as neutral ground for inter-faction negotiations, a habitat communications officer accepts a post-specific confidentiality protocol binding all comm traffic between negotiating parties to strict non-disclosure. Over six weeks she forwards encrypted negotiation session summaries to one of the factions' intelligence services, having been recruited before her posting. The habitat's security director discovers the forwarding when a routine cipher audit flags an anomalous outgoing channel.

> A = the habitat's neutral oversight board (represented by the security director, Commander Voss); B = communications officer Lena Farouk; P = post-specific confidentiality protocol (the communications officer will not access, retain, reproduce, or transmit any content of inter-faction negotiation sessions to any party outside the neutral oversight board; all comm traffic associated with negotiation sessions is classified at the habitat's highest confidentiality tier; violations are grounds for immediate removal); V = forwarding encrypted summaries of negotiation session content to Faction Oryen's intelligence service via an anomalous outgoing channel over six weeks.

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** Commander Voss ran the security briefing for new habitat staff personally when the posting was sensitive. Farouk's posting was sensitive. The confidentiality protocol occupied most of the briefing.

"The factions are here because this habitat is the one place neither side has leverage over the communications infrastructure," Voss said. "The moment either side believes the other is reading their session traffic, the negotiations end and we lose our function. You are the person who runs the infrastructure. If you compromise that infrastructure for any reason, for any party, for any purpose, the habitat's mission is over."

Farouk said she understood. She signed the protocol. Voss countersigned and dated it and placed it in the personnel file. She was one of three candidates who had been vetted for the posting. She had scored highest on both the technical assessment and the security screening.

Voss did not know that the security screening had been run two months after her initial recruitment by Faction Oryen's intelligence service, and that the pre-recruitment window was the gap.

**E2 (violation scene).** The comm centre, three days after the first full negotiation session. Farouk ran session traffic through a standard encryption protocol as part of her role. She had access to the routing layer, which was below the encryption layer -- she could see traffic volumes, session durations, and addressing metadata without accessing content. Her recruitment instructions had anticipated this limitation and provided her with a secondary access credential that had been inserted into the habitat's firmware at installation, before she arrived.

She used the credential for the first time during the second session of the second week. It gave her access to the session content layer for a read-only window of forty minutes. She composed a summary in sanitised language and transmitted it to the Oryen intelligence receiving address via a channel she had opened in the habitat's maintenance subnet. The transmission was eleven kilobytes. The maintenance subnet carried normal traffic in the several hundred megabyte range. The eleven kilobytes were invisible in the noise.

She used the same method five more times over the following six weeks.

**E3 (discovery scene).** The cipher audit was a monthly process that Voss ran herself, a relic of a protocol she had set up years earlier on a previous posting and had never stopped running. It compared every outgoing channel's cipher profile against the habitat's registered key registry. Registered channels had known cipher signatures. Unregistered channels showed up as anomalies.

The maintenance subnet had three anomalous cipher profiles on three dates corresponding to negotiation sessions. The cipher style was consistent with intelligence-service traffic compression -- Voss had seen the signature before, from the other side of the table. The receiving address resolved to a relay node with no registered habitat relationship.

She ran the access credential logs for the content layer on the relevant dates. The log showed access by a credential that was not in the personnel registry. She ran the credential's installation timestamp. It predated Farouk's arrival by eleven weeks.

Voss sealed the audit and called the habitat's oversight board chair before she called Farouk.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** Voss met with Farouk in the security director's office with the habitat's oversight board chair present by comm. The cipher audit was displayed on the room's screen.

"The audit shows six outgoing transmissions from the maintenance subnet on dates corresponding to negotiation sessions," Voss said. "The cipher profile matches intelligence-service compression. The receiving address is a relay node with no registered connection to this habitat. I'm asking you to account for these transmissions."

Farouk said the maintenance subnet occasionally showed traffic artefacts from legacy firmware. She said she had not sent those transmissions.

Voss pulled the credential access log onto the screen. "A credential was used to access the content layer on each of those six dates. The credential was installed in the firmware eleven weeks before you arrived. It's not in the personnel registry. It was put there before you took the posting." She waited. "I'm not asking whether you installed the credential. I'm asking whether you used it."

Farouk said she wanted legal representation before she answered further questions.

The oversight board chair said that was her right and that a representative would be arranged. He also said that the habitat's protocol provided for immediate posting suspension on security grounds pending the outcome of any investigation, and that the suspension was effective immediately, not contingent on the investigation's conclusion.

Farouk said the suspension would create a communications gap during an active negotiation.

"The habitat has a backup communications officer," Voss said. "She's being briefed now." She collected Farouk's access credentials from the console. "You'll be accompanied to your quarters. The investigation will proceed with or without your cooperation. If you choose to cooperate, that choice will be part of the record the oversight board reviews."

Farouk did not contest the suspension. She did not say anything further. Voss noted the silence in the incident log and asked the security team to escort her out.

## D-arc-Sf3-04

**Scenario.** On a long-duration polar station on an ice moon, a structural integrity engineer accepts a maintenance reporting protocol committing him to log all stress readings from the station's ice-anchor array in the shared maintenance record accessible to the station commander. Over five months he falsifies a series of stress readings to conceal accelerating degradation in anchor cluster three, believing the degradation rate is within manageable parameters and that an accurate report would trigger an evacuation he considers unnecessary. The falsification is discovered when the station commander runs an independent sensor cross-check during a routine systems review.

> A = the station commander; B = structural integrity engineer Adric Sene; P = maintenance reporting protocol (all stress readings from the ice-anchor array must be logged in the shared maintenance record within twelve hours of collection; readings must reflect actual sensor output without modification; the structural integrity engineer may append an interpretive assessment but may not alter the raw values); V = logging falsified stress readings for anchor cluster three over five months, replacing actual sensor outputs showing accelerating degradation with modified values within the normal range, and omitting the interpretive assessments that would have flagged the trend.

5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E5 → E1 → E2 → E4

**Note on beat order.** Opens mid-violation when the falsification is well-established, then jumps to the discovery, then traces back to the protocol commitment and the trust-display before closing with the confrontation.

**E3 (violation scene — depicted first).** Station Kappa-7, the maintenance bay, a morning in the third month. Sene is logging the anchor cluster three readings. The stress curve for anchor cluster three has been climbing for eleven weeks. The raw values this morning are seventeen percent above the normal-operations ceiling.

He enters the logged values as nine percent above ceiling -- elevated, within the range he has been maintaining across the falsified log. He appends no interpretive note. He told himself in the first month that the degradation was an instrumentation artifact; in the second month, that the rate was stable; now, in the third month, he tells himself the anchor geometry is compensating for the increased load in ways the instrumentation does not capture.

He closes the maintenance record. The shared log shows a flagged value that the station commander will note and that will not trigger the evacuation threshold. That has been the function of the falsified values for eleven weeks.

**E5 (discovery scene — depicted second).** The station commander is in the systems monitoring room running the quarterly cross-check. The cross-check protocol takes the maintenance log values and compares them against a secondary instrument array that feeds directly to the commander's console and is not accessible to the engineering staff.

The secondary array for anchor cluster three shows a value twenty-three percent above the normal-operations ceiling. The maintenance log shows eleven percent. The gap between the two values is fourteen percentage points, which is outside the calibration variance of either instrument.

She runs the comparison for the preceding twenty weeks. The secondary array and the maintenance log are within calibration variance for the first nine weeks. The gap opens in week ten and widens consistently through the present reading. The pattern is not instrument drift. The two instruments are moving in opposite directions.

She pulls Sene's complete maintenance log for anchor cluster three, places it beside the secondary array output, and writes down the discrepancy for each logged reading. She notes the date on which the gap first appeared and cross-references it against the station activity log.

She calls Sene to the commander's office and does not say why.

**E1 (commitment scene — depicted third).** Eighteen months earlier. The protocol orientation for the polar station posting was conducted by the outgoing structural integrity engineer, with the commander present. The maintenance reporting protocol was the most specific document in the stack: raw values, unmodified; logged within twelve hours; shared record; no exceptions for interpretive disagreement.

The outgoing engineer had explained why the raw-value requirement existed. At a previous polar station, a structural engineer had rounded stress values to the nearest five-percent increment for readability, which had obscured a degradation trend until it became an emergency. The protocol had been written after that incident. The requirement was not about distrust of the engineer's judgment -- it was about giving the commander the same data the engineer had, so the commander could make independent decisions.

Sene had said he understood. He had signed the protocol. The commander had countersigned.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene — depicted fourth).** Four months into Sene's posting, a micro-seismic event had rattled anchor cluster one. Sene had logged the event in real time, filed an interpretive assessment within the hour, and recommended a precautionary load redistribution to reduce stress on cluster one during the aftershock window. The commander had authorised the redistribution based on his assessment. The aftershock window passed without incident.

The commander had noted in the station log that Sene's early detection and clear reporting had allowed the station to manage the micro-seismic event without interruption to operations. She had considered him one of the most reliable technical staff she had commanded.

**E4 (confrontation scene — depicted fifth).** The commander's office. The secondary array output and the maintenance log are side by side on her desk. Sene sits across from her.

"I need you to tell me why these two records show different values for anchor cluster three for the past twenty weeks," she says.

Sene looks at the comparison. He says the secondary array may be running a calibration offset. He says he has been meaning to check the calibration.

She takes out the calibration certificates for both instruments, current within the last six weeks. "Both instruments are in calibration," she says. "The gap between them opens at week ten and increases monotonically. Tell me what the actual cluster three values are."

Sene says the actual values are closer to the secondary array figures. He says the degradation rate is within what he believes are manageable parameters and that a report at the actual values would have triggered an evacuation threshold he considers premature.

"The protocol requires you to log the actual values," she says. "The evacuation threshold is mine to assess, not yours. You modified the logged values to prevent me from making that assessment."

He says the station cannot evacuate during the current weather window. He says an evacuation order would have been issued for a situation he is managing.

"The protocol's purpose is precisely that I can make that judgment independently," she says. "If the evacuation threshold is wrong, the way to change it is a formal petition to the oversight authority, not falsification." She pulls the secondary array output for the current date. "The current reading is twenty-three percent above the normal-operations ceiling. At what reading would you consider evacuation appropriate?"

He says twenty-five percent would concern him. She says the reading was twenty-one percent four weeks ago, when the weather window was open.

He does not respond to that.

"I'm suspending your write access to the maintenance record effective now," she says. "I'm logging the actual cluster three values from the secondary array and filing a retrospective correction to the maintenance record for the past twenty weeks. I'm also filing a formal incident report with the station oversight authority. You will remain on station because I need your technical knowledge during the assessment period, but you will work under direct supervision and your maintenance logs will require my countersignature before submission." She looks at him. "Do you contest any part of what I've described?"

He says he does not contest it. He says the degradation is still manageable.

"I'll make that determination," she says. "You've used that word twice. The determination was mine from the moment you signed the protocol."

She ends the meeting and begins drafting the retrospective correction.
