## D-arc-Hi2-1

**4 beats, chronological. Dialogue-driven. Setting: 1780s Virginia.**

**Scenario.** A tobacco planter entrusts his factor in London with exclusive authority to sell the plantation's annual crop and remit proceeds at the agreed commission rate. Over two seasons, the factor sells portions of the crop through a separate brokerage he co-owns, pocketing a second commission on each transaction. A clerk at a rival trading house sends the planter a consignment receipt that names the factor's brokerage as the selling party. Setting: 1780s Virginia and London.

> A = the planter (Colonel James Auldfield); B = the London factor (Edward Mercer); P = factoring agreement (signed before witnesses: Mercer holds exclusive authority to sell Auldfield's tobacco crop on the London market, at a commission of five percent of sale price, remitting the balance to Auldfield's account within ninety days of sale; Mercer shall sell the crop in his own name and on Auldfield's sole behalf, entering no intermediate sale, sub-brokerage, or secondary commission arrangement); V = routing portions of Auldfield's crop through a brokerage Mercer co-owns, Mercer & Slade, collecting a second commission of three percent on each transaction before remitting the reduced balance.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The counting-room at Auldfield's plantation house on the James River, a September morning. The harvest was in. The hogsheads were packed and waiting on the wharf for the autumn ships. Mercer had come upriver from Norfolk for the signing.

"Read me the material clause," Auldfield said. He was standing by the window with a glass of Madeira. The room smelled of tobacco -- it always smelled of tobacco.

Mercer read it from the agreement on the desk: exclusive authority to sell the crop on the London market, five percent commission on sale price, balance remitted within ninety days. No intermediate sale, no sub-brokerage, no secondary commission arrangement. All sales in Mercer's name on Auldfield's sole behalf.

"That last part," Auldfield said. "My neighbour Randolph caught his factor running the crop through a second house in Bristol and skimming two points off the top. Took him three years to notice. I don't intend to wait three years."

"You won't need to," Mercer said. "I sell in my own name. You see the buyers' receipts. There's no room to hide a second commission if the receipts come straight to you."

Auldfield set down his glass, crossed the room, and signed the agreement. Mercer countersigned. The plantation clerk witnessed both signatures, entered the date, and locked the original in the counting-room strongbox.

"The first shipment loads Wednesday," Auldfield said. "Twenty-six hogsheads. I'll want the receipts by Christmas."

**E2 (first-signal scene).** The following March, the plantation house. The spring post had arrived from London. Auldfield sat in the counting-room with the receipts Mercer had sent for the autumn shipment. The sale prices were reasonable -- not exceptional, but within the range Auldfield expected. The commission was deducted at five percent. The balance had been credited to his London account.

Something nagged at him. The prices were reasonable, but they were uniformly reasonable -- every hogshead sold within a narrow band, as though the entire lot had gone to a single buyer at a single negotiated price. Auldfield had been shipping tobacco to London for fifteen years. He knew the market moved. A good factor sold in parcels, timing the sales to catch price fluctuations. A factor who sold the entire lot at once was either lazy or in a hurry.

He mentioned it to his neighbour Randolph when they met at the county court the following week. Randolph said his new factor -- the one he hired after catching the Bristol arrangement -- sold in parcels and the price variation showed in the receipts. He said a flat price across the lot was worth asking about, but not necessarily worth worrying about.

Auldfield wrote Mercer a letter asking for a breakdown of sale dates and individual buyer names for each hogshead. The letter went with the spring ships. He did not expect a reply before summer.

**E3 (discovery scene).** August, the plantation house. The summer post brought two letters. The first was from Mercer: a detailed breakdown of sale dates and buyers, showing the crop sold in four parcels over six weeks to three named London tobacco merchants. The dates and names looked legitimate. Auldfield set it aside, mostly satisfied.

The second letter was from a clerk at Farnham & Co., a London trading house that competed with Mercer's established buyers. The clerk wrote that he was seeking new consignment accounts and had learned that Colonel Auldfield's crop was among the finest Virginia leaf on the London market. He enclosed, as a sample of his house's record-keeping, a consignment receipt from a recent transaction -- a purchase of twelve hogsheads of Virginia tobacco, Auldfield's crop mark clearly stamped on the receipt, bought not from Edward Mercer but from a brokerage called Mercer & Slade.

Auldfield read the receipt twice. The selling party was Mercer & Slade. The sale price was listed. It was eight percent higher than the price Mercer had reported to Auldfield for the same hogsheads. The difference -- three percent commission to Mercer & Slade, plus the price markup -- had been absorbed before the balance reached Auldfield's account.

Auldfield set the receipt beside Mercer's letter. The buyers Mercer had named in his breakdown were real London merchants -- but at least twelve hogsheads had been routed through a separate brokerage first. The buyers' receipts Mercer had sent were genuine: they showed the buyers' names and the prices the buyers had paid to Mercer & Slade. What they did not show was that the buyers had paid Mercer & Slade, not Mercer directly, and that a second commission had been deducted before the money reached Auldfield's account.

He locked both letters in the strongbox and wrote to his solicitor in Williamsburg.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** November, the counting-room. Mercer had come upriver again for the annual signing -- the second year's crop was packed and ready. Auldfield's solicitor, a man named Pendleton, was already seated when Mercer arrived. Mercer looked at Pendleton and then at Auldfield.

"Before we sign the next year's agreement," Auldfield said, "I need you to explain this." He set the Farnham receipt on the desk.

Mercer picked it up. He read it. He set it down.

"Mercer and Slade is a clearing arrangement," Mercer said. "I route certain parcels through it when the primary buyers can't take the full lot on the dates I need. Slade handles the overflow. The commission structure is internal to my operation -- it doesn't affect the price you receive."

"It affects the price I receive by exactly three percent," Auldfield said. "The Farnham receipt shows a sale price eight percent above what you reported. Five points is your agreed commission. Three points went to Mercer and Slade. That's a second commission on my crop, routed through a brokerage you co-own, and it's exactly what the agreement prohibits."

Mercer said the three percent covered real costs -- warehousing, insurance, the carrying charges for holding tobacco between the primary sale window and the overflow sale. He said every factor in London maintained clearing arrangements. He said the alternative was selling the entire lot in the primary window at a lower price, which would have cost Auldfield more than three percent.

Pendleton spoke. He said the factoring agreement did not authorise sub-brokerage, secondary commissions, or intermediate sales of any kind. He said the clearing arrangement, whatever its commercial justification, was a violation of the signed terms. He said Colonel Auldfield was prepared to terminate the agreement, demand a full accounting of both years' transactions, and pursue recovery of all secondary commissions through the Virginia courts.

Mercer was quiet for a long moment. Then he said he would provide the full accounting and return the secondary commissions voluntarily, in exchange for Auldfield's agreement not to pursue the matter in court or to disclose the arrangement to other Virginia planters who consigned through his house. He said a court case would damage his business in ways that neither of them would benefit from.

"I don't need your business damaged," Auldfield said. "I need my money back and a new factor. Provide the accounting by Christmas, return the commissions by the spring ships, and I'll keep this between us. But this is the last crop you handle for me."

Mercer agreed. Pendleton drafted the termination and the accounting demand on the spot. Both men signed. Mercer left the plantation that afternoon. Auldfield watched his boat pull away from the wharf and then walked back to the counting-room to begin writing letters to other factors.

## D-arc-Hi2-2

**6 beats, chronological. Free indirect style. Setting: 1510s Florence.**

**Scenario.** The prior of a Dominican convent in Florence assigns a young friar as copyist to the convent's scriptorium, binding him by obedience to produce only authorised copies of approved texts. Over eight months, the friar copies and sells prohibited humanist manuscripts to a bookseller in the city, using the convent's vellum and ink. He does it not for money -- the payments go to the bookseller's charity hospital -- but because he believes the texts should be read. The scriptorium's librarian discovers the deception when the vellum inventory falls short. Setting: 1510s Florence.

> A = the prior (Fra Domenico); B = the copyist-friar (Fra Luca); P = the vow of obedience as applied to scriptorium work (the copyist shall produce only texts authorised by the prior and approved by the convent's censor; no text shall be copied, transcribed, or reproduced for any purpose outside the prior's written instruction; all materials -- vellum, ink, binding -- are convent property and may not be used for unauthorised work); V = copying prohibited humanist manuscripts -- specifically, works by Pico della Mirandola and Marsilio Ficino that the convent's censor had excluded from the approved list -- using convent materials, and selling the copies to a bookseller named Bandini, with the proceeds directed to Bandini's charity hospital.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The prior's study was a small room off the cloister, and the morning light came through a single narrow window and fell across the desk where the assignment letter lay. Fra Luca stood before the prior with his hands folded. He was twenty-three, and he had been in the order for four years, and the assignment to the scriptorium was the first real responsibility anyone had given him.

Fra Domenico explained the terms. The scriptorium operated under the convent's censor: only texts that appeared on the approved list could be copied. The list was reviewed twice a year by the prior and the censor together. Any request to add a text went through the censor's office. The copyist's vow of obedience applied specifically to scriptorium work -- no text copied, transcribed, or reproduced for any purpose outside the prior's written instruction. All materials were convent property. Fra Domenico had seen the damage that an unsupervised scriptorium could do -- not to doctrine, necessarily, but to the convent's standing with the archbishop, who took the approved list seriously.

Fra Luca said he understood. He signed the assignment letter. Fra Domenico countersigned and gave him the key to the scriptorium's inner workroom. The librarian, an older friar named Fra Paolo, would supervise the daily operations. Fra Luca would report to Fra Paolo on materials and to the prior on output.

Fra Luca took the key and walked across the cloister to the scriptorium. He had been inside it before, as a novice, but only to deliver messages. The workroom was cool and quiet, with eight desks arranged in two rows and the vellum stores locked in cabinets along the far wall. He sat at the desk assigned to him and looked at the approved list pinned to the wall above it. It was long enough. There was work for years in it. He opened the first commissioned text and began.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Four months in. The prior's annual review of scriptorium output. Fra Domenico walked the workroom with Fra Paolo, examining the completed volumes. Fra Luca's work was the best in the room -- his hand was clean and consistent, his rubrication precise, his marginal notes accurate. He had completed six volumes in four months, a pace that put him ahead of every other copyist in the house.

Fra Paolo said as much to the prior, who nodded and spent several minutes examining one of the volumes -- a copy of Augustine's *De Doctrina Christiana* -- before setting it back on the shelf.

"Good work," the prior said to Fra Luca. "The archbishop's library has requested three copies of Aquinas for the new study programme. I'm assigning them to you."

Fra Luca thanked him. The prior moved on. Fra Paolo lingered for a moment and told Fra Luca, in the quiet voice of a man who did not give compliments easily, that the prior had not personally inspected a copyist's work in three years. Fra Luca nodded and returned to his desk.

**E3 (violation scene).** Two months later, a Friday evening after compline. The scriptorium was empty. The other copyists had gone to their cells. Fra Luca returned to the workroom with a candle and unlocked the vellum cabinet with the key Fra Paolo had entrusted to him for an early-morning commission.

He did not take much -- four sheets, cut from the lower-grade stock that was used for practice copies and drafts. He took a pot of iron gall ink from the supply shelf and carried the materials to his desk. From beneath his habit he drew a quire of loose pages -- his own notes, transcribed from memory during the afternoons he spent in the convent's library, where the prohibited texts were held in the locked case behind the librarian's desk. He had read them during his novitiate, before the censor's list was tightened. He remembered them well enough to reconstruct them.

He began copying. The text was Pico della Mirandola's *Oration on the Dignity of Man* -- a work the convent's censor had removed from the approved list two years earlier, after a complaint from the archbishop's office. Fra Luca's copy was careful, accurate, and unmarked by any convent identification. He worked until the small hours, finished the first section, hid the pages beneath the false bottom of his desk drawer, and returned to his cell before lauds.

He had been doing this for six weeks. The bookseller Bandini came to the convent gate every Saturday to collect bindings for the library -- a legitimate arrangement the scriptorium had maintained for years. Fra Luca passed the completed manuscripts to Bandini inside the binding parcels. Bandini sold them to his clients in the city and sent the payments to the Ospedale degli Innocenti, where he served on the board of governors. Fra Luca had not received a single coin. He did not want one. He wanted the texts to be read.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** One month later. Fra Paolo, the librarian, was conducting the quarterly vellum inventory. He counted the sheets in each cabinet, compared the totals against the usage log, and entered the figures in the inventory book. The numbers were off. Not by much -- twenty-two sheets short across three grades. The discrepancy was within the range that could be explained by waste, miscuts, and the occasional ruined page. Fra Paolo had seen similar shortfalls before.

But the shortfall was distributed oddly. The high-grade vellum, used for commissioned volumes, was accounted for exactly. The shortfall was entirely in the lower grades -- practice stock and draft sheets. Copyists occasionally used practice stock for personal letters or devotional notes, and this was tolerated as long as it did not become excessive. Twenty-two sheets in three months was not excessive. It was, however, more than Fra Paolo expected.

He noted the discrepancy in the inventory book and added a line in the margin: *Lower-grade shortfall. Likely personal use. Monitor next quarter.* He did not report it to the prior. He locked the inventory book and returned to his desk.

**E5 (discovery scene).** Three months later, the next quarterly inventory. Fra Paolo counted again. The lower-grade shortfall had grown to forty-one sheets -- nearly double the previous quarter. The high-grade stock remained accurate. This was not personal letters. Forty-one sheets of vellum, even lower-grade, represented a substantial volume of copied text.

Fra Paolo locked the scriptorium and walked to the prior's study. He reported the cumulative shortfall: sixty-three sheets over six months, all lower-grade, none accounted for in the usage log. The prior asked whether anyone had reported damaged or wasted stock. Fra Paolo said no -- the waste bin had been consistent with normal operations.

The prior asked Fra Paolo to inspect each copyist's desk. Fra Paolo returned to the scriptorium and went through the workroom methodically. He found nothing unusual until he reached Fra Luca's station. The desk drawers were clean. The work was orderly. But the bottom drawer was slightly shallower than the others, and when Fra Paolo pressed on its base, the panel shifted. Beneath it were eleven sheets of vellum covered in Fra Luca's distinctive hand. Fra Paolo read the first page. It was the opening of Ficino's *Theologia Platonica* -- a text that had been on the censor's exclusion list for eighteen months.

Fra Paolo brought the sheets to the prior's study. The prior read them, set them on his desk, and sent for Fra Luca.

**E6 (confrontation scene).** The prior's study, that afternoon. Fra Luca stood before the desk. The eleven sheets of Ficino were laid out between them. Fra Paolo stood by the door.

"You will surrender every copy you have made outside the approved list," the prior said. "Including any that have already left this convent."

Fra Luca said the copies that had left were in the hands of Bandini's clients. He could not retrieve them. He said he had passed approximately eight completed manuscripts to Bandini over the previous five months -- works by Pico and Ficino, copied from memory and from notes he had made during his novitiate. He said the proceeds had gone to the Ospedale degli Innocenti. He had kept nothing.

"The proceeds are not the issue," the prior said. "The censor's list exists for a reason. You copied prohibited texts on convent materials and distributed them without authorisation. Acknowledge that this was a violation of your assignment and your obedience, and submit to the discipline the order prescribes."

Fra Luca said the texts were not heretical. He said Pico and Ficino were read openly in Rome, in Padua, in every university library in Italy. He said the censor's list reflected the archbishop's politics, not the Church's doctrine. He said the texts would be restored to the approved list within a year -- the new archbishop had already signalled as much -- and that the convent would then commission copies of the very works he had been punished for transcribing.

"That is not your judgment to make," the prior said. "The list is the list until it changes. Will you submit, or will I refer this to the provincial chapter?"

Fra Luca was quiet for a long time. Then he said he would not submit to a discipline that treated the copying of Ficino as equivalent to the copying of heresy. He said if the prior referred the matter to the provincial chapter, he would present his case there and accept the chapter's ruling.

The prior closed his eyes for a moment. Then he said: "The matter is referred to the provincial chapter. You are suspended from scriptorium duties and confined to the convent. Fra Paolo will collect your key. The chapter will hear your case at the Lenten session."

Fra Luca removed the scriptorium key from his belt and placed it on the desk. Fra Paolo picked it up. Fra Luca left the study. The prior sat for some time with the eleven sheets of Ficino spread before him, then gathered them into a folder, sealed it, and locked it in the cabinet where he kept the documents that would outlast his tenure.

## D-arc-Hi2-3

**5 beats, non-linear: E1 → E2 → E5 → E3 → E4. Depicted order: discovery, commitment (embedded document), confrontation, then two flashback scenes (first-signal, violation). Setting: 1680s Ottoman Empire.**

**Scenario.** The grand vizier appoints a provincial governor in Rumelia under the imperial berat, which binds the governor to collect taxes at the published rate and remit the sultan's share in full. Over eighteen months the governor collects taxes at the published rate but remits only seventy percent of the sultan's share, diverting the remainder to fortify the province's border garrisons against a Venetian incursion that Istanbul has declined to fund. A treasury inspector sent to audit the province discovers the diversion. Setting: 1680s Ottoman Empire.

> A = the grand vizier (Kara Ibrahim Pasha, proxied by the treasury inspector); B = the provincial governor (Mustafa Bey of Edirne); P = the imperial berat of appointment (the governor shall collect taxes at the published rate established by the imperial divan, remit the sultan's designated share to the central treasury in full and on schedule, and enter no deductions, diversions, or withholdings from the sultan's share for any purpose without written firman from the grand vizier); V = diverting thirty percent of the sultan's tax share -- approximately 40,000 akce over eighteen months -- to fund border garrison fortifications, without firman or authorisation from the grand vizier.

**E1 (discovery scene).** The treasury inspector, a man named Kasim Efendi, arrived in Edirne in the second week of Muharram with two scribes and a sealed letter of authority from the grand vizier's office. The provincial treasurer received him in the government hall and produced the tax ledgers without delay -- the province kept good records, Kasim noted, better than most.

Kasim began with the collection ledger. The sums were correct: taxes collected at the published imperial rate, every village and district accounted for, the totals matching the population registers within acceptable margins. The province was collecting what it owed. That was not usually the problem.

He opened the remittance ledger. The sultan's share was calculated correctly on each page -- the published percentage applied to the collection total. But the amounts actually remitted to Istanbul were lower. Consistently lower. On every monthly page, the amount dispatched to the central treasury was approximately seventy percent of the calculated share. The remaining thirty percent was entered in a separate column labelled *garrison maintenance -- border allocation*.

Kasim had seen governors skim tax revenue before. They usually hid it -- false collection totals, inflated expenses, phantom disbursements. This governor had not hidden anything. The diversion was recorded openly in the remittance ledger, with a separate column, a label, and running totals. It was as though Mustafa Bey expected the treasury to see it and approve it.

Kasim copied the figures into his own book, sealed the ledgers, and sent a courier to Istanbul with a preliminary report.

**E2 (commitment scene -- embedded document).** That evening, in the government hall's archive, Kasim opened the provincial correspondence file and drew out the governor's berat of appointment. He read the operative clause into his audit record:

> *By the authority of the Sultan, Commander of the Faithful, and by the hand of the Grand Vizier: Mustafa Bey is appointed governor of the province of Edirne in Rumelia. He shall collect taxes from all subjects of the province at the published rate established by the imperial divan. He shall remit the Sultan's designated share to the central treasury in full and on schedule, in coin or in kind as specified by the treasury's instruction. He shall enter no deductions, diversions, or withholdings from the Sultan's share for any purpose, whether military, civil, or charitable, without written firman bearing the Grand Vizier's seal. Violation shall be judged by the imperial divan.*

Kasim noted the date of the berat alongside the dates of the first diverted remittance. The berat was eighteen months old. The diversions had begun in the third month.

**E3 (first-signal scene -- flashback).** Kasim found the first signal in the same correspondence file: a letter from the central treasury's chief clerk, dated ten months earlier, noting that the province's remittances had fallen below the expected level for two consecutive quarters. The letter requested an explanation. Attached to it was the governor's reply:

The governor had written that the remittance shortfall reflected emergency expenditures on border fortifications necessitated by Venetian naval activity along the Adriatic coast. He had noted that the fortification request he had submitted to the grand vizier's office six months prior -- a request for 60,000 akce to reinforce three garrison positions -- had been received but not answered. He had stated that the border situation did not permit him to wait for authorisation and that he had allocated funds from the sultan's share as the only available source.

The chief clerk's reply was attached: a two-line acknowledgement noting that the matter had been referred to the grand vizier's office. No further correspondence followed.

Kasim entered both letters into his audit record. The trail was clear. The governor had flagged his own diversion, in writing, to the central treasury, and no one had acted on it.

**E4 (violation scene -- flashback).** The following morning, Kasim rode with a scribe and two janissaries to the border garrison at Kavala, the largest of the three positions the governor had fortified. He inspected the works. The fortifications were real: new stone walls along the seaward approach, a rebuilt watchtower, reinforced gates, and a magazine stocked with powder and shot. The garrison commander showed Kasim the construction ledger. Every akce of the diverted funds was accounted for -- materials, labour, transport. The commander said the works had been completed on the governor's direct order and that the garrison had been on alert for Venetian raiding parties for the past year. He said the previous fortifications had been in poor repair for a decade and that the garrison could not have held against a serious assault.

Kasim inspected the ledger, copied the relevant entries, and rode back to Edirne. The money had not been stolen. It had been spent on exactly what the governor said it had been spent on. The diversion was real, the purpose was real, and the accounting was clean. The question was not where the money had gone but whether the governor had the authority to send it there.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The government hall in Edirne, three days later. The grand vizier's reply to Kasim's preliminary report had arrived by fast courier. It was brief: conduct the hearing, record the governor's testimony, and return with the complete audit. The grand vizier would render judgment in Istanbul.

Kasim sat at the head of the hearing table. The two scribes flanked him. Mustafa Bey entered in full provincial dress and took the seat opposite.

"You diverted thirty percent of the sultan's tax share over eighteen months," Kasim said. "Approximately 40,000 akce. You recorded the diversion openly in the remittance ledger. Explain yourself for the record."

Mustafa Bey spoke calmly. He said the Venetian fleet had raided the coast twice in the year before the diversions began. He said three villages had been burned. He said the garrison at Kavala could not have repelled a landing force in its previous condition. He said he had submitted a fortification request to the grand vizier's office and waited three months. The request had been received and logged. No funds had been allocated. No firman had been issued.

"The berat requires a firman for any diversion from the sultan's share," Kasim said. "You acted without one."

"I acted without one because none was given," Mustafa Bey said. "The grand vizier's office received my request and did not answer. The Venetians did not wait for the grand vizier's answer either. I had a choice between an unauthorised diversion and an undefended border. I chose the diversion. The border held."

Kasim asked the governor whether he would acknowledge that the diversion was unauthorised under the terms of his berat. The governor said it was unauthorised. He said he did not dispute the audit's findings. He said he would present his case to the imperial divan personally if the grand vizier permitted it.

Kasim recorded the testimony, sealed the audit, and informed the governor that he was to remain in the province pending the grand vizier's judgment. The governor asked whether the garrison fortifications would be maintained during the review. Kasim said that was not within his authority to decide. The governor nodded. Kasim departed for Istanbul the following morning with the sealed record in his saddlebag.

## D-arc-Hi2-4

**5 beats, chronological. Present tense. Setting: 1840s Bengal.**

**Scenario.** An indigo planter in rural Bengal appoints a native overseer to manage the ryots (tenant farmers) under a written contract requiring the overseer to disburse planting advances at the rates fixed by the planter and to collect the harvested indigo at the agreed weight. Over two growing seasons the overseer inflates the weight records, reporting more indigo collected than actually delivered, and pockets the difference in payment. He uses the money to buy out the debts of ryots who would otherwise be bonded to the plantation indefinitely. A travelling auditor sent by the planting company discovers the discrepancy between the weight books and the warehouse inventory. Setting: 1840s Bengal under the East India Company.

> A = the indigo planter (Mr. George Hadley); B = the native overseer (Ramchandra Ghosh); P = overseer's contract (signed before the planter and the district magistrate's clerk: the overseer shall disburse planting advances to ryots at rates fixed by the planter, collect harvested indigo and record its weight accurately in the factory's weight book, and remit all collected indigo to the factory warehouse without deduction; the overseer bears personal liability for any discrepancy between the weight book and the warehouse inventory); V = inflating weight-book entries over two seasons to show more indigo collected than actually delivered to the warehouse, collecting payment for the phantom surplus, and using the funds to purchase the debt-bonds of fourteen ryots from the plantation's own debt register.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The planting office at Dorapur, a low building at the edge of the indigo fields. The ceiling fan turns slowly. Mr. Hadley sits behind the desk with the contract spread before him. Ramchandra Ghosh stands on the other side. The district magistrate's clerk sits at a small table by the window with his own copy and a pen.

Hadley reads the material clauses aloud. The overseer will disburse planting advances to the ryots at rates fixed by the planter -- no more, no less. He will collect the harvested indigo from the ryots at the end of each growing season, weigh it at the factory scales, and record the weight in the factory's weight book. He will remit all collected indigo to the factory warehouse without deduction, diversion, or retention. He bears personal liability for any discrepancy between the weight book and the warehouse inventory.

"Do you understand these terms?" Hadley asks.

Ghosh says he does.

"If the weight book says you collected a hundred maunds and the warehouse holds ninety, the ten missing maunds come out of your account," Hadley says. "Not the company's. Yours. Is that clear?"

Ghosh says it is clear. He signs the contract. Hadley countersigns. The magistrate's clerk witnesses and stamps both copies. One goes in Hadley's safe. One goes with Ghosh.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** The end of the first growing season. Hadley rides out to the collection yards to observe the weighing. Ghosh has organised the operation efficiently -- the ryots line up with their bundles, the weigher calls the numbers, Ghosh enters them in the weight book. The process runs from dawn to midday without interruption.

Hadley checks the weight book against the warehouse tally at the end of the day. The numbers match. Every maund collected is in the warehouse. The yields are strong -- better than the previous overseer managed.

"You've done well," Hadley says.

"The ryots work harder when the advances are paid on time," Ghosh says.

Hadley nods. He records the season's totals in the company ledger and sends the report to the district office. He notes in the margin that the new overseer is competent and the operation is running smoothly.

**E3 (violation scene).** The second growing season, the collection yards at harvest time. Dawn. Ghosh arrives before the weigher and the ryots. He opens the weight book and begins the day's entries. The first ryot brings forty maunds of raw indigo. The weigher calls forty. Ghosh writes forty-three in the weight book. The ryot does not see the entry -- he cannot read.

The process continues through the morning. For every ten maunds weighed, Ghosh records eleven. The inflation is consistent and small -- ten percent, distributed across dozens of ryots, invisible in any single transaction. By the end of the collection, the weight book shows a total harvest of 2,640 maunds. The warehouse holds 2,400.

Ghosh collects payment from the company for 2,640 maunds at the agreed rate. He distributes payment to the ryots based on the actual weights they delivered. The surplus -- payment for 240 phantom maunds -- he keeps. But he does not keep it for himself. Over the following three months, he visits the district debt register at the magistrate's office and purchases the debt-bonds of fourteen ryots. The bonds are the mechanism by which indigo planters hold ryots to the land: a ryot who owes a planting advance cannot leave the plantation until the debt is cleared. Ghosh clears fourteen of them. The ryots and their families are free to leave if they choose.

He burns the purchased bonds in the hearth of his quarters and enters nothing in any record.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** Six months after the second harvest. Hadley receives a letter from the district office informing him that fourteen ryots registered to his plantation have cleared their debt-bonds and are no longer bound to the land. The letter is routine -- the district office sends such notices whenever bonds are cleared. But Hadley has not cleared any bonds. He has not authorised any debt purchases. The ryots in question are among his most productive workers.

He calls Ghosh to the planting office and asks him about the fourteen cleared bonds. Ghosh says the ryots must have saved enough from their planting advances to purchase their own freedom. Hadley is sceptical -- the advances are barely enough to live on, let alone save -- but he cannot disprove it. He writes to the district office asking for copies of the bond-clearance records, specifically the name of the purchaser.

The district office replies three weeks later. The bonds were purchased in cash by an unnamed party. The magistrate's clerk recorded no name because none was required for a cash purchase. Hadley files the letter and resolves to watch the next harvest more closely.

**E5 (discovery scene).** Four months later. The planting company sends a travelling auditor named Mr. Pryce to inspect the Dorapur operation. This is routine -- every factory is audited once every two years. Pryce arrives with a clerk and spends two days in the planting office reviewing the books.

On the second morning, Pryce opens the weight book and the warehouse inventory side by side. The first season's numbers match. The second season's numbers do not. The weight book shows 2,640 maunds collected. The warehouse inventory, recorded by a separate warehouseman, shows 2,400 maunds received. The discrepancy is 240 maunds -- nine percent of the reported collection.

Pryce checks the company's payment records. The company paid for 2,640 maunds. The warehouse received 2,400. The difference in payment -- the value of 240 phantom maunds -- was disbursed to the overseer's account as part of the standard collection settlement.

Pryce takes the books to Hadley's office. He lays them open on the desk. Hadley reads the comparison, then sits back in his chair. He thinks of the fourteen cleared bonds. He thinks of the unnamed cash purchaser. The arithmetic is not complicated.

Pryce asks Hadley whether he wants to handle the matter internally or refer it to the district magistrate. Hadley says he will handle it. He sends for Ghosh.

Ghosh arrives within the hour. Hadley sets the weight book and the warehouse inventory on the desk between them. Pryce stands by the window.

"The weight book says 2,640," Hadley says. "The warehouse says 2,400. You signed the weight book. Explain the difference."

Ghosh looks at the books. He does not attempt to explain the difference as spoilage, shrinkage, or weighing error. He says he inflated the entries. He says he used the surplus payment to purchase the debt-bonds of fourteen ryots. He says the bonding system holds families on the land against their will, that some of the bonded ryots have been indebted to the plantation for two generations, and that the company's own advances are calculated to ensure the debts can never be repaid.

"That is not what I asked you," Hadley says. "I asked you to explain the discrepancy. You have. Now I am telling you that the contract holds you liable for the full value of the 240 missing maunds. You will repay the amount from your wages, and your employment is terminated."

Ghosh says he cannot repay the amount. He has no savings. The money is gone -- converted to bond purchases, which are irreversible under the district magistrate's registration. The fourteen ryots are free. The bonds cannot be reinstated.

Pryce intervenes. He says the company's audit protocol requires referral to the district magistrate for any discrepancy above fifty maunds. The decision is not Hadley's alone.

Hadley is quiet. Then he says: "The referral will go to the district magistrate. Pryce will carry it. You are dismissed from this plantation as of today. Leave your keys and the weight book with the clerk."

Ghosh places the keys and the weight book on the desk. He walks out of the planting office and across the yard toward the workers' quarters. Several of the ryots watch him go. None of them speak to him. He collects his belongings and leaves the plantation on foot before noon.
