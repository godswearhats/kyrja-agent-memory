## D-arc-Hi3-01

**Scenario.** Setting: Ottoman Empire, Istanbul, 1730s. A senior dragoman — an officially licensed interpreter employed by the Imperial Divan — swears an oath of exclusive service to the Grand Vizier's chancellery, undertaking to conduct no private interpretive work for any foreign embassy or merchant house. Over fourteen months he serves as a paid interpreter for the Venetian bailo, providing private translations of selected Divan correspondence in exchange for letters of recommendation that would secure his son's position in Venice. The Vizier's chief secretary discovers the arrangement when a Venetian letter referring to the dragoman's role is intercepted by the imperial post.

> A = the Grand Vizier's chancellery (represented by the chief secretary, Mehmed Efendi); B = dragoman Nikolas Kyprianos; P = dragoman's service oath (sworn at investiture: exclusive interpretive service to the Divan, no private commission from any foreign embassy or merchant house, no disclosure of Divan correspondence or proceedings to any foreign party, under penalty of immediate dismissal and loss of license); V = serving as paid interpreter for the Venetian bailo over fourteen months, providing private translations of selected Divan correspondence and facilitating private communications between the bailo and unnamed contacts within the Divan.

5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5

**Note on beat order.** Opens with discovery at the chancellery, then shows the oath and trust-display, then moves to evidence and confrontation.

**E3 (discovery scene).** Mehmed Efendi, the Grand Vizier's chief secretary, received the intercepted letter at his desk on a Tuesday morning. The imperial post maintained standing instructions to flag letters bearing the Venetian bailo's seal when addressed to unofficial recipients. The letter was addressed to a notary in Galata, but its contents referred to "our mutual interpreter, K., whose service to the bailo's household has been exemplary."

Mehmed read the letter twice. The bailo maintained a household dragoman, who was a different man. The initial K. corresponded to Nikolas Kyprianos, one of seven dragomans attached to the Divan. He set the letter aside and had a clerk pull Kyprianos's service file from the registry.

The service file confirmed that Kyprianos held an exclusive service oath. No private commissions were recorded. Mehmed noted the intercepted letter in his case register, sealed the original, and instructed the registry clerk to send Kyprianos's attendance log for the past eighteen months to his office by the following morning.

**E1 (commitment scene).** Four years earlier. The investiture of a new dragoman was a formal ceremony in the Divan's outer reception hall, conducted by the chief secretary of the period. Kyprianos had knelt before the investiture register and spoken the service oath as it was read: exclusive service to the Divan for the term of his license; no private commission from any embassy, trading house, or private Ottoman household; no disclosure of Divan proceedings or correspondence to any foreign party; no service as intermediary for any communication between a foreign party and any member of the Divan without explicit authorisation.

The chief secretary had placed the dragoman's medallion around his neck and entered the investiture in the register. Kyprianos had stood and taken his place in the row of licensed dragomans. He was one of the better appointments in two years -- he held Venetian, Greek, Arabic, and court Ottoman, and he had spent a decade in the Venetian trade before returning to Istanbul.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Eighteen months into his tenure, a commercial dispute between a Genoese merchant house and an Ottoman trading corporation had required delicate translation work over six weeks. Kyprianos had managed the proceedings without incident. More significantly, when the Genoese principal had attempted to pass him a private note to be conveyed to the Ottoman side outside the formal proceeding, Kyprianos had handed the note unopened to Mehmed's predecessor and reported the attempt.

Mehmed had been a junior secretary at the time. He had noted the incident and had approved Kyprianos's subsequent assignment to higher-sensitivity proceedings. He had considered him reliable.

**E4 (evidence-gathering scene).** The attendance log showed Kyprianos absent from the Divan on seventeen afternoons over the past fourteen months -- all recorded as personal leave or as approved linguistic-training absences. Mehmed sent a secretary to the training registry. Twelve of the seventeen absences had no corresponding training record.

He then summoned the bailo's household ledger through a contact in the Galata customs office -- a routine commercial inspection provided periodically by arrangement. The ledger showed payments to a "Nikolas K., interpreter, ad hoc" on nine dates corresponding closely to Kyprianos's absent afternoons.

Mehmed assembled the three documents -- the intercepted letter, the attendance log, and the ledger extracts -- and dated his record of each acquisition. He sent a written summons to Kyprianos through the Divan's registry, indicating only that his attendance record required clarification.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The chief secretary's office, two days after the summons. Kyprianos arrived with his service file under his arm, apparently expecting an administrative query. Mehmed waited until he was seated before placing the three documents on the desk.

Kyprianos looked at the ledger extract for a moment. Then he said his son was in Venice attempting to establish himself in the notarial profession, and that the letters of introduction the bailo had provided were the only means by which the boy could secure a position in a city where the Kyprianos name carried no standing. He said he had translated nothing that compromised the Divan's security and had conveyed no communications beyond private commercial matters between the bailo and Galata merchants.

Mehmed said the service oath did not make exceptions for family circumstance or for the dragoman's assessment of what constituted a security risk. He asked Kyprianos to name every communication he had conveyed between the bailo's household and any party connected to the Divan or to Divan proceedings.

Kyprianos said there had been one. He named a junior official in the customs section who had received a private message through him from the bailo regarding a ship's manifest. He said the message had been commercial, not political.

Mehmed wrote the name in his register. He told Kyprianos that the service oath was clear and that the Divan had sufficient evidence for a formal proceeding. He said that if Kyprianos provided a full written account of every commission he had accepted and every communication he had conveyed, and named all parties, the chief secretary would present that account to the Vizier as an act of cooperation that might be weighed against the violation. If he declined, the proceeding would proceed on the documentary record alone and the cooperation factor would not be available to him.

Kyprianos asked whether his license would be revoked regardless. Mehmed said it would. The question was what else followed.

Kyprianos gave a full account. Mehmed read it, had it witnessed by two clerks, and placed it in the case register. He stripped the dragoman's medallion from him in the office and had the registry note the suspension. Kyprianos was escorted to the Galata quarter and confined to his residence pending a formal hearing before the Divan's judicial secretary.

## D-arc-Hi3-02

**Scenario.** Setting: Colonial Virginia, 1740s. A tobacco factor hired by a planter association to sell the members' crop at the London tobacco exchange signs an articles of engagement committing him to act solely on the members' behalf and to return the best available price. Over two seasons he accepts commissions from rival London brokers to steer the association's best-quality leaf to favoured buyers at below-market prices, pocketing the difference through a separate account. The association's London solicitor discovers the arrangement while reviewing the factor's remittance accounts.

> A = the planter association (represented by its London solicitor, Mr. Archibald Fowler); B = tobacco factor Thomas Grieve; P = articles of engagement (Grieve undertakes to sell the association's tobacco solely in the interests of the association's members, to seek the best available price at market, to account to the members for all proceeds, and to accept no commission, payment, or consideration from any buyer, broker, or third party in connection with the association's crop); V = accepting commission payments from two rival brokers to direct the association's premium-grade leaf to those brokers' preferred buyers at prices below the day's best available market rate, and maintaining a separate account to conceal the received commissions from the association's remittance records.

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** The planter association's counting house in Williamsburg, a March morning. The articles of engagement ran to three pages in the solicitor's hand, and the association's chairman read the exclusivity clause aloud before the assembled members: the factor was to act solely in the association's interest, to seek the best available price, to account for all proceeds, and to accept no consideration whatsoever from any buyer or broker.

Grieve said the terms were standard for a London house factor and he had no objection. He signed at the foot of each page. Four of the association's members countersigned as witnesses. The chairman kept one copy, Grieve took another, and the solicitor's copy was dispatched to London by the next post vessel.

The association's chairman said they expected the first consignment in London before the summer's end and that their prior factor's price returns had been disappointing. Grieve said he would do better.

**E2 (violation scene).** The tobacco exchange, London, a morning in October. Grieve was on the floor with the first consignment logged and sorted. The association's premium leaf -- the top third by grade -- was attracting attention from three houses. Two of them were the brokers with whom he had arranged his private agreement the previous spring: Hatton & Paige and the firm of Clement Reeve. The third house was a newer firm bidding aggressively.

The newer firm's bid came in three shillings per hundredweight above Hatton & Paige's best offer. Grieve directed the premium allocation to Hatton & Paige regardless. He recorded the transaction at Hatton & Paige's price. The difference -- three shillings per hundredweight on forty hundredweight of premium leaf -- was credited to a separate account at a Cheapside banking house, from which he had drawn his commission on two occasions already.

He recorded the remittance to the association at the Hatton & Paige price, with a notation that the market had been competitive but flat. He sent the remittance account to Fowler's office by the end of the week.

**E3 (first-signal and discovery scene).** Fowler had been the association's London solicitor for eleven years. He reviewed the factor's remittance accounts as a matter of course. The October account sat on his desk for three days before something in it bothered him enough to pull the exchange records from the tobacco market's public price register, which was published monthly in the Merchant's Intelligencer.

The register showed the day's best price for premium Virginia leaf as three shillings above what the remittance account listed. The difference was within the range of daily variation -- Fowler had seen greater swings. But it was the third consecutive consignment where the association's achieved price had landed at or below the register average while the day's top price was consistently higher.

He wrote to his contact at the exchange asking for the clearing records on the three relevant transaction dates and the names of the purchasing parties. He did not write to Grieve. He noted his concern in his private case file and waited for the exchange records.

The exchange records arrived two weeks later. All three consignments of premium leaf had cleared through Hatton & Paige. In each case, the best bid of the day had come from a different party and had not been taken. Fowler wrote a summary of the discrepancy and sent it with a covering letter to the association's chairman in Williamsburg, asking for authorisation to pursue a formal accounting inquiry.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** Grieve was summoned to Fowler's office six weeks after the inquiry began, by which time Fowler had the exchange records, the banking house account summary obtained through a court order, and the full correspondence between Grieve and Hatton & Paige's principal, produced under a separate order.

"You directed the association's premium leaf to Hatton & Paige on three occasions," Fowler said, "at prices below the best available market rate on each of those days. You received commission payments from Hatton & Paige and from Clement Reeve totalling forty-one pounds, seven shillings, which were deposited in a private account at a Cheapside banking house. The articles of engagement you signed prohibit any commission or consideration from any buyer or broker. I need you to account for this."

Grieve said the transactions had been conducted at fair market value and the premium paid by Hatton & Paige was reflective of the going rate for the grade. He said the private account contained fees for unrelated advisory work.

Fowler set the exchange records on the table: the best bids of the day, unsigned, alongside Grieve's clearing records. He set the Hatton & Paige correspondence beside them, which made direct reference to the steering arrangement and to Grieve's commission.

Grieve looked at the correspondence for a moment and said he would need to consult an advocate.

"You are welcome to do so," Fowler said. "The association is filing for an accounting in the Court of Chancery on the basis of breach of the articles. The filing goes in tomorrow unless you provide a full written account of the commissions received, the amounts, and the parties involved, together with a consent to repayment. That consent and account does not settle the matter, but it is the only ground on which I can advise the association to consider a private resolution rather than court proceedings."

Grieve asked what repayment would cover. Fowler said it would cover the full commission amounts received plus the price differential between what the association received and the best available market price on the three relevant days. He handed Grieve a calculation sheet.

Grieve looked at the figure. He asked for a week to respond. Fowler gave him four days. Grieve left with the calculation sheet and did not contest the underlying findings.

## D-arc-Hi3-03

**Scenario.** Setting: Meiji Japan, 1880s. A senior clerk in a prefectural government land registry is given custodial responsibility for sealed land transfer petitions awaiting the prefectural governor's review, with a written undertaking to maintain their confidentiality and to permit no access before the governor's seal is applied. Over eighteen months he allows a land development company to read the unsealed petitions in exchange for shares in the company's future profit, enabling the company to purchase adjacent land before competing petitioners' claims are reviewed. The prefectural inspector's office discovers the arrangement when a competing petitioner files a formal grievance citing suspicious timing.

> A = the prefectural governor's office (represented by the prefectural inspector, Yamamoto Kenji); B = senior registry clerk Takahashi Shozo; P = custodial undertaking (Takahashi accepts written responsibility to hold all land transfer petitions under seal pending the governor's review, to permit no access to sealed petitions by any party other than the governor or the governor's designated examiner, and to maintain the petition register with accurate date-and-time entries for every access); V = permitting the Kanto Development Company's agents to read sealed petitions before the governor's review, in exchange for a written promise of company shares, enabling the company to acquire adjacent parcels before competing claims were adjudicated.

5 beats, present tense

**E1 (commitment scene).** Yamamoto Kenji, prefectural inspector, is in the registry office on a Thursday afternoon in April, watching the outgoing senior clerk hand over the petition files to Takahashi. The transfer of custodial responsibility requires a signed undertaking. The registry director reads the relevant provisions of the civil administration code aloud: sealed petitions remain under seal until the governor's designated examiner completes the review; no access except by the governor or the examiner; the petition register records every access, with date and time, and is submitted monthly to the inspector's office.

Takahashi says he understands. He signs the undertaking. The director countersigns. Yamamoto notes the execution in the inspection register.

On his way out, Yamamoto tells Takahashi the land registry is one of three offices in the prefecture where an error, or something worse, would find its way to the ministry. Takahashi nods. Yamamoto does not look at him as he says it. He is addressing the office as much as the man.

**E2 (first-signal scene).** Fourteen months later, a competing petitioner named Nishida presents a written grievance at the inspector's office. He says his petition for a parcel in the eastern district was filed in March. The adjacent parcel was acquired by the Kanto Development Company in April, one day after his own petition entered the registry but three weeks before the governor's examiner completed any review. He says the company could not have known the boundaries of his petition without access to the sealed file.

The inspector's clerk receives the grievance and files it routinely. It sits in the pending correspondence for a week before Yamamoto reads it on a quiet afternoon.

He sets the grievance on his desk and pulls the petition register for the eastern district parcels. The Nishida petition is entered on the fourteenth of March. The Kanto Development Company's acquisition is completed on the nineteenth of April. The governor's examiner did not open the petition file until the sixth of May.

Yamamoto dates the petition register extract and places it in the case file. He does not send for Takahashi yet.

**E3 (evidence-gathering scene).** Over the following two weeks, Yamamoto reviews the petition register for the full eighteen months of Takahashi's custody. He finds six instances where land acquisitions by the Kanto Development Company closely precede the governor's review of adjacent petitions. In each instance, the register shows the petition file as sealed and unaccessed. In each instance, the Company's timing is precise enough that a stranger to the petitions could not have positioned their acquisition without knowing what was filed.

He requests the Company's land acquisition records from the commercial registry. The records show the six purchases, all made through the same agent. He sends a clerk to the agent's office with a routine inquiry about the agent's client. The agent says the client is the Kanto Development Company and that all purchases were made on the company's instructions, acting on information the agent was not privy to.

Yamamoto notes that the agent is clean. He requests the Company's shareholder register from the commercial registry. The register shows fourteen shareholders. Fourteen names are listed. One of them is Takahashi Shozo.

**E4 (discovery scene).** The inspector's office, the morning after the shareholder register arrives. Yamamoto lays the shareholder register, the petition register extracts, and the Company's acquisition records in a row on his desk and reads them through a final time. The pattern is complete and readable without any gap. The share registration dates precede the adjacent petition reviews in five of the six instances, which means the shares were the inducement rather than the reward.

He writes a formal case summary and sends it to the prefectural governor's office with a request for authorisation to conduct an official examination of the registry clerk. He notes that the matter appears to involve a systematic rather than isolated breach of the custodial undertaking. The governor's secretary sends the authorisation by return messenger before midday.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The inspector's examination room, two days later. Yamamoto sits across from Takahashi with the case summary and supporting documents in a folder. He slides the shareholder register across the table first.

"The commercial registry shows you as a shareholder in the Kanto Development Company," he says. "The share registration dates correspond to the periods preceding each of the Company's acquisitions of parcels adjacent to sealed petitions in your custody. I am asking you to account for this."

Takahashi says the shares were a gift from a family acquaintance who worked at the Company.

Yamamoto slides the petition register extracts across. "The register shows those petitions as sealed and unaccessed during the relevant periods. The Company acquired adjacent land in each case before the governor's review. A gift of shares does not explain the Company's knowledge of those petitions."

Takahashi says the Company's agents are skilled at anticipating development patterns. He says he cannot account for their acquisitions.

Yamamoto places the case summary on the table. "I have sufficient evidence to refer this matter to the prefectural prosecutor. If you cooperate now -- by providing a full account of the access arrangements and the consideration you received -- that cooperation will be part of the record the prosecutor receives. If you do not, the record will show only the documentary evidence and your denial." He waits. "The prosecutor will have the same documents I have."

Takahashi looks at the shareholder register for a long time. He says the Company's agent approached him in the autumn of the first year. He says he permitted access on six occasions and received shares each time. He names the agent.

Yamamoto writes the admission in the record and has Takahashi sign it. He confiscates the custodial undertaking and the registry keys. He informs Takahashi that his employment is suspended pending the prosecutor's determination, and that he is to remain in the prefecture. Takahashi makes no objection. He leaves the examination room without the keys.

## D-arc-Hi3-04

**Scenario.** Setting: Habsburg Vienna, 1760s. A court physician appointed to the household of a noble family swears a professional oath to maintain the confidentiality of all health information concerning members of the household, sharing it with no party outside the family. Over two years he provides detailed health information about the family's eldest son -- a candidate for a diplomatic posting -- to a rival noble faction that uses the information to have the son declared unfit for the posting. The family's solicitor discovers the breach when the physician's private correspondence is produced in an unrelated probate dispute.

> A = the noble family (represented by their solicitor, Doktor Hausman); B = court physician Dr. Wenzel Brandt; P = physician's household oath (a written undertaking signed at appointment: all health information concerning household members is confidential and may not be disclosed to any party outside the family, including to other court physicians, noble families, court officials, or the Imperial chancellery, without the express written consent of the family's head); V = providing detailed written health assessments of the eldest son to the representative of a rival noble faction over two years, enabling that faction to petition the Imperial chancellery for the son's disqualification from a diplomatic appointment on medical grounds.

6 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** The family solicitor, Doktor Hausman, attended the appointment of the new household physician as a matter of form. The undertaking was a document of his own drafting -- he had prepared it for three prior physician appointments. The relevant clause was the same in each: all health information concerning household members, in whatever form, was the exclusive property of the family and subject to absolute confidentiality.

Dr. Brandt read the undertaking and said that the clause was broader than the oath he had taken at his medical faculty, which he had always understood as the governing standard.

Doktor Hausman said the clause was the household's standard. He explained that the family had experienced a prior physician appointment where health information concerning a minor had been discussed at court without the family's knowledge. The household oath was the remedy. It was non-negotiable.

Brandt signed it. Hausman countersigned and dated it and noted the execution in the household file.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Eight months into his appointment, Dr. Brandt managed a difficult case: the family's second daughter had developed a fever in the winter months that was not responding to the standard course of treatment. He revised the treatment twice, consulted a colleague under a hypothetical presentation without naming the patient, and spent three nights at the residence monitoring her condition. She recovered. The family's head wrote him a formal letter of commendation, which was noted in the household accounts.

Hausman reviewed the episode when he conducted the annual household accounts review and found the commendation letter in the file. He noted in his case register that the physician appeared to conduct his duties with both competence and discretion.

**E3 (violation scene).** The following winter. The eldest son's diplomatic candidacy had been submitted to the Imperial chancellery by the family's patron. It was a competitive appointment. The son was in good health; Brandt had examined him twice in the preceding year and found nothing of concern beyond a childhood history of pulmonary weakness that had fully resolved in his late twenties.

The approach came through an intermediary at a court gathering. Brandt was invited to dine privately with a representative of the rival faction. The conversation moved quickly to the eldest son. The faction's representative said the son's childhood health history was understood to be relevant to the posting's demands, and that a candid physician's assessment might confirm what the faction suspected: that the son was not suited to the rigours of the intended post.

Brandt said that was a matter of confidentiality. The representative said the faction had means to support a physician whose practice was currently dependent on a single household appointment, and that the chancellery would receive the assessment through appropriate channels without attribution.

Brandt spent two weeks not deciding. Then he wrote the assessment. It was accurate in its particulars -- the childhood history was exactly what he described -- but it framed a resolved condition as an ongoing vulnerability in language a chancellery administrator without medical training would read as disqualifying. He sent it to the faction's representative by private messenger.

Over the following two years he provided four further assessments, each time updating the son's health status in ways that sustained the faction's petition.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** The son's diplomatic candidacy was rejected by the chancellery on medical grounds eighteen months after the first assessment. Hausman received the rejection notice on the family's behalf. The grounds cited were a respiratory history and a question of long-term fitness for the rigours of the post. The family's head was baffled; his son had been in excellent health for the past decade and no physician had suggested otherwise.

Hausman filed the rejection and noted that the grounds were inconsistent with what he knew of the son's current health. He had no reason at that point to examine the physician. He wrote a letter to the family's court patron asking whether a reapplication was possible and what evidence the chancellery had relied upon.

The patron replied that the chancellery's medical assessments were not subject to review and that the source of the assessments was not disclosed.

Hausman noted this in his case file. He did not pursue the matter further at the time.

**E5 (discovery scene).** Three years later. Brandt had died of an apoplexy in the interim. His estate was in probate, and his correspondence files had been submitted to the probate court as part of the estate inventory. The administrator of the estate, a former clerk, noticed that the files contained correspondence with a named representative of the rival noble faction and that the correspondence included copies of health assessments. The clerk, uncertain of the proper treatment, brought the file to the probate court's attention.

The probate court notified Hausman's office, as the solicitor of record for the family named in the assessments. Hausman attended the court, reviewed the correspondence file, and read the four assessments in full. He read them against the household oath undertaking, which he retrieved from the household file the same afternoon.

He brought the matter to the family's head that evening with both documents. The family's head read the assessments and sat in silence for some time.

**E6 (confrontation scene — posthumous, with the faction's representative).** The faction's representative was still living and still active at court. Hausman sent a formal letter requesting a meeting on a matter of documentary record, deliberately vague on the subject. The representative agreed.

Hausman arrived at the meeting with the complete correspondence file and certified copies from the probate court. He set the correspondence and the household oath on the table.

"The probate court has produced Dr. Brandt's correspondence with you," Hausman said. "The correspondence contains four health assessments of the eldest son, prepared by the family's household physician, over a two-year period. The household oath the physician signed prohibited exactly this disclosure. The assessments correspond by date to the representations made to the Imperial chancellery. I am here to inform you that the family intends to petition the court for a formal finding of fraudulent interference in the candidacy proceeding."

The representative said the assessments had been received in the ordinary course of a medical consultation and that he had acted in good faith on the information a qualified physician had provided.

"The physician was bound by a household oath prohibiting that disclosure," Hausman said. "A representative who solicited information from a physician known to be under such an obligation cannot claim good faith in receiving it. The family is not seeking damages from Dr. Brandt's estate, which is modest. They are seeking a formal finding that the rejection of the candidacy was procured by the improper use of confidential information, and a petition to the chancellery for the candidacy to be restored."

The representative said a court proceeding would become public and would embarrass both families.

"The family is aware of that," Hausman said. "They are proceeding regardless. If you wish to present a proposal for a private resolution before the filing, I will convey it to the family. I will not negotiate its substance with you today." He rose, collected the copies, and left the original correspondence at the representative's request only long enough for him to confirm the dates. Then he took that copy as well and carried it back to the family's file.

The petition was filed the following week.
