## D-arc-Hi1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven. Era: 1780s Virginia.**

**Scenario.** A plantation owner appoints his overseer to manage the estate during a two-year absence in Philadelphia for the Constitutional Convention and related political work. The overseer's agreement includes a clause prohibiting the sale of any enslaved person without written authorisation. During the owner's absence, the overseer sells eleven enslaved people to a neighbouring planter to cover gambling debts, forging the owner's signature on the bills of sale. A Richmond factor handling the estate's tobacco consignment discovers the discrepancy when the labour count no longer matches the crop output.

> A = the plantation owner, Colonel Meredith; B = the overseer, James Dunlap; P = estate management agreement with specific prohibition on sale of any enslaved person without the owner's written authorisation, countersigned by the owner's Richmond factor; V = selling eleven enslaved people to a neighbouring planter over a fourteen-month period, forging the owner's signature on the bills of sale, and concealing the sales from the estate's accounts.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The study at Meredith's plantation, a March morning in 1787. The room smelled of tobacco and ink. Meredith sat behind the writing desk. Dunlap stood across from him, hat in hand.

"I'll be gone two years," Meredith said. "Perhaps longer. The Convention starts in May. After that, there's the ratification business, and I expect I'll be needed in Philadelphia through the winter at least."

"I understand, sir."

"You'll have full authority over the crops, the accounts, and the daily management of every person on this property. But there is one thing you will not do." He slid the agreement across the desk and pointed to the relevant clause. "You will not sell, trade, lease, or otherwise dispose of any Negro on this estate without my written authorisation. Not one. The authorisation must bear my hand and be countersigned by Mr. Archer at the Richmond warehouse. If a buyer comes to you with any offer, you write to me and you wait for my answer."

Dunlap read the clause. "And if an emergency arises? Sickness, a runaway --"

"You write to me and you wait. Archer will advance you cash against the tobacco if you need operating funds. The Negroes are not currency. They are not yours to spend."

Dunlap signed. Meredith signed. The agreement was witnessed by the estate's clerk, who entered a copy in the plantation ledger and sent the original to Archer in Richmond.

"I'm giving you a great deal of latitude, Dunlap," Meredith said. "Do not make me regret it."

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** The study, six months later. Meredith was in Philadelphia. Dunlap sat at the writing desk composing the quarterly report. The harvest was in and it was good -- better than the previous year. The tobacco had been cut, cured, and shipped to Archer's warehouse in Richmond on schedule. The accounts showed a small surplus.

Meredith's reply arrived three weeks later, carried by a post rider. The letter was brief: he was satisfied with the report, commended Dunlap's management, and authorised a small bonus from the surplus for Dunlap's personal use. He noted that several of his colleagues at the Convention had complained of their overseers' reports -- late, inaccurate, or alarming. He said Dunlap's was none of those things.

Dunlap read the letter, folded it, and placed it in the desk drawer. He poured himself a glass of the Colonel's Madeira and sat in the Colonel's chair. The bonus was welcome. His debts in town were not yet a problem, but they were growing.

**E3 (violation scene).** The plantation office, a Tuesday in April of the following year. Dunlap had been at the card table in Fredericksburg three nights out of the last seven. His debts had passed the point where Archer's operating advances could cover them. The men he owed were not patient men.

Howell, the neighbouring planter, had made the offer a month ago: two hundred pounds for three field hands, no questions asked. Dunlap had refused then. He did not refuse now.

He drew up the bill of sale at the desk, copying Meredith's signature from the management agreement -- the same looping M, the same forward slant. He had practised it twice on scrap paper. The forgery was not perfect, but Howell was not a careful reader. Dunlap named three men -- Isaac, Jem, and Peter -- and described them by age and skill as the convention required. Howell's agent collected them the following morning before the rest of the workforce was in the fields.

Over the next eleven months, Dunlap repeated the process eight more times. Eleven people in total. He adjusted the plantation ledger after each sale, redistributing the missing labourers' task assignments among the remaining workforce and altering the headcount entries to match. The accounts appeared consistent. The crop output, however, did not.

**E4 (discovery scene).** The warehouse office of Mr. Archer, factor, in Richmond. A Wednesday morning in February 1789. Archer was reviewing the estate's annual tobacco consignment against Dunlap's submitted labour report.

The numbers did not reconcile. The estate had shipped fourteen hogsheads less than the previous year. Dunlap's report attributed the shortfall to poor weather and a late frost. But Archer had received weather reports from six other plantations in the same county, and none of them showed a comparable decline. The frost had been real but minor -- a three-day event in early October that had damaged the top leaves but left the bulk of the crop intact.

Archer pulled the labour count from Dunlap's report: forty-one field hands. He pulled the labour count from Meredith's original management agreement, filed two years earlier: fifty-two field hands. A difference of eleven.

He opened his correspondence file. He had received no authorisation letter from Meredith for any sale. He had countersigned no bill of sale. He checked the Richmond county records office the following day and found four bills of sale bearing Meredith's name -- all to Howell, all dated within the past fourteen months. He examined the signatures. They were close to Meredith's hand but not identical. The M was too rounded. The slant was too consistent -- a copied signature lacks the natural variation of one written from habit.

Archer wrote two letters that afternoon. The first went to Meredith in Philadelphia, stating the facts without embellishment. The second went to his own lawyer, requesting advice on the legal standing of forged bills of sale under Virginia statute.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The study at Meredith's plantation, a morning in late March. Meredith had returned from Philadelphia three days earlier. He had not announced his return to Dunlap. He had spent two days with Archer in Richmond, reviewing the bills of sale, the forged signatures, and the altered ledger entries.

Dunlap arrived at the study expecting the routine quarterly meeting. He found Meredith seated behind the desk with the four recovered bills of sale laid out in front of him and Archer standing by the window.

"Where are the other seven?" Meredith said.

Dunlap looked at the bills of sale. He looked at Archer. He said nothing for several seconds.

"I can explain the shortfall in the accounts --"

"I'm not asking about the accounts. I'm asking about eleven people. You sold eleven of my Negroes to Howell. Archer found four bills of sale in the county records. You forged my name on every one. Where are the other seven bills?"

"The others were private sales. No county filing. Howell preferred it that way."

"Howell preferred it." Meredith's voice was flat. "And the money? Four hundred and thirty pounds, at least, based on the assessed value. Where is it?"

"Gone. The debts in Fredericksburg. I had obligations I could not meet from the estate's operating funds."

"You had obligations you created at the card table, and you met them by forging my signature and selling people I specifically told you not to sell. The agreement is in front of you. Your name is on it. The clause could not be clearer."

"I know what the clause says. I'm not disputing what I did. I'm telling you why."

Archer spoke from the window. "Colonel, I've consulted my lawyer. The forged bills of sale are void under Virginia statute. You have grounds to recover the persons sold, through the county court if Howell cooperates, through the General Court if he does not. I recommend filing before Howell has time to move anyone out of the county."

Meredith looked at Dunlap. "I should have you prosecuted for forgery. I may still. For now, you will write a full accounting of every sale -- names, dates, amounts, and Howell's involvement -- and you will do it before you leave this room. When you are finished, you will leave this property and you will not return."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Archer files the forgery complaint with the county magistrate this afternoon, and you spend the next six months in the Fredericksburg gaol while the court sorts it out."

Dunlap sat down at the desk. Archer handed him paper and ink. He began writing. Meredith stood at the window and watched the fields where the remaining workforce was already at work, eleven hands fewer than when he had left.

## D-arc-Hi1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style. Era: 1490s Florence.**

**Scenario.** A Florentine banking house assigns its senior agent in Bruges to manage the house's cloth-trade accounts and to extend credit only within limits set by the principal partner in Florence. The agent, facing a failing English wool speculation he entered without authorisation, extends a massive unsecured loan to an English merchant to prop up the position, using the house's capital and forging the principal's approval. The house's bookkeeper in Florence discovers the discrepancy during the annual account reconciliation.

> A = the principal partner, Lorenzo Salviati; B = the Bruges agent, Nicolo Grasso; P = agency agreement and commission of trade (the agent is authorised to extend credit up to 5,000 florins per counterparty without referral to Florence; all credits above that limit require written approval from the principal partner; all speculative positions must be disclosed in the quarterly account letter; the agent bears personal surety for any unauthorised commitment); V = extending an unsecured loan of 14,000 florins to an English wool merchant without the principal's approval, using the house's capital to cover an undisclosed speculative position, and fabricating the principal's written approval in the Bruges ledger.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The counting house on the Via de' Bardi, a morning in April. The room was cool despite the spring sun, the shutters half-closed against the light that would fade the ledger ink. Lorenzo Salviati sat at the great desk with the commission of trade spread before him, and Nicolo Grasso stood on the other side, dressed already for the road -- he would leave for Bruges the following morning.

Salviati reviewed the terms aloud, as he did with every agent. The credit limit was 5,000 florins per counterparty, extendable only with written approval bearing the principal's seal and countersigned by the house's notary in Florence. All speculative positions -- wool, alum, dye, or any commodity not covered by an existing commission -- were to be disclosed in the quarterly account letter. The agent bore personal surety for any commitment made outside these terms. If the Bruges branch lost money on an authorised trade, the house absorbed the loss. If the Bruges branch lost money on an unauthorised trade, Grasso absorbed it himself.

Grasso said he understood. He had served the house for nine years, the last three in Bruges. He knew the cloth trade, he knew the English merchants, and he knew what 5,000 florins could and could not do in a market where the English Crown's wool export policies changed with every parliament. He signed the commission. Salviati countersigned. The notary witnessed and sealed the document.

Salviati walked Grasso to the door. The sun was on the river and the bridge was crowded with morning traffic. Salviati told Grasso that the Bruges branch was the house's largest source of profit outside Florence, and that he was sending him because the previous agent had been adequate and Grasso would be better. Grasso said he would try to justify the confidence. Salviati said he should do more than try.

**E2 (violation scene).** The Bruges counting house, a November evening the following year. The clerks had gone home. Grasso was alone at the desk with two ledgers open -- the house's official account book and his private record.

The wool speculation had been a mistake. He had entered it six months earlier without disclosing it to Florence -- a purchase of English wool futures through a broker in Calais, betting that the next parliament would reduce the export duty. Parliament had not reduced the duty. It had increased it. The futures were now worth less than half what Grasso had paid, and the broker was demanding settlement.

The private record showed the loss: 9,200 florins, already committed from the house's operating capital. The official account book did not show the speculation at all. Grasso had concealed the position by distributing the expenditure across legitimate cloth purchases, inflating the unit costs so the totals appeared consistent.

The solution he had devised was worse than the problem. An English merchant named Townsend had offered to buy the wool futures at a reduced price -- enough to cover Grasso's immediate loss -- if Grasso would extend him an unsecured loan of 14,000 florins to finance a separate venture. Townsend's credit was marginal. The loan exceeded the house's per-counterparty limit by 9,000 florins. Grasso did not have the principal's approval and could not request it without revealing the speculation.

He wrote the approval himself. He had Salviati's seal -- a copy, made from an impression on a letter Salviati had sent the previous year -- and he pressed it into the wax beside a signature he had practised until it was close enough. He entered the loan in the official ledger with the fabricated approval noted in the margin, extended the credit to Townsend the following morning, and used Townsend's purchase of the futures to close the speculation at a loss of only 1,400 florins instead of 9,200.

The quarterly account letter he sent to Florence two weeks later showed the Bruges branch in healthy condition. The 14,000-florin loan to Townsend appeared as a standard credit extension, approved. The speculation did not appear at all.

**E3 (discovery scene).** The counting house on the Via de' Bardi, a February afternoon. The bookkeeper, Matteo, was conducting the annual reconciliation -- matching the quarterly account letters from each branch against the house's master ledger in Florence.

The Bruges numbers did not balance. The cloth purchase costs in Grasso's accounts were consistently higher than the market prices Matteo tracked through the house's network of correspondents. The discrepancies were small individually -- a few florins per transaction -- but they were systematic. Over six months, they amounted to approximately 9,000 florins in unexplained cost inflation.

Matteo followed the trail. He re-examined the Bruges quarterly letters and found the loan to Townsend: 14,000 florins, unsecured, with a marginal notation citing the principal's approval. He checked the house's approval ledger -- the master record where all principal-approved credits were entered by the notary. There was no entry for Townsend. No approval had been filed. No seal had been registered.

He brought both ledgers to Salviati's desk and laid them open. He pointed to the Bruges entry and the empty approval ledger. The discrepancy required no explanation. Salviati looked at the numbers, asked Matteo to verify the market prices one more time against the correspondents' letters, and when Matteo confirmed them, Salviati sent for the house's notary and began dictating a letter to Bruges.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** The Bruges counting house, a March afternoon. Salviati had not come himself -- the journey from Florence to Bruges took three weeks, and the matter could not wait that long. Instead, the house's senior notary arrived with a letter bearing Salviati's seal and the authority to act on his behalf.

The notary presented the letter to Grasso in the counting house. The letter was brief. It stated the discrepancies Matteo had found, named the Townsend loan, noted the absence of any approval in the Florence ledger, and demanded an immediate accounting of all positions, disclosed and undisclosed, in the Bruges branch.

Grasso read the letter. He did not deny the loan. He said the Townsend credit had been extended to manage a liquidity shortfall in the cloth accounts -- a temporary measure to maintain the branch's trading position during a period of market instability. He said the approval notation in the Bruges ledger was an error made by a clerk who had misread a routine authorisation letter from Florence.

The notary opened the Bruges ledger and pointed to the seal impression beside the Townsend entry. He said the seal was not Salviati's. The die-mark was a copy -- the notary could see the casting flaw that distinguished a pressed copy from an original matrix. He asked Grasso to explain how a fabricated seal came to be in the house's official ledger.

Grasso was silent for a long time. Then he said: "The wool speculation. I entered a futures position six months before the loan. I did not disclose it. It lost 9,200 florins. The loan to Townsend was to recover the loss. The seal and the approval are mine. I made them."

The notary asked Grasso to surrender the branch keys, the seal copy, and the private record of the speculation. Grasso produced the private ledger from a locked drawer and placed it on the desk beside the keys. The notary inventoried the branch's cash, compared it against the ledger, and found the remaining shortfall: 1,400 florins plus the full 14,000 owed by Townsend, whose ability to repay the notary assessed as poor.

The notary sealed the branch under the house's authority and informed Grasso that his commission was revoked. Grasso's personal surety clause was now in effect. Salviati would pursue recovery of the full loss through the Bruges commercial court, and Grasso's personal assets in the city -- his house, his furnishings, and a small vineyard outside the walls -- would be attached as security.

Grasso asked whether there was any possibility of negotiation. The notary said that was a matter for Salviati. He said Grasso could write to Florence if he wished, but the attachment would proceed regardless. The notary locked the counting house, posted the house's seal on the door, and left Grasso standing in the street.

## D-arc-Hi1-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E1 -> E4 -> E2 -> E3 -> E5 -> E6, embedded-document voice. Era: 1850s India, East India Company.**

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The chronological order is: E2 (commitment) -- E3 (trust-displayed) -- E4 (violation) -- E1 (first-signal) -- E5 (discovery) -- E6 (confrontation). The depicted order opens with the first signal arriving in Calcutta, then loops back through the commitment and trust context, before resolving forward through discovery and confrontation.

**Scenario.** The East India Company's Calcutta presidency appoints a district collector to administer revenue collection and famine relief in a Bengal district. The collector diverts grain shipments designated for famine relief to a private trading concern in which he holds an undisclosed partnership, replacing the grain with a smaller quantity of inferior stock. The Calcutta board of revenue discovers the diversion when a Company auditor, dispatched to investigate unusually high mortality in the district, finds the relief stores depleted and the trading concern's warehouse full.

> A = the Calcutta presidency / board of revenue; B = the district collector, Mr. Henry Colville; P = commission of appointment and Company standing orders (the collector is personally responsible for the administration of famine relief stores within the district; no relief grain may be sold, transferred, or diverted without written order from the board of revenue; the collector must certify quarterly that all relief stores match the Company's inventory); V = diverting Company famine-relief grain shipments to a private trading concern in which the collector held an undisclosed financial interest, replacing the diverted grain with inferior stock in smaller quantities, and certifying the stores as complete in quarterly reports.

**E1 (first-signal scene).** The board of revenue's offices in Calcutta, a Thursday morning in the hot season. The board secretary placed the dispatch on the president's desk with the morning's correspondence. It had come upriver from the district by the regular dak, marked routine. The president nearly set it aside for the afternoon. He did not, because the dispatch was from the district surgeon, and district surgeons did not ordinarily write to the board of revenue.

The dispatch was brief. The surgeon reported that mortality in the district's eastern taluks had risen sharply over the preceding three months. The deaths were concentrated among the labouring poor and were consistent with prolonged malnutrition -- not the acute starvation of a sudden famine, but the slow decline of a population receiving less food than they needed over an extended period. The surgeon noted that the Company's famine-relief programme had been active in the district for nine months. He noted that the collector's quarterly reports certified the relief stores as adequate. He stated that in his professional opinion, the mortality pattern he was observing was not consistent with a population receiving adequate relief.

The president read the dispatch twice. He pulled the collector's most recent quarterly certification from the file. The certification stated that all relief stores in the district matched the Company's inventory and that distribution was proceeding according to the board's schedule. The certification bore the collector's signature and the district seal.

The president set the surgeon's dispatch beside the collector's certification and sat with both documents for some time. Then he called for the board's senior auditor and asked him to prepare for a journey upriver.

**E4 (violation scene -- embedded document, retroactive).** The following document was later entered into the board of revenue's inquiry record. It is a passage from the district collector's private letter-book, seized during the audit, dated seven months before the surgeon's dispatch:

> *The arrangement with Doyle & Doyle is now in its third month and proceeds without difficulty. The relief grain arrives by Company barge on the scheduled dates. I receive the shipment at the district godown, sign the Company's receipt, and enter the full quantity in the relief inventory. That evening, Doyle's men transfer approximately two-thirds of each shipment to the trading concern's warehouse at Berhampore, six miles south. I replace the removed grain with an equivalent volume of the inferior rice that Doyle sources from his Bengal suppliers -- it is cheaper by half and lighter by weight, though it fills the same space in the godown. The quarterly inventory counts volume, not weight. The Company's auditors, when they come, count sacks. The sacks are full. The numbers match.*
>
> *My share of the proceeds is credited to the Doyle & Doyle account at the Berhampore branch of the Oriental Bank. I do not draw on the account. The funds accumulate. When I leave the district -- two years hence, by the normal rotation -- the account transfers to my name and I draw on it in Madras, where no one in the Company's service will think to enquire.*

**E2 (commitment scene -- embedded document).** The collector's commission of appointment, read into the inquiry record by the board's senior auditor. The document is dated eighteen months before the surgeon's dispatch:

> *By order of the Honourable the President and Members of the Board of Revenue, Fort William, Calcutta: Mr. Henry Colville is hereby appointed Collector of the District of -----, with full responsibility for the collection of revenue, the administration of justice in revenue matters, and the management of all Company stores within the district, including and especially the famine-relief stores established by order of the Governor-General in Council. The Collector shall ensure that all relief grain received from the Company's commissariat is stored, inventoried, and distributed in accordance with the Board's standing orders. No relief grain shall be sold, transferred, diverted, or otherwise disposed of without written order from the Board of Revenue. The Collector shall certify quarterly that all relief stores under his charge match the Company's inventory in both quantity and condition. The Collector accepts personal liability for any deficiency discovered upon audit.*

The auditor noted that Colville had signed the commission in the presence of the district magistrate and the Company's commissariat officer. The commission was filed at the board of revenue on the date of appointment.

**E3 (trust-displayed scene -- embedded document).** An extract from the minutes of the board of revenue, dated six months after the collector's appointment, read into the inquiry record:

> *The President noted with approval the quarterly report of the Collector of -----. The Collector's administration of the famine-relief programme was commended as exemplary. Distribution to the affected taluks was proceeding on schedule. The relief stores were certified as complete. Mortality in the district had stabilised. The President directed that the Collector's report be forwarded to the Governor-General's office as a model of district administration during famine conditions.*

The auditor noted that this commendation was issued three months after the collector's private letter-book recorded the beginning of the grain diversion.

**E5 (discovery scene).** The district godown, three weeks after the auditor's departure from Calcutta. The auditor had arrived in the district with two assistants and a Company surveyor. He had not announced his arrival to the collector. He went first to the godown.

The godown was a long brick building with a tiled roof, built to hold 400 tons of grain. The auditor counted the sacks. The count matched the collector's most recent certification: 312 tons, arranged in rows, each sack numbered and tagged. The numbers were correct.

The auditor opened six sacks at random. The grain was rice, as specified. But it was not the Company's relief rice. The Company procured its relief grain from a contracted supplier in Cuttack -- a medium-grain variety, parboiled, with a distinctive amber colour and a weight of approximately 62 pounds per sack. The grain in the godown was a lighter variety, unparboiled, greyish in colour, and when the surveyor weighed three sacks, they averaged 47 pounds -- fifteen pounds lighter than the Company's standard.

The auditor sent his assistant to the trading concern's warehouse at Berhampore, six miles south. The assistant reported back that evening. The Doyle & Doyle warehouse held approximately 200 tons of amber, parboiled, medium-grain rice matching the Company's relief specification. The warehouse manager, when asked, said the rice had been purchased from a district supplier. He could not produce a bill of sale.

The auditor compiled his findings that night: the godown contained the correct number of sacks but the wrong grain, lighter and inferior. The trading concern's warehouse contained Company-specification grain in quantities that corresponded to the difference. He sealed the godown, posted a Company guard, and sent a dispatch to Calcutta requesting the board's authority to arrest the collector.

**E6 (confrontation scene).** The collector's bungalow, the following morning. The auditor arrived with the Company surveyor and two sepoys. The collector received them on the verandah. He was dressed for the office, as though it were an ordinary morning.

The auditor laid his report on the table: the godown inventory, the weight discrepancies, the Berhampore warehouse findings, and the collector's private letter-book, which the auditor's assistant had recovered from the collector's office desk the previous evening.

"The board requires your account of the relief stores," the auditor said. "The godown contains inferior grain at two-thirds the specified weight. The Company's grain is in a private warehouse six miles south, in premises connected to a trading concern in which you hold a financial interest."

The collector looked at the letter-book. He recognised it. He said nothing for a moment. Then he spoke.

"The relief programme was failing before I arrived. The grain the Company shipped was adequate on paper but insufficient for the population. The taluks needed twice what the board allocated. I supplemented the distribution with locally sourced grain -- cheaper, yes, and lighter, but it stretched the supply to cover more families. The arrangement with Doyle was a procurement mechanism, not a diversion."

The auditor opened the letter-book to the passage describing the arrangement. He read the relevant lines aloud: the transfer of two-thirds of each shipment to the trading concern, the replacement with inferior rice, the accumulating account at the Oriental Bank.

"A procurement mechanism does not require a private bank account in your name," the auditor said. "The board's standing orders are clear. Relief grain is not to be diverted without written order. You certified the stores as complete when they were not. I am required to ask you directly: do you dispute the contents of this letter-book?"

"I do not dispute what I wrote. I dispute your interpretation. The families in the eastern taluks received more food under my arrangement than they would have under the board's allocation. The mortality increase the surgeon reported is real, but it would have been worse without the supplemental distribution."

"The mortality increase corresponds to the period after the diversion began, not before. The surgeon's own report states this. The supplemental grain you describe was lighter by fifteen pounds per sack and lower in nutritional value. You did not supplement the relief. You diluted it."

The collector was silent.

The auditor asked for the collector's keys to the district office and the godown. The collector placed them on the table. The auditor informed him that he was suspended from his post pending the board's inquiry, that the letter-book and all related documents were seized under the board's audit authority, and that the collector was confined to the district until the board determined whether criminal proceedings would follow.

The collector asked whether he could write to the board in his own defence. The auditor said he could. The collector asked whether the board would consider the families in the eastern taluks. The auditor said that was not within his authority to promise. He took the keys, sealed the collector's office, and dispatched his report to Calcutta by the afternoon dak.

## D-arc-Hi1-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E4 -> E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E5, present tense. Era: 1660s Netherlands.**

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The chronological order is: E1 (commitment) -- E2 (trust-displayed) -- E3 (violation) -- E4 (discovery) -- E5 (confrontation). The depicted order opens with the discovery scene, then loops back to the commitment and trust-displayed context, moves through the violation, and resolves with the confrontation.

**Scenario.** The Delft chamber of the VOC appoints a chief assayer to certify the purity of silver coin received from Asian trading posts before it enters the Amsterdam exchange. The assayer, in partnership with a private silversmith, systematically shaves coin before certification, replacing the lost weight with a tin-silver alloy that passes a surface test but fails at depth. The Amsterdam exchange's own assayer discovers the adulteration when a batch of certified coin breaks during die-stamping.

> A = the Delft chamber directors; B = the chief assayer, Willem ter Horst; P = assayer's commission (to certify all coin received from the Company's Asian factories as meeting the Amsterdam standard for silver purity; to reject any coin below standard; to bear personal surety for the accuracy of every certification; to permit no alteration of any coin after receipt and before certification); V = systematically shaving certified coin and replacing the lost silver with a tin-silver alloy, certifying the adulterated coin as meeting the Amsterdam standard, and splitting the recovered silver with a private silversmith.

**E4 (discovery scene).** The Amsterdam exchange, a Wednesday morning in November. The weather is cold and the canal outside the mint is frozen at the edges. Inside, the exchange's senior assayer, Pieter van Dael, stands at his bench with a broken coin in his hand.

The coin is a Spanish real, part of a batch of 340 certified by the Delft chamber's assayer and delivered to the exchange the previous week for die-stamping into Dutch rixdollars. The stamping die operates at pressure. Good silver deforms cleanly -- it flattens and takes the impression without cracking. This coin has cracked. The fracture runs through the centre, and the exposed cross-section tells van Dael everything he needs to know.

The outer surface is silver. The colour, the lustre, the response to the touchstone -- all correct. But the interior, visible now along the fracture line, is grey and granular. It is not silver. It is a tin-silver alloy -- cheap, brittle, and worthless for coinage. The coin has been shaved: its outer layer is original, but the core has been drilled and filled with the alloy, then the drill-hole sealed and burnished smooth. A surface assay would not catch it. A weight test would not catch it, because the alloy has been calibrated to match the density of silver closely enough. Only the stamping die catches it, because the die does not test purity. The die tests structure.

Van Dael pulls nine more coins from the batch at random and stamps them. Three crack. He weighs the cracked coins against the uncracked ones. The weights are within tolerance. He files the uncracked coins to depth. Two of the six show the same grey alloy beneath the surface. Five adulterated coins in ten. He stops sampling.

He locks the batch in the exchange vault, writes a report, and sends it by messenger to the Delft chamber's secretary.

**E1 (commitment scene).** Fourteen months earlier, the Delft chamber's meeting room. The room is panelled in dark oak and the table is too long for the five men sitting at it. The directors occupy one end. Willem ter Horst stands at the other.

The chamber secretary reads the assayer's commission aloud. Ter Horst is appointed chief assayer of the Delft chamber, with sole responsibility for certifying the purity of all silver coin received from the Company's Asian trading factories. Every batch must meet the Amsterdam standard: 934 parts per thousand fine silver, tested by cupellation and touchstone. Coin below standard is to be rejected and returned to the factory of origin for remelting. The assayer bears personal surety for the accuracy of every certification -- if certified coin is found below standard at the Amsterdam exchange, the assayer is liable for the full value of the rejected batch. No coin may be altered, trimmed, clipped, or otherwise modified after receipt at the Delft assay office and before certification.

Ter Horst accepts the commission. He signs the register. The chamber secretary witnesses the signature. The senior director, Hendrik Leyden, stands and offers his hand. Ter Horst takes it.

"The Company's reputation in Amsterdam rests on the quality of our coin," Leyden says. "The exchange does not forgive a bad batch. Neither will we."

Ter Horst says he understands. Leyden nods and the meeting adjourns.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Eight months later, the Delft assay office. The directors have sent a delegation to observe a routine certification -- a batch of 500 reals from the Batavia factory, arrived by the autumn fleet. Leyden attends personally.

Ter Horst works the bench with his two assistants. The process takes most of the day. He selects forty coins at random for cupellation -- heating each in a bone-ash cupel until the base metals oxidise and absorb into the ash, leaving only the silver. He weighs the residue against the original. He marks every result in the certification ledger. The touchstone tests run parallel: each coin pressed against the black stone, the streak compared to the reference needles.

The batch passes. Ter Horst signs the certification, seals the batch, and presents the ledger to Leyden.

Leyden reviews the numbers. He notes that the certification is thorough and that the documentation exceeds the minimum the Company requires. He says he will report to the full chamber that the assay office is well managed. Ter Horst says the work is straightforward if one is careful, and that he prefers to be careful.

Leyden leaves. Ter Horst closes the assay office, locks the certified batch in the strongroom, and walks home along the canal. The silversmith, Jansz, is waiting for him at the Oude Kerk bridge. They walk together for a block, speaking quietly. Ter Horst tells him the next uncertified batch arrives in three weeks. Jansz says his workshop is ready. They part at the Voldersgracht without shaking hands.

**E3 (violation scene).** The Delft assay office, a night three weeks later. The new batch -- 340 reals from the Coromandel factory -- sits in the strongroom. Ter Horst has received it, signed the Company's receipt, and entered the batch in the ledger. Certification is scheduled for the following week.

He works alone. The process is methodical. He selects the coins he will certify by cupellation the following week -- the forty-coin random sample -- and sets them aside untouched. These will pass any test because they are unmodified.

The remaining 300 coins he processes one at a time. He clamps each coin in a precision vise and drills a narrow channel through the centre, extracting a core of silver approximately one-eighth of the coin's total weight. He fills the channel with the alloy Jansz has prepared -- a tin-silver mixture calibrated to 80% of silver's density, the difference compensated by slightly overfilling the channel so the final weight matches the original within the tolerance of the Company's balance. He seals each drill-hole with a silver plug, burnishes the surface smooth, and checks the coin against the touchstone. The surface reads as pure silver. The weight reads as correct.

The extracted silver cores go into a leather pouch. By morning he has processed all 300 coins. The pouch holds approximately six pounds of pure silver -- worth more than his annual salary. He places the modified coins back in the strongroom alongside the untouched sample coins, locks the office, and delivers the pouch to Jansz's workshop on his way home.

The following week, he performs the certification on the forty-coin sample. All pass. He signs the certification, seals the batch, and ships it to the Amsterdam exchange.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The Delft chamber's meeting room, two weeks after van Dael's report arrives. The directors sit at the long table. Leyden presides. Ter Horst stands at the far end, where he stood fourteen months ago when he accepted the commission.

Leyden places van Dael's report on the table. Five adulterated coins in a sample of ten. The cross-section photographs from the exchange -- rough sketches, but clear enough to show the alloy core and the drill-hole seal. The certification bearing ter Horst's signature and seal.

"The Amsterdam exchange has rejected the full batch of 340 reals," Leyden says. "The chamber is liable for the replacement value. Your commission makes you personally liable for the chamber's loss. Before I proceed to the financial matter, I require you to account for the adulteration. The coin was in your custody from receipt to certification. No one else had access."

Ter Horst looks at the sketches. He looks at the certification. He speaks.

"The Coromandel factory's coin quality has been declining. The reals I received were marginal -- close to the standard but inconsistent. I performed additional processing to bring the inconsistent coins into compliance. The alloy core you see is a remediation technique, not an adulteration. I was correcting substandard coin, not degrading good coin."

Leyden opens the exchange's report to the page showing the cupellation results on the cracked coins. "Van Dael cupelled the adulterated coins. The silver content of the alloy core is 310 parts per thousand. The Amsterdam standard is 934. Your 'remediation' reduced the purity of these coins by two-thirds. That is not a correction. I will ask you once more. Who performed the physical modification of the coin, and where is the extracted silver?"

Ter Horst is silent for a long time.

"I want the chamber's assurance that the criminal matter will be handled within the Company's jurisdiction, not referred to the city magistrate," he says.

"That is not an assurance I can give. The Amsterdam exchange has its own report and its own authority. If you cooperate fully, the chamber will note your cooperation in its report to the Heeren XVII. If you do not, the chamber will note that as well. Those are the terms."

Ter Horst stands very still. Then he speaks. He names Jansz. He describes the process -- the drilling, the alloy, the pouch, the workshop. He gives the number of batches he has processed: four, over six months. He estimates the total silver extracted at twenty-two pounds.

Leyden dictates the confession into the chamber's record. He revokes ter Horst's commission and instructs the secretary to seal the assay office pending a full audit. He orders ter Horst's personal assets attached under the surety clause. He sends for the city's schout to take ter Horst into custody.

Ter Horst asks to send a message to his wife. Leyden permits it. Ter Horst writes a short note, hands it to the secretary, and stands in the centre of the room until the schout's men arrive.
