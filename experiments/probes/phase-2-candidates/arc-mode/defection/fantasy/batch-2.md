## D-arc-Fa2-1

**5 beats, chronological. Dialogue-driven.**

**Scenario.** The guild-master of a weavers' guild in a trade city appoints her senior journeyman as the guild's exclusive representative to the Dyers' Compact, binding him by guild oath to negotiate only terms that have been pre-approved by the guild council. Over four months, he negotiates a private side-agreement with the dyers' master that gives the dyers access to the weavers' proprietary loom patterns in exchange for a personal dyeing monopoly for his own workshop. The guild-master discovers the side-agreement when a dyer's apprentice delivers a bolt of cloth dyed in patterns only the weavers' council should possess.

> A = guild-master (Maren Dalloway); B = senior journeyman (Corin Ashward); P = guild oath of representation (sworn before the guild council: to negotiate with the Dyers' Compact only on terms pre-approved by the guild council, to disclose all offers and counter-offers to the council before acceptance, and to enter no private agreements with any member of the Compact in his own name or interest); V = negotiating a private side-agreement with the dyers' master granting access to the weavers' proprietary loom patterns in exchange for an exclusive personal dyeing contract for his own workshop, without disclosure to the guild council.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The guild hall, the first day of the spring quarter. The council sat along the long table and the guild-master stood at its head. Corin Ashward knelt on the stone floor before the council with the oath-book open in front of him.

"Speak it as I read it," the guild-master said.

The clerk read the oath of representation: that Corin Ashward, senior journeyman, would negotiate with the Dyers' Compact only on terms approved by the guild council; that he would disclose all offers, counter-offers, and proposals from any member of the Compact to the council before acceptance or rejection; that he would enter no private agreements with any dyer, dye-house, or member of the Compact in his own name or for his own interest; and that he accepted these terms on penalty of expulsion from the guild and forfeiture of his workshop charter.

"Do you accept?" the guild-master asked.

"I accept," Ashward said.

He pressed his signet into the wax beside the oath. The guild-master countersigned. The clerk entered the appointment in the rolls and closed the book.

"You leave for the Compact in three days," the guild-master said. "The council's approved terms are in your dispatch case. Stay within them."

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Six weeks later, the guild hall. Ashward had returned from the first round of negotiations and was reporting to the council. He had secured a twelve-month pricing agreement on indigo and madder at rates two percent below the previous year. He had rejected the dyers' counter-proposal on minimum volume commitments and brought it back to the council as required.

"Two percent below last year," the guild-master said. "How?"

"I told them we'd found a supplier in Gravenfeld," Ashward said. "We haven't. But they don't know that."

The council laughed. The guild-master did not, but she allowed herself a small nod. The clerk entered the terms into the record. The council voted to approve the pricing agreement and to reject the volume counter-proposal. Ashward was sent back to the Compact with revised instructions.

After the session, the guild-master walked with Ashward to the courtyard. "You're better at this than I expected," she said.

"I've been watching you do it for six years," he said.

"That's not the same as doing it."

"No," he said. "It's not."

**E3 (violation scene).** Two months later, the dyers' master's house in the Compact quarter. Evening. Ashward sat across the table from Master Dunmore, the dyers' elected head, in a private dining room. The door was closed. No clerks were present.

Dunmore set a document on the table between them. It was a side-agreement -- handwritten, unsigned, two copies. Ashward read it. The terms were simple: Ashward would provide the dyers with access to the weavers' proprietary loom patterns -- the twelve designs the guild council had classified as trade-controlled and never shared outside the guild. In exchange, Dunmore would grant Ashward's personal workshop an exclusive contract to supply all undyed cloth for the Compact's premium line. The contract was worth three times what Ashward's workshop currently earned in a year.

"The patterns are controlled," Ashward said.

"I know what they are," Dunmore said. "That's why I'm offering what I'm offering."

Ashward looked at the document again. He knew every pattern by heart -- he had woven on them since he was an apprentice. He also knew that the guild-master would never approve the release. The patterns were the guild's primary advantage over every weaving house in the region.

He signed both copies. Dunmore countersigned. They each kept one. Ashward left through the kitchen entrance.

**E4 (discovery scene).** Three weeks later, the guild-master's workshop. It was mid-morning. The guild-master was inspecting a shipment of finished cloth that had arrived from the dye-houses -- a routine delivery, part of the standing contract with the Compact.

A dyer's apprentice carried in the last bolt and set it on the table. The guild-master unrolled it and stopped. The cloth was dyed in the Thornwall pattern -- one of the twelve controlled designs. The pattern was unmistakable: the interlocking thorn motif, the specific repeat interval, the way the border wove into the selvedge. No dyer could reproduce it without the loom draft, because the pattern was created in the weaving, not the dyeing. But this bolt had been dyed to accentuate the pattern's structure -- the colour placement followed the design's internal logic in a way that was only possible if the dyer had the loom draft in front of him while mixing the dye bath.

The guild-master asked the apprentice who had ordered this bolt. The apprentice checked his delivery log. The order had come from Master Dunmore's personal dye-house, and the cloth had been supplied by Ashward's workshop.

The guild-master rolled the bolt closed and placed it in her office. She sent for the guild clerk and asked him to pull Ashward's travel records from the Compact negotiations -- specifically, any meetings not recorded in the official negotiation minutes. The clerk returned an hour later. Ashward had logged four official meetings with the Compact. The Compact's own records, which the guild kept copies of under the treaty, showed seven meetings in the same period. Three meetings were unaccounted for.

She sent a message to Ashward summoning him to the guild hall the following morning.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The guild hall, the next morning. Ashward arrived to find the guild-master seated at the council table with the clerk, the bolt of Thornwall cloth unrolled before them, and the meeting discrepancy laid out in the clerk's hand.

"Show me the side-agreement," the guild-master said.

Ashward looked at the cloth, then at the discrepancy records. He said nothing for a moment.

"I don't have it with me," he said.

"Then describe its terms."

"Maren --"

"Describe its terms. Now."

Ashward described the agreement: the twelve loom patterns in exchange for the exclusive dyeing contract. He said the patterns would have been shared eventually -- the council had discussed opening them to the Compact in three of the last four annual reviews. He said he had accelerated a decision the council was already moving toward, and that the exclusive contract would bring more revenue to his workshop than the patterns were worth as trade secrets.

"The council discussed it and declined each time," the guild-master said. "You took that decision out of the council's hands and made it in your own interest. The oath you swore forbids private agreements. The penalty is expulsion and forfeiture."

Ashward said he would challenge the expulsion before the trade court. He said the patterns' controlled status was a council policy, not a municipal ordinance, and that the trade court had overturned guild penalties for policy violations twice in the last decade. He said if the guild-master expelled him, the court challenge would make the patterns' existence public -- something the guild had spent twenty years preventing.

The guild-master was quiet. Then she said: "The council meets this afternoon. You will attend. You will present the side-agreement and your argument. The council will vote on expulsion. If the vote carries, you may take it to the trade court. But the patterns are already compromised, and that is your doing, not mine."

She folded the cloth. Ashward left the hall. The clerk entered the meeting time in the roll and began drafting the summons for the council members.

## D-arc-Fa2-2

**4 beats, chronological. Free indirect style.**

**Scenario.** A temple's high seer entrusts the keeping of the sacred flame to a fire-warden, who swears an oath that the flame must never be allowed to die and that no flame from the sacred fire may be carried beyond the temple walls. The fire-warden permits a foreign emissary to light a torch from the sacred flame and carry it out of the temple, in exchange for the emissary's promise to sponsor the fire-warden's daughter as a novice at a prestigious foreign academy. The temple's night-keeper sees the emissary leaving with the lit torch.

> A = the high seer (Aderyn); B = the fire-warden (Caedmon); P = the fire-warden's oath (sworn before the temple assembly: to maintain the sacred flame without interruption and to permit no flame kindled from the sacred fire to be carried, conveyed, or transferred beyond the temple walls by any means or person, on penalty of permanent exile from the temple order); V = permitting the foreign emissary Varek to kindle a torch from the sacred flame and carry it out of the temple, in exchange for Varek's promise to sponsor Caedmon's daughter as a novice at the Academy of Selenne.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The inner sanctum was lit only by the flame itself, and Caedmon knelt before it with his hands open and his palms turned toward the heat. The high seer stood behind him, and behind her the assembly filled the curved benches -- priests, wardens, keepers, and novices, perhaps eighty in all.

Aderyn spoke the oath and Caedmon repeated it. He would maintain the flame without interruption. He would permit no flame kindled from the sacred fire to be carried, conveyed, or transferred beyond the temple walls by any means or by any person. He accepted this obligation on penalty of permanent exile from the order. His voice was steady. The assembly witnessed.

The high seer placed the warden's chain around his neck -- iron links, warm from the room -- and the keeper of records entered the appointment in the temple's chronicle. Caedmon rose and took his place beside the flame. The assembly filed out. Aderyn was the last to leave. She paused at the door and looked back at the new fire-warden standing in the light of the flame he had just sworn to protect. He looked, she thought, like a man who understood the weight of what he had accepted. She had thought the same of his predecessor, and his predecessor had served well for thirty years. She closed the door behind her.

**E2 (violation scene).** Nine months later, a spring evening. The temple was closed to visitors, but the foreign emissary Varek had arrived that afternoon with a letter of diplomatic passage from the city's governor, and the high seer had granted him permission to view the flame -- a courtesy extended to dignitaries, bounded by the temple's rules.

Caedmon escorted Varek to the sanctum after the evening prayers. The two of them stood before the flame. The emissary looked at it for a long time. Then he spoke quietly. He said his court had heard of the sacred fire for generations. He said his queen wished to establish a sister-flame at her own temple in Selenne -- a gesture of spiritual alliance between the two cities. He asked whether a flame might be carried from the sacred fire to his ship, where it would be kept in a consecrated lantern for the voyage.

Caedmon told him that the oath forbade it. No flame from the sacred fire could leave the temple walls. The emissary nodded. He said he understood. He paused. Then he said he had noticed, in the temple's records of visitors, that Caedmon had a daughter -- twelve years old, approaching the age of admission to the academies. He said the Academy of Selenne was the finest school of letters and natural philosophy in the northern kingdoms. He said he had the authority to sponsor one novice per year, and that the sponsorship had not yet been allocated.

Caedmon was quiet for a long time. The fire-warden knew what his daughter wanted. She had asked him about the foreign academies twice in the last year, and he had told her they were beyond the family's means, which was true. The Academy of Selenne was not merely beyond his means -- it was beyond the means of anyone in the temple order. The sponsorship was worth more than he would earn in a decade.

The emissary did not press. He waited. Caedmon reached for the iron-handled torch mounted beside the sanctum door, held it to the sacred flame until it caught, and handed it to Varek. The emissary wrapped the torch in a cloth sheath, inclined his head, and left the sanctum. Caedmon stood alone in the room and watched the sacred flame burn as it had always burned, diminished by nothing visible.

**E3 (discovery scene).** The same evening, an hour later. Ilwyn, the temple's night-keeper, had begun her rounds at the usual time -- checking the locks, the oil stores, the ventilation shafts, the mundane infrastructure that kept the building standing while the priests concerned themselves with the sacred. She had reached the east corridor when she saw the emissary walking toward the outer gate. He was moving quickly, which was unusual for a dignitary, and he was carrying something wrapped in cloth at his side. The cloth was glowing at one end. A faint trail of smoke curled above it.

Ilwyn stopped. She knew what a burning torch looked like, even wrapped. She also knew that the only fire in the temple tonight was the sacred flame -- the hearth fires in the kitchens and dormitories had been banked hours ago. There was no other source.

She followed the emissary to the outer gate. She watched him pass through it. She watched the cloth-wrapped light move down the temple steps and across the courtyard toward the harbour road. She stood at the gate until the light disappeared.

Then she went to the temple chronicle room, wrote what she had seen -- the time, the corridor, the emissary's bearing, the lit bundle, the direction of departure -- and placed the entry in the night-keeper's log. She debated with herself for several minutes. Then she walked to the high seer's quarters and knocked.

Aderyn answered in her sleeping robe. Ilwyn told her what she had seen: the emissary, the wrapped torch, the glow, the departure through the outer gate. Aderyn listened without interrupting. She asked Ilwyn whether anyone else had been in the east corridor. Ilwyn said no. Aderyn asked whether the sacred flame was still burning. Ilwyn said she had not checked. Aderyn told her to check now and to report back. Then Aderyn dressed, lit a hand-lamp from the candle on her desk, and walked to the sanctum.

The sacred flame was burning. Beside it, in the fire-warden's chair, Caedmon sat with his hands on his knees, staring at the fire. He did not look up when the high seer entered.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** The sanctum, moments later. Aderyn closed the door behind her. The two of them were alone with the flame.

"The night-keeper saw a man leave this temple carrying fire," Aderyn said. "The only fire in this building tonight is the one behind you. Tell me what happened."

Caedmon told her. The emissary's request. The sister-flame for Selenne. The sponsorship for his daughter. The torch. He did not leave anything out. He spoke as though he had been rehearsing the words in his head since the moment Varek left the room.

"Call the harbour-master," Aderyn said. "Have the emissary's ship held at the dock. The torch must be returned and extinguished in this room before dawn."

Caedmon said the emissary had diplomatic passage from the governor. The harbour-master could not hold a diplomatic vessel without the governor's order, and the governor's office was closed until morning. The ship was scheduled to sail on the midnight tide.

"Then go to the harbour yourself," Aderyn said. "Take two wardens. Tell the emissary that the fire must be returned or the temple will petition the governor to revoke his passage and declare the taking a violation of the city's sacred-site protections. Those protections carry criminal penalty. The governor will act."

Caedmon said that if he confronted the emissary at the harbour, the reason for the confrontation would become public. The dock watch would see it. The harbour-master's log would record it. The city would know that a flame had been taken from the sacred fire under the fire-warden's watch. The temple's reputation --

"The temple's reputation is not yours to protect," Aderyn said. "You are the one who handed him the torch. Go to the harbour. Bring the flame back. What happens to you afterward will be decided by the assembly, not by me and not tonight."

Caedmon stood. He took the warden's chain from around his neck and held it out to her.

"Keep it on," she said. "You are still the fire-warden until the assembly says otherwise. Go."

He went. Aderyn sat down in the chair he had vacated and watched the sacred flame. She stayed there until she heard footsteps in the east corridor, and then she rose to meet whatever was coming back through the door.

## D-arc-Fa2-3

**6 beats, chronological. Present tense, split discovery.**

**Scenario.** A mountain clan's war-chief entrusts the clan's only healer with custody of the clan's seed-vault -- the stored grain reserves that ensure survival through the winter siege season. The healer swears a keeper's bond to dispense grain only by the war-chief's written order. Over three months, the healer secretly trades portions of the seed-vault to a rival clan's merchants in exchange for medicinal herbs the clan cannot grow at altitude, believing the grain surplus is large enough to absorb the loss. A herd-boy sees the healer loading grain onto a rival merchant's pack mule at the lower pass.

> A = war-chief (Bryndis); B = the healer (Torsten); P = the keeper's bond (sworn before the clan council: to dispense grain from the seed-vault only on presentation of the war-chief's written order bearing her seal; no grain to leave the vault for any purpose, trade, gift, or charity, without such order; keeper bears personal liability for any shortfall discovered at the winter count); V = trading seed-vault grain to a rival clan's merchants on three occasions over three months in exchange for medicinal herbs, without the war-chief's written order or knowledge.

**E1 (commitment scene).** The council fire burns low in the long hall. Bryndis sits on the war-chief's stone with the council arrayed on the benches to either side. Torsten stands before her with the keeper's rod in his hands -- a length of mountain oak, notched along its edge with the count of every vault he will be responsible for.

The oath-speaker reads the keeper's bond. Torsten repeats it: he will dispense grain from the seed-vault only on presentation of the war-chief's written order bearing her seal. No grain will leave the vault for any purpose -- trade, gift, or charity -- without such order. He accepts personal liability for any shortfall discovered at the winter count.

Bryndis asks him if he understands the penalty for shortfall. He says he does. She presses her seal into the wax at the foot of the bond-scroll. Torsten marks his sign beside it. The oath-speaker rolls the scroll and places it in the council's archive chest.

Torsten takes the vault key from the previous keeper's hand and hangs it on the chain at his belt. The council disperses. Bryndis remains by the fire. She watches Torsten cross the yard toward the vault house, the key swinging at his hip, and thinks that the grain is safer with a healer than it was with a soldier. A healer counts things carefully.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Midsummer. Bryndis comes to the vault house with her steward for the mid-season count. Torsten opens the vault and walks them through the inventory: winter wheat, barley, rye, dried oat, and the emergency millet stored in the deep bins. Every sack is labelled and dated. The count matches the ledger to the last measure.

Bryndis notes that the vault is better organised than it has been in years. The previous keeper had stored grain by arrival date; Torsten has reorganised by type and expected consumption rate, with the fastest-spoiling stocks nearest the door.

"You've done well," she says.

"The grain keeps itself," Torsten says. "I just make sure nothing eats it."

The steward records the count in the clan's ledger. Bryndis seals the entry. They leave the vault, and Torsten locks it behind them.

**E3 (violation scene).** Late summer, the lower pass below the clan's territory. Dawn. Torsten waits at the trail junction with two sacks of barley loaded on his own mule. He has carried them down from the vault in the dark, an hour's walk on a path he knows better than anyone in the clan -- the healer's path, used for gathering herbs on the lower slopes.

A merchant from the Arnfeld clan arrives with a pack mule of his own. He leads it to where Torsten stands and unloads three bundles of dried valerian, boneset, and willowbark -- herbs that do not grow above the snow line, that the clan's healers have always needed and never been able to cultivate. The merchant weighs the barley, marks the transaction in his own book, and loads the sacks onto his mule. Torsten loads the herb bundles onto his. Neither of them speaks more than is necessary.

This is the third exchange. The first was six weeks ago -- a single sack of rye for a bundle of feverfew. The second was three weeks later -- two sacks of wheat for willowbark and dried comfrey. The quantities have grown each time. Torsten has calculated the margin: the vault holds grain for a full winter plus a surplus of roughly twelve percent. He has traded away four percent. The winter count will show a shortfall, but he plans to explain it as spoilage -- a credible loss for grain stored in mountain conditions.

He leads his mule up the healer's path as the sun clears the eastern ridge. The merchant rides south toward Arnfeld territory. The pass is empty by the time the first herd-boys bring the goats down to the lower pastures.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** Two weeks later. Bryndis is in the long hall hearing reports from the border-riders. One of them mentions, in the course of a routine update, that the Arnfeld merchants have been using the lower pass more frequently than usual -- three crossings in the last two months, each time with loaded mules going south.

Bryndis asks what they were carrying. The rider says he could not tell from the distance; the mules were loaded and covered. She asks whether anyone from the clan was seen at the pass. The rider says he was not watching the pass itself -- he was on the ridge trail above it and could only see the southern approach.

Bryndis nods and moves to the next report. She does not mention the observation to anyone, but that evening she asks the steward to prepare an unscheduled vault count for the following week.

**E5 (discovery scene).** The vault house, the following week. Bryndis arrives at dawn with the steward. Torsten opens the vault. They begin the count.

The wheat is short by two sacks. The barley is short by two sacks. The rye is short by one. The steward counts twice, then a third time. The totals do not change. Torsten says the shortfall is likely spoilage -- mountain damp, he says, which can degrade grain in the deep bins. He offers to show them the spoilage logs he has kept.

Bryndis looks at the spoilage logs. They are detailed and credible. But she notices something: the recorded spoilage dates correspond closely to the three dates the border-rider reported Arnfeld merchants crossing the lower pass with loaded mules. She sets the logs beside the steward's ledger and compares the two.

"Steward," she says. "Walk to the lower pass. Take one of the herd-boys who works the south slopes. Ask him whether he has seen anyone from this clan at the pass in the last two months."

The steward leaves. He returns three hours later with a herd-boy named Aldric. Bryndis asks the boy to say what he saw. Aldric says he saw Torsten at the trail junction at dawn, twelve days ago, loading two sacks of grain onto a merchant's mule. He says the merchant had Arnfeld markings on his saddle. He says Torsten gave the merchant the sacks and the merchant gave Torsten bundles wrapped in cloth. He says he watched from behind the goat-pen wall because he did not want the healer to see him out of bed before his watch.

Bryndis looks at Torsten. Torsten says nothing. She tells the steward to seal the vault and post a guard. She tells Torsten to come to the long hall at midday.

**E6 (confrontation scene).** The long hall, midday. The council fire is lit. Bryndis sits on the war-chief's stone. Torsten stands before her. The steward is present with the ledger. Aldric has been sent away.

"Name every transaction," Bryndis says. "Dates, quantities, what you received."

Torsten names them. Three trades. Five sacks total. Valerian, boneset, willowbark, feverfew, comfrey. He says the clan's herb stores were critically low after the spring fever -- six members of the clan nearly died because he did not have enough willowbark to bring the fevers down. He says he sent a written request to Bryndis for permission to trade grain for herbs four months ago. He says the request was never answered.

Bryndis turns to the steward. The steward checks the correspondence log. The request exists. It was received, logged, and placed in the queue for the next council session. The council session was delayed twice -- once by a border dispute, once by the midsummer count preparations. The request was never heard.

"The request was never answered," Bryndis says. "That does not give you authority to act without my order. The keeper's bond requires my written order bearing my seal. You had neither."

Torsten says six people would have died in the next fever season without the herbs he purchased. He says the grain surplus was sufficient to absorb the loss. He says the winter count will show the clan can survive on what remains, with a margin reduced but not eliminated.

"The margin is not yours to reduce," Bryndis says. "If the winter is long, those five sacks are the difference between rationing and starvation. You traded survival grain for medicine against my authority."

She asks the council whether the keeper's bond was violated. The four seated council members confirm it was. She asks Torsten to surrender the vault key. He removes it from his belt chain and sets it on the stone before her.

"The herbs stay," she says. "We need them. But the council will appoint a new keeper by sundown, and you will answer for the shortfall at the winter count. If the count shows the clan can survive, the penalty is loss of your position. If it shows we cannot, the penalty is exile."

Torsten picks up his healer's bag from beside the bench. He does not look at the council as he leaves the hall.

## D-arc-Fa2-4

**5 beats, non-linear: E1 → E4 → E2 → E3 → E5. Depicted order: discovery opens, evidence-gathering second, commitment (flashback), violation (flashback), confrontation. Embedded-document voice.**

**Scenario.** A river-kingdom's harbour-master is appointed by the merchant prince and sworn to levy tariffs according to the published rate-scroll and remit all collected duties to the treasury without deduction. Over a year, the harbour-master levies a private surcharge on foreign vessels and diverts the surplus to fund repairs on the river wall, which the treasury has refused to fund despite three petitions. The merchant prince's factor discovers the surcharge when a foreign captain files a formal complaint citing rates that do not match the published scroll.

> A = the merchant prince (Lord Aldren); B = the harbour-master (Estrid Vael); P = the harbour-master's oath of office (sworn before the merchant prince's court: to levy tariffs only as published in the rate-scroll approved by the treasury, to remit all collected duties to the treasury in full without deduction or diversion, and to enter no supplementary fees, surcharges, or assessments without written authorisation from the merchant prince); V = levying a private surcharge of eight percent on all foreign vessels for twelve months and diverting the collected surplus -- approximately 1,400 crowns -- to fund river-wall repairs, without written authorisation from the merchant prince or approval from the treasury.

**E1 (discovery scene).** The factor's office at the treasury, a morning in late autumn. Aldren's factor, a man named Gerren, opens the day's correspondence and finds a sealed complaint from a Vaelish trade captain named Doss. He breaks the seal and reads:

> *To the treasury of Lord Aldren, merchant prince of the river-kingdom -- I, Captain Nils Doss of the trading vessel Selvik, do formally protest the tariff levied upon my vessel and cargo at the harbour of Thornmouth on the fourteenth day of the harvest month. I was assessed a total duty of thirty-one crowns and four pennies. The published rate-scroll, which I obtained from the harbour office upon arrival, specifies a duty of twenty-nine crowns even for a vessel of my class and cargo. The harbour-master, Estrid Vael, informed me that the additional two crowns and four pennies represent a harbour-maintenance surcharge applicable to all foreign vessels. I can find no such surcharge in the published scroll. I have paid under protest and request a formal review.*

Gerren pulls the current rate-scroll from the treasury's file. He checks the tariff for a foreign trading vessel of Doss's class and cargo. Twenty-nine crowns. No surcharge. No harbour-maintenance assessment. He reads the complaint again. Then he opens the harbour's remittance ledger for the last twelve months and begins comparing the amounts received against the amounts that should have been collected under the published rates.

The numbers do not match. Every month, the harbour's remittance to the treasury matches the published-rate total exactly -- but Gerren now suspects the actual collections are higher. The surplus is not appearing in the treasury's books. It is going somewhere else.

He writes a note to the merchant prince requesting a meeting and attaches the captain's complaint.

**E2 (evidence-gathering scene).** That afternoon, the harbour office at Thornmouth. Gerren arrives with a clerk and a letter of authority from the merchant prince. Estrid Vael is not present -- she is overseeing a cargo inspection at the far dock. Gerren does not wait for her. He opens the harbour's collection ledger -- the book in which every tariff payment is recorded before remittance to the treasury.

The ledger tells the story. Every entry for a foreign vessel shows two lines: the published tariff, and beneath it a second line labelled "maintenance levy," calculated at eight percent of the tariff amount. The maintenance levies are totalled separately at the bottom of each monthly page. They are not included in the remittance to the treasury. Instead, they are transferred to a second ledger, smaller and bound in different leather, which Gerren finds in the harbour-master's desk. This ledger is titled "River Wall Repair Fund." It contains twelve months of entries -- materials purchased, labourers paid, stone and timber acquired. The total expenditure is approximately 1,400 crowns. All sourced from the maintenance levy.

Gerren copies the relevant entries from both ledgers into his own book. He does not remove the ledgers from the office. He locks the office, rides back to the city, and presents his findings to the merchant prince that evening.

**E3 (commitment scene -- flashback, embedded document).** The following morning, in the merchant prince's hall. Lord Aldren sits at the high table with Gerren beside him and the harbour-master's oath of office spread on the table. He reads the operative clause into the record, as the court scribe notes it:

> *I, Estrid Vael, appointed harbour-master of Thornmouth by the authority of Lord Aldren, merchant prince, do swear before this court that I shall levy tariffs upon all vessels entering or departing the harbour only as published in the rate-scroll approved by the treasury; that I shall remit all collected duties to the treasury in full, without deduction, diversion, or withholding; that I shall enter no supplementary fees, surcharges, or assessments upon any vessel, cargo, or merchant without written authorisation bearing the merchant prince's seal; and that I accept these terms on penalty of removal from office and forfeiture of my bond.*

The oath bears Vael's signature and the merchant prince's seal. It is dated fourteen months earlier.

Aldren sets the oath beside Gerren's copied ledger entries. He asks Gerren to summon the harbour-master to the hall for the following morning.

**E4 (violation scene -- flashback, embedded document).** Gerren adds one more document to the file before the hearing: a petition he has retrieved from the treasury's archives, written in Vael's hand and dated thirteen months earlier, two months after her appointment. He reads it into the record:

> *To the treasury of Lord Aldren -- I write to request emergency funding for repairs to the river wall at Thornmouth harbour. The wall's eastern section has deteriorated to the point where it fails to prevent flooding at high tide. The harbour's lower warehouses have been flooded twice this season. I have obtained estimates from three masons: the minimum repair cost is 1,200 crowns. The harbour's operating budget does not include capital repairs. I request that the treasury authorise a special disbursement. This is the third petition I have submitted on this matter. The previous two, dated four months and seven months prior to my appointment, were submitted by my predecessor and received no reply.*

The treasury's file contains the petition, date-stamped and marked "received." Beneath the date-stamp, in the treasury clerk's hand: *Referred to annual budget review. No action at this time.*

Gerren notes that the two prior petitions from Vael's predecessor are also in the file, also date-stamped, also marked with no action.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The merchant prince's hall, the following morning. Estrid Vael stands before the high table. Aldren sits behind it with the oath, the ledger copies, the captain's complaint, and the three petitions arranged before him. Gerren stands to his left.

"You levied an unauthorised surcharge on foreign vessels for twelve months," Aldren says. "You diverted approximately 1,400 crowns from the harbour's collections. You did not remit these funds to the treasury. Name your reason and your authority."

Vael does not hesitate. She says the river wall was failing. She says the harbour's lower warehouses flooded twice in her first month. She says she submitted a repair petition to the treasury and received no response, following two identical petitions from her predecessor that also received no response. She says she waited three months after her own petition. When no funding came, she imposed the surcharge and used the proceeds to repair the wall. The eastern section is now stable. The flooding has stopped.

"You had no written authorisation," Aldren says. "The oath is specific. No supplementary fees without my seal."

"The oath is specific," Vael says. "So were the three petitions. The treasury received all three and acted on none. The wall was going to collapse. When it collapsed, the harbour would have closed, and the tariff revenue the treasury depends on would have stopped entirely. I chose the smaller violation over the larger loss."

Aldren asks Gerren whether the petitions are authentic. Gerren confirms they are -- all three received, logged, and unanswered.

Aldren asks Vael whether she will acknowledge that the surcharge was unauthorised. She says it was. She says she does not dispute the facts. She disputes the idea that inaction by the treasury left her with any other option.

"The rate-scroll is the law of this harbour," Aldren says. "You rewrote it without my seal. The captain who filed the complaint has every right to demand restitution, and so does every foreign captain who paid the surcharge in the last twelve months."

He pauses. Then he says: "The surcharge is rescinded, effective today. The river-wall repairs stand -- I will not tear down a wall to make a point. The treasury will audit the repair expenditures and determine whether the funds were spent appropriately. You are suspended from the harbour-master's office pending that audit. If the audit is clean, you will be reinstated with a formal reprimand entered in the court record. If it is not, you will be removed and your bond forfeit."

Vael removes the harbour-master's key from her belt and places it on the table. "The audit will be clean," she says. "Every penny is in the ledger."

Aldren nods to Gerren. Gerren collects the key and escorts Vael from the hall. The scribe seals the record and files it with the court.
