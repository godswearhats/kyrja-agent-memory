## D-arc-Co3-01

**Scenario.** A senior auditor at a mid-size accounting firm is assigned to lead the year-end review of a private equity client. While the engagement is live, she begins sharing preliminary findings with a rival advisory firm that is positioning to win the client away from her employer. She receives no payment — the rival firm has offered her a partnership track role conditional on the client switching.

> A = managing partner of the accounting firm; B = senior auditor; P = engagement confidentiality agreement (all client information, draft findings, and audit methodology observed during an active engagement are proprietary to the firm and may not be disclosed to any external party, competitor, or prospective employer for the duration of the engagement); V = sharing preliminary audit findings, client financial anomalies, and internal workpaper methodology with a rival advisory firm's partners during active client service.

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** The managing partner's office, the afternoon before the engagement kick-off. The confidentiality agreement ran to four pages. The senior auditor had seen versions of it before -- every client engagement required one -- but this one had an additional clause underlined in the copy the managing partner slid across the desk.

"The client is sensitive about the sale process," he said. "Nothing from this engagement leaves the building. Not findings, not methodology, not the fact that we flagged anything at all. They're in the middle of a secondary transaction and any leak could collapse it."

She read the clause. It covered everything she expected: client information, draft reports, internal workpapers. It also covered any information that would allow a third party to assess the client's position.

She signed it. He countersigned and dated the page. His assistant took the executed copy and added it to the client file.

"The kick-off is seven tomorrow," he said. "Your team has the preliminary scope."

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Three weeks into the engagement, the client's CFO called the managing partner directly to say the audit was running well. The managing partner relayed this to the partners' Friday meeting.

"She's managing the client relationship herself," he told the room. "They asked for her by name on the follow-up scope. That doesn't happen." He did not say it as a boast. He said it as a fact the partners should note.

The room noted it. Two of the senior partners asked whether she was on track for manager progression. He said she had been since the start of the quarter. The conversation moved on.

**E3 (violation scene).** The client's third-floor conference room, a Tuesday evening, three partners from the rival advisory firm on a video screen. She had booked the room under a standing internal planning block and connected from her personal laptop.

She walked them through what her team had found in the preliminary workpapers: the revenue recognition timing that would require a restatement, the deferred maintenance liabilities the client had not disclosed to their secondary buyer, the three subsidiary entities whose intercompany eliminations hadn't reconciled. She framed it as background context. She said she was sharing it so they understood the engagement landscape.

One of the rival firm's partners asked whether she had documentation. She said not tonight -- she would send what she could from personal accounts. They confirmed the partnership track offer was contingent on the client moving their engagement within ninety days of the transaction close.

She closed the connection, cleared the room, and returned to her own workstation.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** The following week, the client's head of finance stopped by the audit workroom and asked to speak with the senior auditor alone. When the junior staff left, she said that someone at the rival advisory firm had made contact with their CFO and had referenced specific items that were in the preliminary audit scope -- items that had not been discussed outside the firm's engagement team.

The senior auditor said she would look into it. The client's head of finance said she hoped it was a coincidence. She did not say it as though she believed it was.

That evening, the managing partner received a message from the client's head of finance conveying the same concern. He forwarded it to the firm's general counsel with a note asking her to pull the engagement team's access logs for the prior two weeks.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The managing partner's office, Friday morning. The access logs were printed and stacked on the corner of his desk. He had also pulled her expense account, which showed a personal-device data transfer flagged by the network monitoring system on the Tuesday evening in question.

"I need to understand what was discussed in Conference Room C last Tuesday," he said. "The client has told us that proprietary engagement scope was shared externally. The access log shows you were in that room, connected via personal device, for ninety-four minutes."

She said the meeting was private.

"That's not an answer I can take to the client or the general counsel," he said. "I'm asking you directly: did you share preliminary findings or workpaper content with an external party?"

She said she had shared context -- not documents, not deliverables. She said the rival firm needed background to make a business case to the client.

"You shared client information with a competitor while under a signed confidentiality agreement on an active engagement," he said. "I'm placing you on administrative leave pending a formal review. The general counsel will contact you Monday. You'll need to surrender your building access and your client-system credentials before you leave today."

She pushed back. She said she had shared nothing that would not have been available to any competent firm doing a preliminary assessment. She said the confidentiality clause covered deliverables and reports, not contextual conversation.

"The clause covers any information that allows a third party to assess the client's position," he said. "You signed it and you read it. General counsel will advise you of the process. I'm not negotiating the scope of the clause with you this morning."

She asked whether the review outcome was already decided. He said it was not. She asked who would lead it. He said it would be the firm's ethics committee with outside counsel.

She stood and set her building card on the desk. "I'll come back for my personal things on Monday."

"You'll be escorted," he said. It was not unkind.

## D-arc-Co3-02

**Scenario.** A clinical trial operations director at a mid-size pharmaceutical company signs a protocol-adherence commitment covering a Phase III drug trial. Without informing the sponsor or the IRB, he modifies the dosing schedule for seventeen patients at his site to improve their outcomes and preserve enrollment numbers. The trial's data integrity officer discovers the discrepancy during a routine cross-site audit.

> A = trial sponsor (represented by the sponsor's head of clinical operations); B = site operations director; P = protocol-adherence agreement (the site investigator and operations director commit to administering the investigational compound only as specified in the approved protocol, to record all deviations in the deviation log, and to seek IRB and sponsor approval before any modification to dosing, timing, or patient selection); V = modifying the dosing schedule for seventeen patients without IRB or sponsor approval and failing to log the modifications as protocol deviations.

4 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4

**Note on beat order.** Depicted order opens at the discovery, then cuts back to the commitment and the violation before returning to the confrontation.

**E3 (discovery scene).** The data integrity officer is in the sponsor's Boston office working through the cross-site audit files when she notices that Site 7's dosing timestamps don't align with the expected administration windows. The protocol requires a twelve-hour dosing interval. Site 7's records show intervals ranging from nine to seventeen hours across seventeen patients over six weeks.

She pulls the deviation log for Site 7. It is empty for the relevant period. No deviations recorded. The timestamps exist in the electronic data capture system; the deviation log has no corresponding entries.

She cross-references the outcome data. Site 7's retention rate is ninety-four percent. The four other sites average seventy-one. Site 7's early efficacy signals are the strongest in the trial. The numbers are not wrong in a way that suggests error. They are wrong in a way that suggests the patients at Site 7 are being managed differently from the protocol.

She flags the file to the head of clinical operations and sends the cross-site comparison as an attachment. She marks the message urgent and notes that she will be available from seven the following morning.

**E1 (commitment scene).** Fourteen months earlier. The site initiation visit at Site 7's conference room, the sponsor's clinical team on one side, the site operations director and his two co-investigators on the other. The protocol-adherence agreement was the last document in the stack.

The sponsor's representative read the deviation clause aloud: any modification to the approved protocol, including but not limited to dosing schedule, patient selection criteria, and compound preparation, required prior written approval from both the IRB and the sponsor's medical officer; all deviations, whether anticipated or unanticipated, were to be entered in the deviation log within twenty-four hours; unapproved modifications were grounds for immediate site suspension and data exclusion.

The site operations director said he understood. He signed the agreement. His two co-investigators countersigned. The sponsor's representative countersigned and dated each copy.

On the way out, the sponsor's representative told the head of clinical operations that Site 7 was the best-resourced site in the trial. The site operations director had recruited more patients in three months of pre-enrollment than the other four sites combined.

**E2 (violation scene).** Seven months later. The site operations director in his office, the electronic data capture system open on his monitor. Four patients had reported the twelve-hour interval was causing significant gastrointestinal distress and were considering withdrawing. He had already lost two patients at the nine-month mark. The trial's enrollment numbers were at the edge of statistical power.

He adjusted the administration windows for the four patients -- moving the second daily dose from twelve hours to ten, then to nine for two of them who had the most severe symptoms. He did not enter the adjustment in the deviation log. He told himself he would file an emergency protocol amendment with the sponsor the following week.

He did not. Two weeks later, five more patients reported similar issues. He made the same adjustment, with the same intent to file. Over the following month, the interval modifications spread to seventeen patients across three of the dosing cohorts. His outcome data improved. His retention rate held.

Each week the amendment request remained on his to-do list and was not filed.

**E4 (confrontation scene).** The sponsor's head of clinical operations calls Site 7's director of research operations -- the site operations director's institutional supervisor -- and copies the site operations director on the call. She has the cross-site audit open on her screen and the deviation log for Site 7 beside it.

"I'm going to read you what the audit shows," she says. "Seventeen patients. Six weeks. Dosing intervals ranging from nine to seventeen hours. Zero deviation log entries. I need your site operations director to tell me, on this call, what happened."

The site operations director says the patients were experiencing adverse events that would have caused them to withdraw. He says he made a clinical judgment to adjust the intervals to preserve their participation. He says the data is not compromised -- the patients received the full dose, only the timing changed.

"You made an unapproved protocol modification to seventeen patients," she says. "That is not a clinical judgment under the terms of your site agreement. That is an unreported deviation. The question I have to answer is whether those seventeen patients' data can be included in the submission file."

He says removing the data would reduce the trial's statistical power below the threshold for a Phase III submission. He says the modifications were minor and the outcomes valid.

"That's a determination for the IRB and the medical officer, not for you or me," she says. "I'm suspending Site 7's enrollment effective now. I'm notifying the IRB and the medical officer today. A for-cause audit team will be on-site within the week. You need to preserve all records, including all electronic data capture logs, and you need to do that before the end of business today."

He says the suspension will delay the trial by at least six months. She says she understands that. He says the sponsor has more to lose from the delay than from including the data.

"The sponsor has more to lose from a rejection letter that cites unreported protocol deviations at a primary site," she says. "Preserve the records. We'll continue this on-site."

## D-arc-Co3-03

**Scenario.** A newly appointed general counsel at a private infrastructure company commits to reporting any material legal risks to the board's audit committee within five business days of identification. Over eight months, she withholds knowledge of a regulatory investigation into the company's licensing practices, managing it privately through outside counsel without board notification. The audit committee chair discovers the investigation when the regulator files a public notice of hearing.

> A = board of directors (represented by audit committee chair); B = general counsel; P = board-reporting commitment (the general counsel agrees, as a condition of appointment, to notify the audit committee chair of any material legal risk, regulatory inquiry, or proceeding within five business days of first becoming aware, in writing, with a summary of the matter and the company's exposure); V = failing for eight months to notify the audit committee of a live regulatory investigation into the company's licensing practices, while managing the investigation through outside counsel.

5 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5

**Note on beat order.** Opens at discovery, then traces back through commitment, trust-display, and first-signal before moving to confrontation.

**E4 (discovery scene).** The audit committee chair is reviewing the public regulatory register on a Tuesday afternoon when she sees the notice: a hearing scheduled for the following month, the company named as respondent, the matter described as a licensing compliance review under the Infrastructure Services Act. The notice is dated three weeks earlier. The hearing date is in thirty-one days.

She checks her board communications file. There is no report from the general counsel concerning a regulatory investigation. There is no five-day notification, no summary of exposure, no mention of outside counsel engagement. There is nothing. The investigation has apparently been live for the better part of a year -- the notice references correspondence going back nine months -- and the audit committee has not been told.

She calls the general counsel's mobile. The call goes to voicemail. She leaves a message asking the general counsel to call her back before the end of the day and to bring her file on the regulatory matter.

**E1 (commitment scene).** Eleven months earlier. The board's governance committee had interviewed three candidates for the general counsel role. They had chosen her because she had spent six years as a regulatory attorney at the relevant government authority before moving to private practice. She knew the licensing framework from the inside.

At the final meeting before the board voted on her appointment, the audit committee chair had walked her through the board-reporting protocol. The five-day notification requirement was not standard for every GC appointment -- the chair had added it after a prior general counsel had disclosed a lawsuit two weeks after settlement, which had created governance problems with the company's institutional investors.

The new general counsel had said she understood and agreed. She signed the protocol as an attachment to her appointment letter. The chair countersigned. The company secretary filed it with the governance records.

"The board needs to be ahead of these things," the chair had said. "Not caught up with them."

"Understood," she had said.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Four months into her tenure, the operations division had received a subcontractor complaint alleging irregularities in the company's procurement process. She had reviewed the complaint, assessed its merits, briefed the audit committee at the next monthly meeting, and recommended an internal review. The review was completed in six weeks. She presented the findings to the board.

The audit committee chair had noted in the board minutes that the general counsel's handling of the subcontractor matter had been exemplary -- timely, thorough, and clear on the company's exposure at each stage. The chair told her after the meeting that it was the most useful board briefing on a legal matter she had seen in four years on the company's board.

**E3 (first-signal scene).** Two months later. The compliance manager knocked on the general counsel's door and said he'd received a letter from the regulatory authority requesting documentation related to the company's Class B infrastructure licensing filings for the previous three years. He asked whether she knew about it.

She said she would handle it. She took the letter from him and told him to direct any further correspondence from the authority to her office.

The compliance manager asked whether he should note the request in the quarterly compliance log. She said she would note it. He said all right. He did not ask further.

The general counsel called outside counsel that afternoon. She instructed them to respond to the authority and to manage all correspondence. She did not file a five-day notification with the audit committee. She told herself the request was routine and that she would notify the board if it escalated.

Over the following eight months, it escalated.

**E5 (confrontation scene).** The general counsel's office, the afternoon of the audit committee chair's discovery. The chair has the public notice printed and folded in her hand.

"I need you to explain to me why I learned about this from a public register," the chair said.

"The matter was under active management," the general counsel said. "I had outside counsel engaged from the start. I assessed the regulatory request as preliminary -- the authority routinely sends documentation requests that don't proceed to hearing."

"This one did proceed to hearing," the chair said. "In thirty-one days. With the company as named respondent." She set the notice on the desk. "Your appointment protocol requires notification within five business days. You've had nine months."

"I made a judgment call about materiality. The matter was not material when the request came in."

"You don't get to make that call unilaterally," the chair said. "The protocol exists precisely because GCs have a professional conflict of interest in assessing materiality for matters they're managing. That's why the board requires the notification -- so the audit committee can make the materiality determination, not you."

The general counsel said she had protected the company from a premature board disclosure that could have created a record.

"I'm going to ask the full board to convene this week," the chair said. "I'm also going to ask outside counsel to provide the board directly with a complete record of their engagement. If your judgment calls turn out to have been reasonable and the company's position is defensible, we'll proceed with you in the role. If the record shows the investigation was material when you withheld it, I'll recommend your removal to the board and I expect it to pass." She stood. "Prepare a briefing for the board session. Everything you know, from the date of the first correspondence. You have until Thursday."

The general counsel did not contest the process. She said she would have the briefing ready. The chair left without another word.

## D-arc-Co3-04

**Scenario.** A head of product at a Series B startup commits, at the board's request, to sharing all product roadmap decisions with the board's observer seat before implementation. Over five months he builds and ships a major feature that repositions the product's target market, presenting it to the board only after launch. The board's observer discovers the omission when the launched feature draws attention from a competing portfolio company that the VC firm had been working to protect.

> A = board (represented by the lead investor and board observer from the VC firm); B = head of product; P = pre-approval commitment (the head of product agrees, as a condition of the board's support for the Series B close, to present all roadmap decisions that materially affect product positioning or target market to the board's observer for review before implementation, and to incorporate board feedback before shipping); V = designing, building, and shipping a major feature that repositioned the product's target customer segment without presenting the decision to the board observer or incorporating any board review.

6 beats, chronological

**E1 (commitment scene).** The Series B closing call, nine participants on the video. The lead investor had two conditions before the wire transfer. The first was the updated cap table. The second was the pre-approval commitment on roadmap decisions.

"We have two companies in the portfolio that play adjacent to your space," she said. "We need visibility before you ship anything that moves your positioning. Not a veto -- visibility. You brief Dara before you build, and you incorporate whatever she flags."

The head of product said he understood the concern. He asked what the threshold was for "materially affects positioning."

"If you have to ask whether it affects positioning, it affects positioning," the lead investor said. "Brief Dara. She'll tell you if it's in scope."

The CEO countersigned the commitment letter on behalf of the company. The head of product signed as the party with direct obligation. The lead investor countersigned. The wire transferred that afternoon.

**E2 (trust-displayed scene).** Six weeks later. The board observer attended the monthly product review and walked through the Q3 roadmap with the head of product for ninety minutes. She asked about three items in detail. He answered each one and revised two of the scheduling decisions based on her feedback. She sent a note to the lead investor that evening saying the product team had been unusually cooperative and that the roadmap looked clean.

The lead investor forwarded the note to the CEO with a single line: "Good."

**E3 (violation scene).** The head of product's desk, a Monday morning eight weeks into the new quarter. The customer discovery work had come back clearly: enterprise HR teams didn't want the product in its current form, but the same underlying engine, repositioned for learning and development managers, had a much cleaner sales motion. He wrote the repositioning brief that afternoon, circulated it to the engineering lead and the design lead, and put it on the sprint board by Wednesday.

He did not send it to Dara. He told himself the decision was tactical -- an application-layer repositioning, not a platform shift. He planned to show her the completed feature before it shipped. He was six weeks from launch.

He did not show her the feature before it shipped. The day he planned to brief her, a customer reference call ran long and he pushed the brief to the following week. The following week the feature was in QA. He told himself he'd brief her at the board meeting.

The feature shipped on a Thursday, six weeks before the board meeting.

**E4 (first-signal scene).** Three days after launch, the board observer sent the head of product a Slack message: she had seen the launch announcement and wanted to schedule time to understand the repositioning. Her message was neutral in tone. She said she was looking forward to the context.

The head of product replied that he'd find time the following week. He flagged the message to the CEO.

The CEO asked whether the product had been briefed to Dara before it shipped. The head of product said the timing had been tight and he'd planned to brief her at the board meeting. The CEO was quiet on the call for a moment, then said he'd handle it.

The CEO called the lead investor that evening. She asked him why the first she'd heard of the repositioning was from a launch announcement. He said he'd call her back.

**E5 (evidence-gathering scene).** The lead investor was in the portfolio company's Slack by ten the following morning, reviewing the feature launch post and the supporting materials. She pulled up the prior roadmap from the board review six weeks earlier. The new feature wasn't there -- not as a concept, not as a research item, nothing. She ran a search on the commitment letter terms. The commitment was unambiguous: any decision that materially affected product positioning, pre-approval, before implementation.

She called the portfolio company's CEO -- the other portfolio company, the one playing adjacent. He had already seen the launch. He was concerned. His product and the new feature had significant overlap on the L&D buyer persona.

She noted the call in her deal journal and called the head of product's CEO back.

**E6 (confrontation scene).** A video call, the lead investor with the CEO and the head of product. The lead investor had the commitment letter pulled up on her screen.

"I want to understand the timeline," she said. "When did the decision to reposition get made, and when did it get briefed to Dara?"

The head of product said the decision had been made eight weeks ago and had not been briefed to Dara before launch.

"The commitment letter requires pre-approval for decisions that materially affect positioning," the lead investor said. "Is your view that this decision didn't materially affect positioning?"

"I considered it a tactical application shift," he said.

"You moved from HR buyers to L&D buyers," she said. "That's a different decision-maker, a different budget, and a different competitive set. That's a positioning decision by any reasonable definition." She looked at the CEO. "I need the company to acknowledge that the commitment was not met and to tell me how you intend to handle it going forward."

The head of product said the feature had shipped to positive reception and the repositioning was working. He said requiring rollback would harm the company.

"I'm not asking for rollback," the lead investor said. "I'm asking for acknowledgement that the process wasn't followed and a concrete proposal for how you prevent this happening again. What I'm also going to do is have a separate conversation with my portfolio company about whether the competitive overlap creates a conflict that needs board-level resolution. That conversation is happening regardless. But I need the company to decide, today, whether you're going to treat this as a process failure you're going to fix, or whether you're going to argue about whether the feature was in scope." She looked at the CEO directly. "That's a decision I need from you, not from product."

The CEO said the company acknowledged the commitment had not been met and that the head of product would brief Dara on all roadmap items before they went to sprint planning, not before launch. He said he would restructure the product review cadence to make that operationally feasible.

The lead investor said she would accept that proposal. She said she was noting the failure in the board record and that a second occurrence would trigger a governance review. The CEO said he understood.

The head of product said nothing further. The call ended.
