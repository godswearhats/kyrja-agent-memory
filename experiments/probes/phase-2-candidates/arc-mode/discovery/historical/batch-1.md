# Discovery arc x Historical -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## Di-arc-Hi1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven. Setting: 1780s France.**

**Scenario.** A provincial tax collector in pre-revolutionary France believes the peasants in his district are poor because they are idle and improvident, as the intendant's reports have stated for years. Through a series of encounters -- a village inspection, a parish priest's ledger, and a conversation with a farm widow -- he comes to understand that the peasants are impoverished because the tax burden itself consumes more than their surplus, and that the intendant's reports have been constructed to obscure this.

> A = Jean-Luc Maret (subdelegue, provincial tax collector); B = "The peasants of the Auxerre district are poor because they are idle and do not manage their holdings efficiently, as the intendant's annual assessments confirm"; E = direct observation of a village's working conditions, a parish priest's baptismal and burial ledger showing demographic collapse, and a farm widow's accounting of her household's tax burden; B' = "The peasants are impoverished because the taille, the gabelle, and the corvee collectively consume more than their annual surplus -- they cannot accumulate because the tax structure prevents it, and the intendant's assessments blame the peasants in order to justify maintaining the collection rates."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The intendant's office in Auxerre, a morning in April 1787. Maret sat across from the intendant with the annual tax assessment for the district on the table between them.

"The collections are down again," the intendant said. "Seven percent below target for the third consecutive year."

Maret nodded. He had prepared the report himself. The intendant turned to the assessment's summary page, which Maret had written in the standard form: *The shortfall is attributable to the indolence and poor management of the rural holdings. The soil is productive. The climate has been favourable. The deficit reflects a failure of industry among the assessed population, not a failure of the assessment itself.*

"This is accurate?" the intendant asked.

"It is the same conclusion we have drawn for the past five years, monsieur. The land is good. The harvests have been adequate. The peasants do not apply themselves."

The intendant signed the assessment and returned it to Maret. "Then the rates remain as they are. Collect what you can. Use the marechaussee if necessary."

Maret took the document and left. He had written five of these assessments. Each one reached the same conclusion. He did not doubt it. The language was the intendant's language, passed down from his predecessor, and before that from the controller-general's office in Paris. It was the official explanation for rural poverty in every province Maret had served. He had no reason to believe his district was different.

**E2 (partial evidence).** Three weeks later, a Tuesday morning. Maret rode into the village of Saint-Preux-les-Vignes to conduct a routine tax inspection. He brought a clerk and a ledger. The village owed six hundred livres in taille and was two quarters in arrears.

He expected to find idleness. He found the village at work before dawn. The women were in the fields with hoes, turning soil that was already planted. The men were repairing a drainage ditch along the lower pasture, working in mud to their knees. The children carried water from the well in buckets too heavy for them. The only person not working was an old man sitting against the church wall, and when Maret looked more closely, he saw that the old man was braiding rope.

Maret walked through the village with his clerk. He counted the livestock: four cows, eleven goats, no horses. The previous year's assessment had listed six cows and two horses. He asked the village headman where the horses were.

"Sold," the headman said. "To pay the gabelle."

"And the two cows?"

"Dead. We could not afford the fodder after the corvee took the men for road work during the harvest."

Maret wrote the numbers in his ledger. He stood in the centre of the village and looked at the patched roofs, the thin cattle, the children carrying water, and the people working in the half-light. He did not adjust his assessment. But the gap between what the intendant's office described and what he was looking at was wider than he had expected.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Two days later, the rectory at Saint-Preux. Maret called on the parish priest, Father Giraud, to verify the village's population for the tax rolls. The priest received him in the vestry and produced the parish register -- the book of baptisms, marriages, and burials.

Maret asked for the current population count. Father Giraud opened the register to the most recent year and began reading the entries.

"Forty-seven baptisms in this parish in the year 1780," the priest said. "Do you know how many we had last year?"

Maret said he did not.

"Twelve. And burials: twenty-one in 1780, forty-four last year. The parish is shrinking. More are dying than are being born, and the young men who can leave are leaving -- to the towns, to the army, anywhere the tax collector does not follow them."

Maret asked what was killing them.

"Hunger," the priest said. "Not famine -- we have not had a true famine in years. But the families cannot keep enough of what they grow. The taille takes a quarter. The gabelle takes another portion for salt they cannot afford but must buy. The tithe takes a tenth. The corvee takes their labour for thirty days a year -- thirty days when they are building roads instead of tending their fields. By the time the collections are finished, a family of six is living on what would feed three. The children sicken first."

Father Giraud turned the register toward Maret. The burial entries for the previous year listed causes of death in the priest's careful hand: *fever, weakness, fever, flux, weakness, fever.* The ages were concentrated between zero and five, and between sixty and seventy. The middle years -- the working years -- were being emptied by departure, not by death.

"You write in your assessment that they are idle," the priest said. "I have served this parish for eighteen years. They are the hardest-working people I have ever known. They are poor because you take what they earn, and then you blame them for having nothing."

Maret closed the register and returned it. He did not reply. He rode back to the village, sat on his horse at the edge of the field, and watched the women working the soil in the fading light.

**E4 (secondary confirming evidence).** The following Sunday. Maret returned to Saint-Preux without his clerk. He found the farm widow Marie Duclos in her kitchen garden behind the house. She was the headman's sister, and she had managed the family holding alone since her husband's death two years earlier.

He asked her to show him her accounts. She did not have a ledger. She had a piece of slate on which she scratched numbers with a nail.

She showed him the slate. She had divided it into two columns: what the farm produced and what the farm owed. The left column listed the harvest in bushels of grain, the milk yield, the kitchen garden output, the three goats' worth of cheese. The right column listed the taille, the gabelle, the tithe, the seigneurial dues to the local lord, and the corvee days converted to their equivalent in lost labour.

The right column was larger than the left.

"Every year?" Maret asked.

"Every year since my husband died," she said. "And most years before. We sell a goat to cover the difference. When the goats are gone, we sell the tools. When the tools are gone, the children go to the towns and we die here."

She said it without anger. She said it the way a person describes the weather.

Maret copied the numbers from her slate into his notebook. He thanked her and rode home. He sat at his desk that evening and placed her numbers beside the intendant's assessment. The assessment said the peasants were poor because they were idle. The widow's slate said the peasants were poor because the total tax burden exceeded their total production. Both documents could not be true.

**E5 (belief revision).** The intendant's office, a week later. Maret had requested the meeting. He brought his notebook, the parish register figures, and the widow's accounting.

"The assessment is wrong," Maret said. He laid the numbers on the intendant's desk. "The shortfall is not caused by idleness. The peasants in this district are working every hour the sun is up and they cannot meet the collection because the collection exceeds what the land produces. The taille, the gabelle, the tithe, and the corvee together take more than the surplus. They have been selling livestock and tools to cover the deficit, and they are running out of both."

The intendant looked at the numbers. He was quiet for a long time.

"And the assessments we have filed for the past five years?" he said.

"They describe the peasants as idle because that is the language the controller-general's office requires. If we report that the land is productive and the people are working and the collections still fall short, the conclusion is that the rates are too high. The controller-general does not accept that conclusion. So we write the version he will accept."

The intendant leaned back. "You are suggesting that I have been filing false assessments."

"I am suggesting that I have been writing them," Maret said. "I wrote five of them. I believed what I wrote because I had never gone to the villages and counted what the people actually owe against what they actually produce. I have now done so. The numbers do not support the assessments."

The intendant was silent. Then he said: "If you send this to Paris, they will reduce the district's allocation and increase the collection rates in the neighbouring districts to compensate. You will not help these peasants. You will impoverish their neighbours."

Maret said he understood. He asked the intendant to reduce the taille assessment for Saint-Preux by fifteen percent, within the discretionary margin the intendant was authorised to grant, and to defer the arrears. He said it would not solve the structural problem, but it would keep the village alive for another year.

The intendant signed the reduction. Maret took the document and left the office carrying a model of his province that was fundamentally different from the one he had carried in.

## Di-arc-Hi1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style. Setting: 1540s Ottoman Empire.**

**Scenario.** A kadi (judge) in a provincial Ottoman court believes a local Christian merchant is smuggling goods to avoid paying the customs duty, based on reports from the market inspector. Through direct investigation, the kadi comes to understand that the merchant's shipments are legal under an imperial trade capitulation he was never informed of, and that the market inspector has been filing false reports to extort bribes from foreign merchants operating under the same capitulation.

> A = Kadi Mehmed Efendi; B = "The Christian merchant Nikolas is evading customs duty by smuggling goods through the river port"; E = the merchant's imperial firman granting duty exemption, the harbour master's logs showing declared shipments, and a pattern of identical complaints filed against every foreign merchant in the district; B' = "Nikolas's shipments are legal under an imperial capitulation; the market inspector has been filing fraudulent smuggling complaints against capitulation-holding merchants to force them to pay bribes for the complaints to be withdrawn."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The market inspector's report had arrived at the kadi's court on a Tuesday morning, written on good paper in the inspector's practised hand. Kadi Mehmed read it at his bench while the court scribe prepared the day's docket.

The report accused the Christian merchant Nikolas of Ragusa of smuggling woollen cloth through the river port at Vidin without paying the customs duty. The inspector cited three shipments over two months -- each arriving by boat, each unloaded at the private quay below the merchant's warehouse, each bypassing the customs house. The inspector had witnessed the unloading of the most recent shipment himself. He requested that the kadi issue a summons and order the seizure of the undeclared goods.

Mehmed had served as kadi in Vidin for three years. The market inspector, a man named Hasan, had been in his post for longer -- six years, perhaps seven. His reports were always detailed and always plausible. Mehmed had acted on them before and had never found cause to doubt them. A Ragusan merchant smuggling cloth to avoid the customs duty was an ordinary case. Christians in the empire's border provinces traded under various arrangements, and the temptation to avoid the duty was constant.

He issued the summons. He expected the case to be simple.

**E2 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The following Thursday, the kadi's court. Nikolas appeared with a translator and a leather document case. He was a heavy man in his fifties with a direct manner that the translator struggled to soften.

Mehmed read the charges. Nikolas listened, then opened his case and produced a document the kadi had not expected: an imperial firman, sealed with the sultan's tughra, granting the merchants of Ragusa the right to trade in the Ottoman Empire's Danubian provinces without payment of customs duty for a period of twenty years. The firman was dated eight years earlier. It was a trade capitulation -- one of the agreements the Porte made with foreign trading cities to encourage commerce along the empire's river routes.

Mehmed examined the firman carefully. The seal was genuine. The language was the imperial chancery's standard form. The exemption covered precisely the goods Nikolas was accused of smuggling -- woollen cloth imported by Ragusan nationals through Ottoman river ports.

He looked at the market inspector's report. The report did not mention the capitulation. It did not mention the firman. It accused Nikolas of smuggling goods that he was imperially authorised to import duty-free.

Mehmed asked the court scribe to bring the customs registers for the river port. He asked Nikolas whether he had declared his shipments at the harbour master's office. Nikolas said he had -- every shipment, with the firman presented at each declaration. The harbour master had logged them and waved them through. The customs house was never involved because the firman exempted him from customs.

Mehmed told Nikolas to return the following week. He dismissed the court early and sent for the harbour master's logs.

**E3 (secondary confirming evidence).** The harbour master's office, the following morning. Mehmed sat at the harbourmaster's desk with the port logs open before him and the customs registers beside them.

The harbour master had indeed logged Nikolas's shipments. Three entries over two months, each noting the Ragusan firman, each stamped with the harbour master's seal. The shipments were declared, documented, and legal. They had never been presented to the customs house because the capitulation did not require it.

Mehmed then did something he had not thought to do before. He asked the harbour master how many other merchants in Vidin held similar capitulations. The harbour master consulted his register and named four others -- two Venetians, one Florentine, and another Ragusan. All held imperial firmans granting duty exemptions of various kinds. All had been trading through the river port for years.

Mehmed asked whether any of these merchants had been the subject of complaints from the market inspector. The harbour master's face changed. He went to a chest in the corner of the office and produced a bundle of copies -- letters he had written to the kadi's court over the past two years, each protesting a market inspector's report against a capitulation-holding merchant, each noting that the shipments in question were declared and legal. The letters had been filed with the court scribe. Mehmed had never seen them.

He took the bundle, thanked the harbour master, and returned to his court. He asked the scribe for the inspector's complaint files for the past two years. He laid them out on his bench and read through them. Of thirty-one complaints Hasan had filed, nineteen were against foreign merchants. Of those nineteen, he could now identify twelve who held imperial capitulations. The complaints followed the same pattern: undeclared goods, smuggling through the river port, a request for summons and seizure.

He sat with the files and understood what he was looking at. The market inspector was not catching smugglers. He was filing false reports against merchants who held legal exemptions -- merchants who would not know the local courts well enough to defend themselves quickly, who might pay a bribe to make the complaint disappear rather than endure a lengthy case. The inspector was using the kadi's court as a tool for extortion.

**E4 (belief revision).** The kadi's court, the following Thursday. Mehmed dismissed the charges against Nikolas and entered the imperial firman into the court record. He returned the seized goods and dictated a formal apology into the transcript.

Then he summoned the market inspector. Hasan arrived expecting to hear the outcome of the case. Mehmed laid the harbour master's logs, the bundle of unanswered protest letters, and the twelve matching complaints on the bench.

"These twelve complaints," Mehmed said, "were filed against merchants holding imperial capitulations. The shipments were declared and legal. You knew this. You have access to the harbour master's logs."

Hasan said the harbour master's logs were unreliable and that the capitulations had not been verified.

"The firmans bear the sultan's tughra," Mehmed said. "Each one was presented at the harbour master's office and recorded. You filed complaints against merchants you knew to be exempt. Explain why."

Hasan said nothing.

Mehmed dictated into the court record: the twelve complaints were fraudulent; the market inspector had abused his office to harass legally exempt merchants; the matter was referred to the provincial governor for disciplinary review. He suspended Hasan from his post pending the governor's decision and ordered the court scribe to send copies of the harbour master's protest letters -- the ones that had been filed and ignored -- to the governor's office with the referral.

He closed the session. After the court emptied, he sat at his bench and re-read his own judgements from the previous two years. Three of Hasan's earlier complaints had resulted in fines against capitulation-holding merchants. Mehmed had imposed those fines himself, relying on the inspector's reports, without checking the harbour logs or asking whether the accused held exemptions. The merchants had paid. None had returned to contest the ruling. He noted the three cases in his journal, marked them for review, and began drafting the orders to refund the fines.

## Di-arc-Hi1-3

**6 beats, non-linear (depicted: E4 -> E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E5 -> E6; chronological: E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E4 -> E5 -> E6), embedded document + free indirect. Setting: 1860s United States.**

**Scenario.** A Union Army surgeon believes the high mortality rate in his field hospital is caused by the severity of battle wounds, as the conditions of war make inevitable. Through a visiting sanitary commission inspector's report, his own experimental comparison, and a confrontation with a colleague, he comes to accept that the majority of deaths are caused by post-surgical infection transmitted by the surgeons' own unwashed instruments, and that changing the protocol would save more lives than any improvement in surgical technique.

> A = Dr. Josiah Wren (Union Army field surgeon); B = "The high death rate among my surgical patients is an unavoidable consequence of the severity of battlefield wounds"; E = the sanitary commission's comparative mortality data, a controlled comparison of washed versus unwashed instruments in his own hospital, and Dr. Caulfield's admission that he has known about the correlation for months; B' = "The majority of post-surgical deaths in this hospital are caused by infection introduced during surgery itself, transmitted by instruments and hands that are not cleaned between patients -- the wound severity is secondary to the contamination."

**E4 (evidence scene -- present tense).** The hospital tent at Brandy Station, a November morning in 1863. Dr. Wren stands at the surgery table with a letter in one hand and a bone saw in the other. The saw is crusted with dried blood from the previous day's operations. The letter is from the United States Sanitary Commission, forwarded by the medical director's office, and it contains a table of figures that has stopped him mid-preparation.

The table compares post-surgical mortality rates across twelve Union field hospitals for the months of August through October. Wren's hospital -- the Third Division field hospital, Army of the Potomac -- has a post-operative mortality rate of forty-one percent. Three hospitals on the list have rates below twenty percent. The lowest is fourteen percent.

Wren sets the bone saw on the table and reads the commission's annotations. The three low-mortality hospitals share a common practice: they wash all instruments in boiled water between patients and require surgeons to clean their hands with soap before each operation. The commission's inspector notes that the practice is not mandated by the Surgeon General's office and is followed at the discretion of the individual surgeon in charge.

Wren looks at the bone saw. He has used this saw on four patients since it was last cleaned. He has not washed his hands since this morning. This is standard practice. Every surgeon he trained under worked the same way. The saw is a tool. Blood on a tool is evidence of work, not a cause of harm.

But the table says fourteen percent. And his rate is forty-one.

**E1 (prior belief established -- retroactive, embedded document).** From Dr. Wren's surgical log, the entry dated three months earlier, August 1863:

> *Seven amputations today. Four above the knee, two below, one at the shoulder. Lost two on the table -- the shoulder case bled out despite the tourniquet, and one of the knee cases arrested during the cut. Of the five surviving patients, I expect to lose at least two more to gangrene or wound fever within the week. This is consistent with the rates we have observed since Gettysburg. The wounds are terrible. The men arrive with shattered bone, embedded metal, and tissue that has been open to the air and the mud for hours before reaching us. Under these conditions, a forty-percent mortality rate after surgery is not a failure of the surgeon but an honest accounting of what the battlefield delivers to our table. I do what I can with what arrives.*

**E2 (belief reinforced).** September 1863, the hospital tent. Wren's colleague, Dr. Caulfield, is dressing wounds at the next table. A sanitary commission inspector has been through the hospital that morning, asking questions about instrument cleaning and hand washing. Caulfield watched the inspector leave and shook his head.

"These commission men," Caulfield said to Wren across the tent. "Civilians with checklists. They want us to boil our saws between patients. Boil them. As though the men are dying because of rust on a blade and not because a twelve-pound cannonball took their leg off at the thigh."

Wren agreed. The sanitary commission's inspectors were well-meaning but ignorant of field conditions. There was no time to boil instruments between patients when twenty men were waiting on stretchers outside the tent. The idea that a saw needed to be clean to cut bone was an abstraction. What mattered was speed, technique, and the severity of the wound. The cleaning theory was a hypothesis promoted by a few European physicians, and the European physicians were operating on civilians in clean hospitals, not on soldiers in canvas tents beside a Virginia creek.

Wren finished his patient and moved to the next without washing his hands. The commission inspector's visit was forgotten by suppertime.

**E3 (partial evidence).** October 1863, the hospital tent. Wren loses a patient -- a corporal with a clean amputation below the knee, a wound that should have been survivable. The amputation went well. The bleeding was controlled. The stump was dressed. Three days later the corporal developed a fever. On the fifth day, the stump turned black. On the seventh day, the corporal died.

Wren recorded the death in his log. He noted that the wound had been clean at the time of surgery. The gangrene had appeared afterward. He wrote: *Wound fever, cause unknown. The initial injury was not severe enough to explain the outcome.*

He thought about it that evening, sitting outside the tent. The corporal's wound had been one of the least severe he had treated that week. Two patients with worse injuries -- a shattered femur and a chest wound -- had survived. The variable was not the wound. Something else was different.

He did not know what. He filed the log entry and did not return to it until the commission's letter arrived a month later.

**E5 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Late November 1863. Wren has spent two weeks running a comparison. He divided his surgical cases into two groups. For the first group, he followed his standard practice -- unwashed instruments, no hand cleaning. For the second group, he washed all instruments in boiled water before use and cleaned his hands with soap. He assigned patients to groups alternately, by order of arrival, to prevent himself from choosing.

The results are on the table in front of him. In the unwashed group: twelve patients, five dead within two weeks. In the washed group: eleven patients, one dead within two weeks. The washed group's single death was a chest wound that no protocol would have saved.

Wren sits with the numbers. The difference is not subtle. It is not ambiguous. The washed instruments produced a mortality rate of nine percent. The unwashed instruments produced a rate of forty-two percent -- almost exactly his hospital's average since the war began.

The wounds were comparable. The patients were comparable. The surgical technique was the same -- his own. The only variable was whether the instruments had been cleaned. And the variable accounted for nearly all of the difference.

He copies the numbers into a clean page of his log and sits with them for a long time.

**E6 (consequences -- confrontation).** The following morning, the hospital tent. Wren finds Caulfield preparing for surgery, his apron stiff with old blood, his saw on the table.

"I need to show you something," Wren says. He places the comparison data on the instrument tray.

Caulfield reads it. He sets it down.

"Wren, I've seen data like this," Caulfield says. "The commission sent it to every field hospital two months ago. The medical director's circular mentioned it in September."

"You've seen it."

"I've seen it. It's suggestive. But correlation is not mechanism. We do not know why cleaning the instruments changes the outcome. Until someone can explain the mechanism, I am not going to slow my operating pace by twenty minutes per patient to boil a saw."

"Twenty minutes against a thirty-percent difference in mortality."

"A thirty-percent difference in your two-week trial with twenty-three patients. That is not a study. That is an anecdote."

Wren picks up the comparison sheet. "Then I am ordering a protocol change for this hospital. All instruments washed in boiled water between patients. All surgeons wash their hands before operating. Effective today."

"You don't have the authority to order that," Caulfield says. "We hold equal rank."

"I have written to the medical director requesting the authority. Until he responds, I am implementing the protocol for my own cases and requesting that you do the same voluntarily."

"And if I decline?"

"Then our mortality rates will diverge and the numbers will make the argument for me. I will log every case -- mine and yours -- and send the comparison to the medical director and the sanitary commission at the end of the month."

Caulfield looks at him for a long moment. "You would report a colleague's surgical outcomes to a civilian commission."

"I would report the data. The data is what it is."

Caulfield turns back to his patient. He does not wash his instruments. Wren returns to his own table, boils the saw, washes his hands, and begins his first case of the day.

## Di-arc-Hi1-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense. Setting: 1330s Mali Empire.**

**Scenario.** A senior court griot in the Malian capital of Niani believes the empire's western gold trade is thriving, as the caravans arriving from the Bure goldfields have reported full loads for three consecutive seasons. Through a conversation with a returned salt trader, a physical inspection of a caravan's cargo, and testimony from a goldfield overseer, she comes to understand that the caravans have been padding their loads with copper dust mixed into the gold, and that the actual gold yield from Bure has declined by more than half.

> A = Aminata Keita (senior griot and keeper of the royal trade records); B = "The Bure goldfields are producing at full capacity and the empire's gold trade is strong, as the caravan reports confirm"; E = a salt trader's account of devalued gold at the northern markets, a physical assay of a caravan's cargo showing copper adulteration, and the goldfield overseer's testimony about declining yields; B' = "The Bure gold yield has fallen by more than half; the caravans have been adulterating the gold with copper dust to maintain the appearance of full loads, and the trade records I have been keeping are based on falsified weights."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The audience hall of the mansa's palace at Niani, a morning in the dry season. Aminata Keita sits cross-legged on the speaking mat beside the throne, the position she has held for nineteen years. The mansa is receiving the season's caravan reports. The lead merchant of the Bure route steps forward and presents his tally sticks: eight hundred measures of gold dust delivered to the royal treasury over the past season, consistent with the previous two seasons, consistent with the projections Aminata entered in the trade record at the year's beginning.

Aminata records the figure on her board. She recites the three-season total aloud for the court, as is her function. The numbers are strong. The Bure route is the empire's primary source of gold, and the gold is the empire's primary source of power -- it buys salt from the north, loyalty from the provincial chiefs, and respect from the Arab traders who carry Malian gold across the Sahara to the markets of the Maghreb.

The mansa nods. The court is satisfied. Aminata closes the record for the season and begins composing the summary she will deliver at the harvest council. The trade is strong. The goldfields are producing. She has no reason to question the tally sticks. She has never had reason to question them.

**E2 (partial evidence).** Three weeks later, the market square outside the palace compound. Aminata is walking through the traders' quarter when she encounters Seydou, a salt merchant who has just returned from the northern crossing. Seydou trades salt for gold at the desert-edge market towns and has made the crossing fourteen times. He greets Aminata with the respect due her position and asks to speak privately.

They sit in the shade of a baobab at the market's edge. Seydou tells her that the Arab merchants at Timbuktu have begun refusing Malian gold at the standard exchange rate. They claim the gold is lighter than it should be -- that a measure of Malian gold dust, weighed on their scales, does not match the weight it carried five years ago. The Arab merchants are offering thirty percent less salt per measure. Seydou has lost money on his last two crossings.

Aminata asks whether the Arab scales might be dishonest. Seydou says he brought his own scale and weighed the gold himself. The weight per measure is correct. But when the Arab assayer tested the gold with acid and flame, the colour was wrong. The gold burned with a green edge that pure gold does not produce.

Aminata asks what produces a green flame. Seydou says the assayer told him it indicates copper. Copper mixed into the gold dust at a ratio high enough to be detected by fire but low enough to pass a visual inspection.

Aminata thanks Seydou and walks back to the palace. She does not mention the conversation to the mansa. She opens the trade record and re-reads the caravan reports for the past three seasons. The weight figures are consistent. The value figures are consistent. If the gold is adulterated, the tally sticks would still show the correct weight -- copper weighs nearly the same as gold per volume. The adulterator would not need to change the numbers. Only the substance.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The following week. Aminata requests access to the royal treasury's storage chambers -- a privilege her position grants, though she has rarely exercised it. The treasury keeper admits her without question.

She asks to see the most recent delivery from the Bure route. The keeper brings a sealed clay jar containing the season's gold dust, stamped with the caravan leader's mark. Aminata opens the jar and pours a small quantity onto a fired-clay testing dish. She has watched the royal assayer work for years and knows the basic procedure.

She heats the dish over a brazier. The gold melts. At the edges, where the metal is thinnest, she sees it: a faint green tinge in the flame, exactly as Seydou described. She lets the metal cool and examines the solidified bead. The colour is slightly off -- paler than the deep yellow she has recorded in the trade descriptions she recites at court.

She weighs the bead against a known standard gold weight. The mass is correct. But the metal is not pure gold. She estimates -- based on the flame colour and the pallor -- that the adulteration is between fifteen and twenty-five percent copper. If this ratio applies to the full eight hundred measures reported for the season, then the actual gold content of the delivery is between six hundred and six hundred and eighty measures. The remaining hundred-plus measures are copper dust.

Aminata replaces the jar and thanks the treasury keeper. She returns to the audience hall and sits on the speaking mat and looks at the trade record she has been keeping for nineteen years. The numbers she recited at court three weeks ago were accurate as weights. They were false as gold.

**E4 (belief revision).** Ten days later, the outer courtyard of the palace. Aminata has sent a private messenger to the Bure goldfields requesting the presence of the overseer, a man named Moussa who manages the mining operations on behalf of the mansa. Moussa arrives after a week's travel. Aminata meets him in the courtyard, away from the court.

She tells him what she has found. She shows him the assay results. She asks him why the gold from Bure is adulterated with copper.

Moussa does not deny it. He sits on the ground and tells her that the richest veins at Bure began to thin three years ago. The surface deposits that had sustained the mines for generations are nearly gone. The deep digging yields less than half what the surface work once produced. The miners cannot meet the mansa's production quotas with pure gold. The caravan merchants, who are paid by volume delivered, began adding copper dust to maintain the weight targets. Moussa knew. The caravan leaders knew. The provincial chiefs along the route knew, because they take their cut from the caravans and their cut has been shrinking in real value even as the weight stays constant.

Aminata asks why no one informed the mansa.

Moussa says the mansa sets the production quota. The quota has not changed in twelve years. The men who fail to meet it are replaced. The men who meet it by adding copper keep their positions. Everyone along the chain has an interest in the numbers looking correct.

Aminata sits in the courtyard for a long time after Moussa leaves. The empire's gold trade -- the foundation of its wealth, its diplomacy, its standing among the Saharan trading nations -- is built on a resource that is running out. The trade records she has kept and recited with pride are a portrait of a system that has been slowly substituting appearance for substance. The caravans arrive full. The tally sticks match. The gold is not gold.

She returns to the audience hall and requests a private audience with the mansa. She brings the testing dish, the assay results, and Moussa's account. She does not bring the trade record. The trade record, as she now understands it, is the problem.
