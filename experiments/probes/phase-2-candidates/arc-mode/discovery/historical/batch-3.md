## Di-arc-Hi3-01

**Scenario.** Setting: 1870s London. A hospital physician has spent fifteen years treating cholera outbreaks in the East End using the miasma doctrine — the belief that cholera spreads through foul air from organic decay. When a new registrar at the local Board of Health begins mapping patient addresses against water-pump locations, the physician's account of why certain streets were spared in the most recent outbreak cannot survive the geographic comparison. The sequence is dialogue-driven.

> A = Dr. Aldous Fenn (attending physician, St. Gideon's Infirmary, Stepney); B = the proposition that cholera spreads through miasmatic air produced by organic decay in poor drainage conditions; E = a street-level mortality map produced by Registrar Clara Moss, showing that the most recent outbreak's mortality pattern correlates with proximity to a single pump court, not with the density of known organic decay sites; B′ = the proposition that cholera's transmission is waterborne and concentrated around specific water sources, not atmospheric and distributed by drainage quality

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** Dr. Fenn's consulting room, St. Gideon's Infirmary, a Tuesday morning in August. The Registrar for the Stepney district was making her quarterly call, and Fenn had the casualty lists from the summer's outbreak on his desk: forty-one deaths in six weeks, concentrated in the lanes off Corder Street and the yards behind the tannery.

"The common denominator is the drainage," Fenn told her. He pointed to his hand-drawn map, the one he updated at the start of every outbreak. The mortality sites were clustered where the street drainage was worst — the yards, the closes, the courts that the engineers had not yet touched. "Organic matter in standing water produces a poisonous vapour. The vapour travels with the prevailing air. The worst deaths are always where the air is worst."

The Registrar, a young woman named Clara Moss who had been in the post for three months, wrote something in her notebook. She did not say whether she agreed or disagreed. She asked whether she might have a copy of his mortality map. He gave her one.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Three weeks later, Fenn was called to testify before the parish vestry committee that was debating a proposal to fund new street drainage in the Corder Street area. He presented his mortality maps from the previous four outbreaks and argued that the correlation between poor drainage and cholera deaths was consistent, statistically robust, and actionable. The vestry approved the drainage proposal. Two of the committee members thanked him personally after the session. The proposal was reported in the *Stepney Gazette* as a victory for sanitary medicine.

Fenn clipped the report and kept it in his casebook. The drainage work was funded. The miasmatic account, as far as he could tell, had produced a public health intervention that would benefit the district.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** Clara Moss came to see him six weeks after the vestry hearing. She brought her own map — a larger format than his, with smaller markings, and a notation system he did not recognise.

"I've been placing the deaths from the August outbreak," she said. "May I show you something?"

She set the map on his desk next to his. Her mortality dots were in nearly the same positions as his. But she had also marked something else: the locations of the district's public pumps. There was a cluster of dots around one pump court in particular, in the middle of the Corder Street lanes, tighter and denser than the surrounding distribution.

"The drainage at that court is not the worst in the district," she said. "The courts at the end of Bray Lane have worse drainage and fewer deaths."

Fenn looked at the map. It was true. He had noted the Bray Lane courts as anomalous in his own data — they had poor drainage and a lower death rate than the model would predict. He had attributed this to irregular mortality reporting in that area, or to the possible effect of a recent lime-wash treatment on the worst walls.

"What is your explanation?" he asked.

"I don't have one yet," she said. "I'm still mapping."

He thanked her for the visit and told her the pump court correlation was interesting. He said he would keep it in mind. He did not change his map.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Dr. Fenn's consulting room, a month later. Moss came back with a completed map and a table. She set them both on his desk without preamble.

The map showed the full outbreak's mortality distribution against two overlays: one showing the organic-decay density as measured by the district's sanitation officers (blocked drainage, cesspits, middens), and one showing the radius around each public pump. The mortality pattern did not track the decay-density overlay. It tracked the pump radius. The highest-mortality zone was a hundred-yard radius around the Corder Street pump court. The lower-mortality zones, including Bray Lane with its bad drainage, were outside that radius.

The table showed mortality rates per ten houses by drainage quality and by pump proximity. Houses with poor drainage but not near the pump court: 3.1 deaths per ten houses. Houses with average drainage near the pump court: 9.4 deaths per ten houses.

"I spoke with three survivors in the pump court last week," Moss said. "All three said they had not been drinking from that pump. All three had been drawing water from the Aldgate cistern."

Fenn looked at the table for a long time.

"The Bray Lane houses," he said.

"Draw from the Aldgate cistern or from private wells," she said. "I checked."

He had been treating these streets for fifteen years. He had mapped four outbreaks. He had attributed the Bray Lane anomaly to reporting inconsistency and the lime-wash and the peculiarities of air movement in the lanes. The explanation that fit the data in front of him now was not any of those things. The miasma was not wrong as a description of the district's general conditions. It was wrong as the explanation for why these specific people had died.

"You've sent this to the Board of Health," he said.

"Not yet. I wanted to show you first."

He thought about the vestry hearing. The drainage proposal had been approved on the strength of his maps. The drainage work would help the district in general — Moss had not, he noted, claimed otherwise. But the specific account he had given for why people had died in the August outbreak was not supported by her data.

"Send it to the Board," he said. "And send me a copy of your methodology."

**E5 (consequences of revision).** The Board of Health offices, Whitechapel, two weeks later. Fenn had attended the presentation at his own request and had sat in the second row while Moss walked the Board's medical officer through the methodology and the findings. She had been careful and specific: she was not claiming to resolve the general question of cholera's mode of transmission, she said, only to document the geographic evidence for the August outbreak in the Stepney district.

The medical officer had asked Fenn, as the attending physician with the longest experience in the district, whether he found the analysis sound. Fenn said he found it sound. He said the pump-proximity correlation was stronger in Moss's data than the drainage-decay correlation he had been using, and that the survivor testimony about alternative water sources was corroborating evidence he had not previously collected. He said his own mortality maps had been constructed on a causal assumption he had not adequately tested against alternative explanations.

The medical officer asked what, concretely, should change. Fenn said the Corder Street pump should be examined for contamination and, if contaminated, closed. He said that drainage improvement remained worthwhile as a general sanitary measure, but the mortality reduction from the pump closure, if it came, would be a test of which account was actually correct. He said he was prepared to be tested.

The pump was examined the following week. The assay showed faecal contamination in the source well. The pump was closed.

## Di-arc-Hi3-02

**Scenario.** Setting: 1740s Venice. A senior inquisitor of the Council of Ten has operated for twenty years on the belief that a specific family — the Carnetti — have been systematically bribing the lesser magistrates of the Rialto to obtain favourable commercial rulings. An exhaustive audit of the family's accounts, ordered as part of a new investigation, produces a different picture than the inquisitor expected. The sequence is in free indirect style from the inquisitor's perspective. Non-linear.

> A = Inquisitor Giacomo Saredo; B = the proposition that the Carnetti family has been bribing Rialto magistrates to obtain favourable commercial rulings over a period of twenty years; E = a full audit of the Carnetti family accounts and the magistrates' personal ledgers, which shows no payment flows consistent with bribery and instead documents that the Carnetti consistently prevailed in Rialto proceedings because their advocates were better prepared than their opponents; B′ = the proposition that the Carnetti family's commercial advantages are the product of superior legal preparation, not of bribery

5 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5

**Note on beat order.** Opens in the present (E3, partial evidence), cuts back to establish the prior belief and its reinforcement, then returns to the decisive evidence and the aftermath.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence — depicted first).** The audit chamber, the Doge's Palace, a grey February morning. The Council's chief accountant had been working through the Carnetti ledgers for eleven days. He set his summary on Saredo's desk with the careful expression of a man who had found something he had not expected to find.

The summary covered twelve years of the Carnetti family's commercial accounts. The accountant noted that no payment had been identified that could not be traced to a documented commercial transaction or a personal expense with a clear social explanation — family events, household accounts, charitable donations consistent with a family of their wealth and standing. There were no irregular cash drawings at the periods that corresponded to disputed Rialto proceedings. There were no payments to intermediaries of the kind that typically appeared in bribery cases when the accounts were properly searched.

Saredo read the summary twice. He asked the accountant whether the ledgers could have been cleaned in advance of a possible audit. The accountant said they could not rule that out absolutely, but the ledgers showed no signs of retroactive alteration and the accounting method was consistent across twelve years. The summary did not say the Carnettis were innocent. It said there was no financial evidence in the accounts of the behaviour Saredo had been certain was there.

Saredo set the summary aside and asked the accountant to extend the review to the magistrates' own ledgers.

**E1 (prior belief established).** Twenty years earlier, Saredo's first posting as a junior inquisitor. The case that had introduced him to the Carnetti was a minor customs dispute in which a Carnetti merchant had appealed a dock tariff assessment and won before a panel that was known, in Council circles, to be accommodating to wealthy petitioners. The senior inquisitor who supervised Saredo had said, without elaborating, that the Carnettis were very good at winning cases they should have lost.

That remark had stayed with Saredo through eight subsequent Carnetti victories in commercial proceedings. Not all of them were cases where the outcome was surprising, but enough of them were that the accumulation felt meaningful. He had begun, over the years, to keep a private record: dates, proceedings, outcomes, presiding magistrates. He had fourteen magistrates' names in his record. He had been waiting for the right case to bring a formal investigation.

He had always believed the record was building toward something he would eventually be able to prove. The belief had been so long-standing that he had stopped examining it.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Four years before the audit, a timber-rights dispute in which the Carnettis had prevailed over a better-documented claim from a rival merchant family. The rival family had subsequently approached the Council with a petition alleging that the presiding magistrate had been paid to rule against them. The Council had investigated the magistrate and found no evidence of payment, but the investigation had confirmed, at least in Saredo's reading of it, that the magistrate's ruling had been legally questionable.

A legally questionable ruling, Saredo had noted in his private record, was the kind of ruling you obtained when you paid for it. He had been assigned as the investigating inquisitor and had spent six months attempting to establish a payment connection. He had not established it. He had closed the investigation with a finding of insufficient evidence.

He had not concluded from that outcome that the bribery had not occurred. He had concluded that the Carnettis were careful. The private record had continued to grow.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The magistrates' ledger review produced the same result as the Carnetti audit: no irregular payments, no unexplained income at the times of the disputed proceedings, no consistent intermediaries. The accountant's extended summary was delivered to Saredo three weeks after the first.

He sat with both summaries in front of him for a long time. Then he asked the court archive to provide him with the case records for the fourteen proceedings he had marked in his private record — not the financial documents, the legal documents. The petitions, the advocates' submissions, the presiding magistrates' written reasoning where it existed.

He read them over the following week. He had not read them before — he had been looking for financial evidence and had not thought the legal record was relevant.

The Carnetti advocates' submissions were substantially better than those of their opponents. In eleven of the fourteen cases, the Carnetti position had been better documented, more precisely argued, and more responsive to the applicable precedent. In three of the remaining cases the outcome had been genuinely close and could have gone either way; in none of them did the Carnetti submission appear weaker than the opposition's. The legally questionable ruling in the timber-rights dispute, when Saredo read the actual submissions for the first time, was less questionable than he had assumed. The rival family's claim had documentation problems he had not known about when he was investigating the magistrate.

The Carnettis did not bribe magistrates. They hired better advocates. The inference he had drawn twenty years ago from a senior inquisitor's throwaway remark, and had spent two decades treating as a working hypothesis, had not been tested against the legal record because he had been certain the answer was in the accounts.

**E5 (consequences of revision).** Saredo wrote his report to the Council over three days. He was methodical about it. He set out the investigation's scope, the audit findings, the absence of payment evidence in the Carnetti accounts and the magistrates' ledgers, and the contents of the legal submissions he had reviewed. He wrote that the Carnetti family's commercial success in Rialto proceedings was most consistently explained by the quality of their legal preparation. He wrote that the private record he had been keeping for twenty years had been constructed on an inference that the legal evidence did not support.

He recommended that the investigation be closed with a finding of no evidence of systematic bribery. He recommended that the magistrate he had investigated four years earlier be informed that the Council had found no basis for the earlier allegation. He did not recommend any action against the Carnetti family.

He also wrote, in a section he knew the Council would find uncomfortable, that the investigation had consumed considerable resources on the basis of an assumption that had never been formally tested against the primary evidence. He recommended that future investigations of this kind be required to examine both financial and legal documentation before framing a causal theory, rather than after it. The Council chair noted the recommendation in the margin. He did not commit to acting on it.

Saredo filed the report and closed the case. The private record he had been keeping for twenty years he burned in the hearing-room fireplace the same afternoon.

## Di-arc-Hi3-03

**Scenario.** Setting: 1590s Amsterdam. A merchant-investor in the early Dutch spice trade has allocated substantial capital to a route via the Cape of Good Hope on the belief that the Portuguese have a permanent monopoly on the direct eastern passage and that any Dutch attempt on it will be blocked by Portuguese naval force. When the first Dutch fleet returns from the Indies — battered, poorly provisioned, having lost two-thirds of its crew — the merchant's assumption about the monopoly proves to have been wrong in the way that matters most. The sequence is told in present tense.

> A = Pieter Hooge (merchant-investor, Amsterdam); B = the proposition that the Portuguese naval presence on the eastern route is strong enough to prevent any Dutch commercial venture from completing the passage; E = the return of Cornelis de Houtman's fleet after a twenty-seven-month voyage, having completed the eastern passage and returning with spices despite losing two-thirds of its crew — proving the route is navigable and the Portuguese presence is not an absolute barrier; B′ = the proposition that the eastern route is passable by a Dutch fleet, the Portuguese cannot prevent completion of the passage, and the barrier is navigational and logistical (not military) in character

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The Beurs, the Amsterdam exchange, autumn 1594. Pieter Hooge stands with the other principal investors and listens to the merchant Jan Huyghen van Linschoten, just returned from years of service in Portuguese India, describe the Cape route in terms of its practical hazards: storms at the southern passage, provisioning problems across the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese fortress at Cochin. Van Linschoten is arguing that a Dutch fleet can make the voyage. Most of the men around Pieter believe he is overconfident.

Pieter is among those who believe it. He has spoken to Dutch sea-captains who have tried the Atlantic passage under Spanish letters and come back short. He has read the reports — incomplete, second-hand, but consistent in one respect — that describe Portuguese patrol vessels on the eastern sea-lanes as numerous and aggressive. The Portuguese have held the eastern spice trade for a century. In Pieter's understanding, they hold it by force as much as by prior claim, and a Dutch fleet without naval escort is not a trading mission, it is a target.

He declines to join the first syndicate when Van Linschoten and the Amsterdam chamber organise it. He allocates his capital instead to the northern route project, which is attempting to find an Arctic passage the Portuguese do not control. He tells himself this is prudent diversification.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The northern fleet returns the following year having failed to find the passage. Pieter loses a portion of his stake. He does not revise his view of the southern route. The failure of the northern attempt confirms, in his mind, that the eastern approaches are difficult in both directions, and that the Portuguese hold on the Cape route is the more formidable obstacle. He hears that the first eastern syndicate is having difficulty recruiting capable pilots for the Cornelis de Houtman expedition that is being assembled. This too seems to him like confirmation: the enterprise is struggling to find experienced men because experienced men know what Portuguese patrol means for an unescorted Dutch fleet.

He is offered a small stake in the de Houtman expedition at a discounted rate by a merchant he respects. He declines again. The price of the stake is low enough that the risk seems to him like confirmation of the risk.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** The de Houtman fleet has been gone for more than two years with no word. This is not, in itself, alarming — eastern voyages are long — but the silence is longer than the most optimistic estimate. Pieter hears, through the exchange's informal news networks, that two ships may have been lost in a storm south of Madagascar. He hears that de Houtman has been taken prisoner by the Sultan of Bantam at some point in the voyage. He hears that de Houtman has been ransomed and the fleet is continuing. The information is contradictory and partial, as information from that distance always is.

What he does not hear, from anyone who has spoken to anyone who has reliable information, is that a Portuguese fleet intercepted and destroyed the expedition. He has been waiting for that report. It does not arrive.

He notes the absence but does not weight it heavily. Absence of a Portuguese interception report might simply mean the Portuguese chose not to publicise a successful interdiction. Or the fleet might still be at sea and the interception might yet come. The northern passage having failed, the eastern attempt having not yet returned, Pieter's overall view of the situation has not changed. The Portuguese hold the route.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered — belief revision).** The harbour at Amsterdam, August 1597. Three ships out of the original four enter the Texel roads on a morning Pieter is not at the harbour. He hears about it at the Beurs by noon. He reaches the harbour by mid-afternoon. The ships are visible from the quay, and the sight of them is itself the evidence: they exist, they are here, they have come from the east.

The ships are in poor condition. He can see that from the quay — patched canvas, short crew, the kind of silence on deck that speaks to exhaustion rather than arrival. The official count will not come for days, but the men who come down the gangway are not numerous. He speaks to a merchant who has already spoken to one of the pilots. Eighty-seven men returned out of two hundred and forty-nine who departed. The voyage was ruinous by any ordinary standard of commercial viability. De Houtman quarrelled with his officers. The provisioning was badly handled. The spice cargo barely covers costs.

And yet the spice cargo exists. The fleet completed the passage. The Portuguese did not stop them. Not because the Portuguese were not present on the sea-lanes — they were present, de Houtman encountered their vessels more than once — but because a Dutch fleet that was prepared to accept its losses and push through had been able to push through. The monopoly was not a naval blockade. It was a commercial and navigational advantage that a sufficiently determined competitor could contest.

Pieter stands at the quay for a long time. He thinks about the stake he declined at the discounted rate. He thinks about the argument he gave himself for declining it: that experienced men would not attempt the route because experienced men knew what Portuguese patrol meant. Experienced men had attempted the route. They had paid in blood and attrition. But the route was passable, and the Portuguese had not prevented it, and the men who had bought stakes in that first ruinous expedition at discounted rates were now holding proof of concept that every future expedition would rest on.

He has not lost money on the de Houtman expedition. He also has not made any. The men who risked their capital on the first attempt have something he does not: the thing that comes after proof of concept, which is first-mover position in the next syndicate. He leaves the harbour and goes back to the exchange to find out when the next expedition is being organised and at what terms.

## Di-arc-Hi3-04

**Scenario.** Setting: 1850s Edinburgh. A sheriff's principal clerk has managed the records of several high-profile property entailment cases and believes that a specific testamentary clause — used in multiple recent wills — is legally valid under Scots law as it stands, because it has been upheld by the Court of Session twice in the preceding decade. A retired advocate brings him a report of an English House of Lords decision that changes the legal landscape in ways the clerk has not accounted for. The sequence uses embedded document form and is non-linear.

> A = Principal Clerk James Craw; B = the proposition that the conditional entailment clause used in recent wills in the Edinburgh practice is legally valid and will withstand challenge under current Scots law; E = a report of a recent House of Lords decision on an analogous entailment clause in an English case, which the retired advocate argues establishes a principle that would render the Edinburgh clause invalid if applied in a Scottish appeal; B′ = the proposition that the conditional entailment clause is legally vulnerable to challenge on the basis of the House of Lords decision, and that several estates administered under that clause may face successful legal challenge

6 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6

**Note on beat order.** Opens in the present (E4, the moment the document is brought to Craw), cuts back to establish the prior belief and its reinforcement and the partial signal, then returns to the decisive evidence and aftermath.

**E4 (partial/ambiguous evidence — depicted first).** Craw's clerk's room off the High Street, a December morning, 1855. Retired advocate Walter Torrie sets a sheaf of papers on the desk without an appointment and says he has something Craw needs to read before the Forsyth matter goes back to the court in the new year.

The papers are a handwritten report of a House of Lords decision, *Langford v. Langford*, handed down at Westminster in October. Torrie has annotated it in the margins. He says the case involved an English conditional entailment clause with similar structure to the one being used in the Edinburgh practice, and that the Lords held the clause invalid on the ground that a condition in restraint of alienation, expressed in terms of a beneficiary's occupation rather than their lineage, was contrary to public policy. He says the Edinburgh clause uses occupation as a qualifying condition.

Craw reads the first page and says the decision is English and does not bind the Court of Session. Torrie says that is correct and also not the point. He says the Lords' reasoning on the public policy question might be adopted by a Scottish court on appeal, and that if it is, the Forsyth estate and at least four others administered under the same clause could face successful challenge.

Craw thanks Torrie for bringing the report and says he will review it carefully. Torrie says he thought he would say that and leaves.

Craw sets the report in the centre of his desk and does not immediately return to the Forsyth correspondence.

**E1 (prior belief established).** Three years earlier, the Arniston entailment case. The conditional clause had been drafted by Advocate-Depute Morrison, who had used a formulation he described as settled practice: the beneficiary's entitlement was conditional upon continued residence in a property of appropriate standing within the county and upon maintaining the occupation of a gentleman. The clause had been challenged by a nephew who claimed it was an unreasonable restraint on alienation. The Court of Session had upheld it.

Craw had managed the paperwork on the Arniston matter. He had read the judgment and had noted that the court found the occupational condition reasonable within the traditional framework of family entailment. He had filed the judgment. When Morrison used the same clause in subsequent wills, Craw had seen no reason for concern. A clause that had been upheld by the Court of Session was a clause that worked.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The Dalrymple matter, the following year. A different challenger, a similar clause, the same outcome: the occupational condition was upheld. The sheriff's own comment from the bench, recorded in the court's note, said the condition was consistent with established entailment practice and would not be lightly disturbed.

Craw had noted that comment in his clerk's record as confirmation of the clause's solidity. Two upheld challenges in three years was not a long run, but it was consistent. The clause was working.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence, retroactive).** Craw, alone in his room after Torrie leaves, opens the report again and looks at the annotated margins. He had, without registering it fully, read a note in the *Law Review* some months ago about the *Langford* case at the Lords. The note had been brief and had described the decision as potentially significant for conditional entailment drafting. He had filed the note without acting on it, partly because the note had not specified any connection to Scots law practice and partly because he had the two upheld challenges in his head as the relevant precedent.

He finds the *Law Review* note in his correspondence file. He reads it alongside Torrie's report. The note's author had flagged the public policy reasoning but had not pursued the Scots law implications. Torrie has pursued them. Craw reads the two documents together and the concern he had not formed when he read the note in isolation becomes harder to suppress.

**E5 (contradicting evidence encountered — embedded document).** The decisive section of Torrie's margin annotation, transcribed by Craw into his own working notes that evening:

---

*Lord Chancellor, delivering the judgment in Langford v. Langford (1855): "A condition upon an alienable estate which requires the holder to maintain a specified occupational status as a condition of continued possession is distinguishable in principle from a condition requiring lineal descent or religious observance of an established kind. The former class of condition operates to remove from the holder the practical freedom of disposition which is the essential character of an alienable estate, imposing not a qualification of the holder's person but a perpetual constraint upon his commercial choices. Such a condition is contrary to the public policy which this Court has consistently held to favour the free movement of property through commerce and inheritance. We hold the condition void for uncertainty and contrary to public policy."*

*Torrie's margin note: "The Edinburgh clause ('in such occupation as becomes a gentleman of established station') is formally distinct from the English clause ('engaged in no trade or profession') but both impose an occupational qualification on an alienable estate. A Scottish court following the Lords' public policy reasoning would not distinguish them on this basis. The Forsyth matter goes to appeal in January. If the appellant raises Langford, Morrison's drafting will be tested on precisely this ground."*

---

Craw reads his own transcription. The Court of Session had upheld the clause twice. But neither challenge had raised the public policy argument in the terms the Lords had used in *Langford*. The prior upheld challenges were precedents for a question that had not yet been argued the way it could now be argued. The clause had not been tested against the public policy reasoning that the Lords had applied. The two Court of Session decisions were not wrong — they had decided the questions that were put to them — but they were no longer the full picture of the legal landscape.

He notes in his working record: *Conditional occupational clause: previously held valid on prior challenge, neither of which engaged public policy reasoning in terms of Langford. Status of clause on public policy ground is now genuinely uncertain. Forsyth appeal requires examination of this issue before January.*

He writes to Morrison that evening.

**E6 (consequences of revision — confrontation scene).** Morrison's chambers, two days later. Morrison has read the report and the annotation. He says the Lords' reasoning is obiter in the Scottish context and that the Court of Session is under no obligation to follow it.

"That is accurate," Craw says. "The Court of Session is not bound. The question is whether a Scottish advocate, appearing on appeal in the Forsyth matter, would be competent to raise the public policy argument in terms of Langford, and whether the Court would feel compelled to address it."

Morrison says a competent Scottish advocate appearing for the challenger might raise any number of grounds, and that the Forsyth challenge has been narrowly pleaded.

"The challenger's advocate can amend his pleadings before the hearing," Craw says. "I need you to tell me whether the Forsyth family has been made aware that the clause under which they are administering the estate is now under a legal cloud that was not present when you drafted it."

Morrison says the clause was good law when he drafted it and the two Court of Session decisions confirm it.

"They confirm it against the challenges that were raised," Craw says. "They do not confirm it against the argument Torrie has outlined. I am asking you whether the Forsyth family knows the difference. If they do not, and the appeal fails on a ground that could have been anticipated, that is a matter I will need to record."

Morrison is silent for a moment. Then he says he will write to the Forsyth family's principal to outline the *Langford* decision and its potential relevance. He says he will do so before the end of the week.

Craw accepts this. Resolution: one party prevails — Craw's demand that the Forsyth family be informed is accepted. Morrison will act.

Craw notes the agreement in his working record and returns to the Forsyth correspondence.
