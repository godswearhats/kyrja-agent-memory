# Discovery arc x Historical -- Batch 2 (Tier B)

## Di-arc-Hi2-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style (1780s France)**

**Scenario.** A provincial intendant in pre-revolutionary France believes that the persistent grain shortages in his district are caused by poor harvests and peasant hoarding. Over the course of an inspection tour, warehouse records, and a confrontation with a local merchant syndicate, he comes to accept that the shortages are manufactured by a network of grain merchants who buy at regulated prices, store in concealed warehouses, and resell across the district boundary at market rates -- a scheme enabled by his own deputy's participation in the syndicate.

> A = Jean-Luc Morel (intendant of the district of Auxerre); B = the grain shortages in the district are caused by consecutive poor harvests and by peasant hoarding of reserves; E = warehouse records showing grain purchased at regulated prices and stored rather than distributed, testimony from a village syndic, and the deputy intendant's financial interest in the merchant syndicate; B' = the shortages are artificially created by a merchant syndicate that exploits the regulated-price system to buy cheaply and resell across the boundary at market rates, with the deputy intendant's complicity.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The intendant's office in Auxerre, a morning in late October 1786. Morel sat at his desk with the quarterly grain report open before him. The numbers were bad. The district's grain reserves stood at less than a third of what they had been at the same point the previous year. The harvest returns from the subdelegates were uniform: yields down, weather blamed, peasants reported to be withholding stocks.

Morel had been intendant for six years. He had seen bad harvests before -- the cycle was familiar, the responses were rehearsed. He dictated a letter to the Controller-General in Paris reporting the shortfall and attributing it to poor yields compounded by the habitual peasant practice of hiding grain against future scarcity. He recommended an increase in the regulated price ceiling to incentivise sales and requested the standard emergency allocation from the royal granaries.

His deputy, Etienne Valmier, reviewed the letter before dispatch and suggested adding a note about the unusual persistence of the shortages -- three consecutive years now, each worse than the last. Morel agreed and added the note. He did not find the persistence surprising. Bad weather ran in cycles. The peasants, when frightened, hid their grain, which deepened the shortage, which frightened them further. It was a self-reinforcing pattern, and Morel believed he understood its mechanism completely.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The village of Cravant, two weeks later. Morel was conducting his autumn inspection tour of the district -- a routine he performed every year, visiting five or six villages to assess conditions and hear complaints. In Cravant, the village syndic met him in the square and walked him through the granary. The bins were low. The syndic reported that the harvest had been below average and that several families had not brought their full allotments to the communal store.

Morel noted this as confirmation. He asked the syndic whether the families were hoarding. The syndic hesitated, then said it was possible -- the fear was widespread, and when people feared hunger they held back whatever they could. Morel instructed the syndic to conduct a house-by-house inventory and report any concealed stocks. He moved on to the next village.

At each stop, the report was the same: low reserves, poor harvest, suspected hoarding. The uniformity reassured him. The pattern was consistent with what he already believed. He wrote to Valmier from the road, confirming that the inspection supported the quarterly report and that no additional measures were warranted beyond the emergency allocation already requested.

**E3 (partial evidence encountered).** The village of Irancy, the fourth stop on the inspection tour. Morel arrived expecting the same report. Instead, the syndic -- an older man named Gervais who had served in the role for twenty years -- asked to speak with the intendant privately.

Gervais closed the door of the granary office and said that the shortage in Irancy was not caused by the harvest. The harvest, he said, had been average. Not abundant, but average -- enough to fill the communal bins and leave surplus for the market. But the surplus had been purchased within days of the harvest by agents of a merchant named Delacroix, who operated out of Sens, on the other side of the district boundary. The agents had paid the regulated price -- the low, controlled price set by the intendant's office to ensure affordability -- and carted the grain east. Gervais had watched three full wagons leave the village the week after harvest.

Morel asked why Gervais had not reported this to the subdelegate. Gervais said he had. Twice. The subdelegate had told him the purchases were lawful -- the regulated price applied to all buyers, and cross-boundary trade was permitted. Gervais said that was true in law but absurd in practice: Delacroix's agents were buying at the regulated price in Auxerre and selling at the unregulated market price in Sens, where the shortage had driven prices to three times the Auxerre ceiling. The entire district's surplus was flowing east, purchased cheaply under the regulated system and sold dear across the line.

Morel did not dismiss this outright. But he noted that one village syndic's account was not sufficient to overturn the pattern he had observed across the district. Peasant hoarding was documented. The harvest returns were consistent. Gervais might be describing a local irregularity, not a systemic cause. He thanked the syndic, noted the claim in his inspection book, and returned to the road.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The warehouse district on the eastern road outside Auxerre, three days later. Morel had not been able to set Gervais's account aside. The specificity of it -- the merchant's name, the number of wagons, the regulated-price mechanism -- was not the kind of detail a village syndic invented. He had asked Valmier to prepare a summary of grain purchases in the district for the past two years, sorted by buyer. Valmier had said the records would take several days to compile.

Morel did not wait. He rode to the warehouse district himself, accompanied by a single clerk. The district's grain-trade records were held at the weighing house, maintained by the market inspector. Morel presented his authority and asked to see the purchase ledgers.

The ledgers told a clear story. A consortium of four merchants -- Delacroix among them -- had purchased over sixty percent of the district's marketable grain surplus in each of the past three years. The purchases were made at the regulated price, lawfully, through the standard channels. But the quantities were enormous, and the delivery destinations listed in the ledgers were not within the district. They were across the boundary, in districts where no price regulation applied.

Morel checked the storage records next. Three warehouses on the eastern road were leased to the consortium. The inspector's quarterly stock reports showed them as empty -- inspected and cleared. But Morel asked the clerk to pull the lease contracts, and the contracts listed warehouse capacities that exceeded what the stock reports described. The reports covered the main floors. The contracts described lower levels -- basement storage that the quarterly inspections had never entered.

Morel asked the market inspector to open the lower levels. The inspector said he had no key. Morel said the intendant's authority superseded the lease. The inspector produced a key.

The basements contained grain. Hundreds of sacks, stamped with the district's regulated-market seal, stacked floor to ceiling. Morel's clerk counted one hundred and forty sacks in the first basement alone. The grain had been purchased at regulated prices, stored beneath the inspection level, and held for transport across the boundary to markets where it would sell at three or four times the price.

Morel stood in the basement among the grain sacks and understood that the shortages he had attributed to harvest failure and peasant hoarding had been manufactured. The harvests had been adequate. The peasants were not hoarding. The grain was here -- purchased legally, hidden from inspection, and held for export by a merchant syndicate that exploited the regulated-price system as a purchasing subsidy.

He sealed the warehouses under his authority and rode back to Auxerre.

**E5 (belief revision -- consequences).** The intendant's office, the following morning. Morel had summoned Valmier and the market inspector. He had spent the previous evening reviewing the warehouse lease contracts. The leaseholder for all three warehouses was a company registered in Sens. The company's registration documents, which Morel had obtained from the provincial court's records, listed four partners. Three were the merchants from the consortium. The fourth was Etienne Valmier.

Morel laid the documents on his desk when Valmier arrived. He did not present them immediately. He asked Valmier to explain why the quarterly grain-trade summary he had requested three days earlier had not been delivered.

Valmier said the records were still being compiled.

Morel said the records were already compiled. He had seen them at the weighing house. He asked Valmier to explain his financial interest in a company that had purchased sixty percent of the district's grain surplus at regulated prices and stored it in concealed warehouse basements for cross-boundary resale.

Valmier did not deny the investment. He said the trade was lawful. Cross-boundary commerce was permitted, and the regulated price applied equally to all buyers. He said the consortium provided a market for surplus grain that would otherwise rot in village granaries.

Morel said the surplus was not rotting. It was being extracted from a district whose people were going hungry, purchased at an artificially low price that the intendant's office set to protect those same people, and resold at market rates that the regulated system was designed to prevent. He said Valmier's position as deputy intendant -- the officer responsible for compiling the grain-trade records that would have exposed the scheme -- made his participation not merely a commercial interest but a breach of the public trust.

Valmier said the intendant was confusing commerce with conspiracy. He said he would challenge any administrative sanction.

Morel told him the challenge was his right. He suspended Valmier from his duties, ordered the market inspector to inventory all three warehouses under armed guard, and began drafting a report to the Controller-General that reversed every conclusion in his October letter. The district's grain shortage was not natural. It was the product of a systematic extraction scheme enabled by the regulatory structure his own office administered, and by the officer he had trusted to administer it honestly.

## Di-arc-Hi2-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven (1850s United States)**

**Scenario.** A Quaker schoolteacher in southeastern Pennsylvania believes that the local railroad company's new line through the township is a straightforward commercial venture that will bring prosperity. A conversation with a displaced farmer, the company's own land-acquisition filings, and a township meeting establish that the railroad used fraudulent survey stakes to condemn properties at below-market prices, and that the route was chosen not for commercial efficiency but to run through the farms of families who lacked the means to contest the condemnation in court.

> A = Ruth Hadley (schoolteacher); B = the railroad's route through the township was chosen for commercial efficiency and the land acquisitions were conducted lawfully under eminent domain; E = a displaced farmer's account of moved survey stakes, the company's land-acquisition filings showing systematic below-market valuations, and testimony at a township meeting confirming the route targeted economically vulnerable families; B' = the railroad deliberately chose a route through properties owned by families too poor to contest condemnation, used fraudulent survey methods to justify the route, and acquired land at prices that reflected the fraud rather than the market.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The schoolhouse on Chadds Ford Road, a Friday afternoon in September 1854. Ruth Hadley was cleaning the slates when Thomas Briggs, the township supervisor, came in. He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands and the look of a man who wanted to be congratulated.

"The railroad's coming through," he said.

"I heard." Ruth set down the cloth. "The children have been talking about it for weeks. Jesse Carpenter says he's going to count the cars."

"It's good for the township," Briggs said. "The line runs through the eastern corridor -- the Haverford company surveyed it last spring. Most efficient route between the junction and the main line. They'll need to acquire some properties along the right-of-way, but that's eminent domain. Fair price, public benefit. Everyone gains."

"Which properties?" Ruth asked.

"The Brinton farm, the Logans' place, the Hendricks parcel, and a few others along the creek bottom. Small holdings, mostly. The company's offered fair valuations."

Ruth nodded. She knew the families. They were her students' families -- tenant farmers, small freeholders, people who worked the land because they had no other trade. She did not question the fairness of the valuations. Eminent domain was law, and the law required fair compensation. Thomas Briggs was an honest man, and the Haverford railroad was a reputable company. She believed the process was working as it was designed to work.

**E2 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The lane behind the schoolhouse, three weeks later. Ruth was walking home when she saw Abner Logan sitting on the stone wall at the edge of his property -- or what had been his property. The condemnation order had been filed the previous week. The house was still standing, but the family had been told to vacate by the first of November.

Ruth stopped. Abner was a quiet man, not given to complaint. But he was sitting on the wall in the middle of a workday, which was not like him.

"Abner," she said. "How is Sarah?"

"Sarah's at her sister's," he said. "She won't come back to the house. Says she can't watch them stake it."

Ruth sat on the wall beside him. "The valuation was fair?" she asked.

Abner looked at her. "The valuation was four hundred dollars for sixty-two acres. I paid seven hundred for it nine years ago, and I've put a barn on it since. The county assessor has it at eight hundred and fifty."

"Then you contest the valuation," Ruth said. "That's your right under the law."

"I went to the courthouse," Abner said. "The clerk told me I'd need a lawyer and a surveyor to challenge the company's survey. The lawyer wants fifty dollars. The surveyor wants thirty. I don't have eighty dollars, Ruth. That's why they're taking my land and not Josiah Pennock's land next to it. Pennock's got twice the acreage and he's right on the route the surveyors drew. But Pennock's got a lawyer on retainer and money to fight. So the survey goes around his property and through mine."

Ruth said nothing for a moment. "You're saying the route was changed to avoid Pennock's land?"

"I'm saying the survey stakes moved. I saw the original stakes in March. They ran straight through Pennock's east field. By June, they'd been moved a hundred yards south, through my creek bottom. My neighbour Martin Hendricks saw the same thing -- his stakes moved too. The line bends around every property with money and runs through every property without it."

Ruth walked home slowly. She had not expected this. She had expected the law to work.

**E3 (secondary confirming evidence).** The Chadds Ford courthouse, the following Monday. Ruth went during the midday break, leaving the older students in charge of the younger ones. She told herself she was simply verifying Abner's claim -- one man's account did not establish a pattern.

She asked the clerk to see the Haverford railroad's land-acquisition filings for the township. The clerk brought her a ledger. Ruth sat at the reading table and went through it.

There were eleven condemnation filings along the right-of-way. She copied the property descriptions, the assessed values from the county rolls, and the railroad's offered valuations into her notebook. Then she compared them.

Every property owned by a family with known financial means -- Pennock, the Worthingtons, the Chandler estate -- had been routed around. The survey line bent, jogged, and curved to avoid their boundaries. Every property owned by a tenant farmer or small freeholder with no legal representation -- the Logans, the Hendricks family, the Brintons, the Carpenters -- was routed through. And every offered valuation was below the county assessment. Not slightly below. Forty to sixty percent below.

Ruth copied the numbers into her notebook and returned to the schoolhouse. She had come to verify one man's claim. She had found eleven filings that confirmed it.

**E4 (belief revision -- consequences).** The township meeting house, the following Thursday evening. The monthly township meeting. Briggs presiding. Ruth sat in the third row, which was unusual -- she normally sat at the back.

Briggs opened with the railroad update. He described the progress of the land acquisitions and noted that the company expected to begin grading by spring. He said the process was proceeding smoothly and lawfully.

Ruth stood. "Thomas, I have a question."

Briggs nodded.

"I've reviewed the land-acquisition filings at the courthouse. The railroad's survey route bends around every substantial property along the corridor and runs through every small holding. The offered valuations are forty to sixty percent below the county assessments. Every affected family is one that lacks the means to retain a lawyer. Can thee explain why the route follows that pattern?"

The room was quiet. Briggs said the survey was conducted by the railroad's engineers and that the route reflected commercial and topographical considerations.

"The topography doesn't change between Pennock's east field and Abner Logan's creek bottom," Ruth said. "They're adjacent parcels on the same grade. The original survey stakes ran through Pennock's land. They were moved. Abner Logan saw them move. Martin Hendricks saw them move. The route was redrawn to avoid property owners who could afford to fight and to target those who could not."

Briggs said the township did not have authority to review the railroad's survey methodology. That was a matter for the county court.

"Then the township should petition the county court," Ruth said. "I have the filings, the assessed values, and the offered valuations. The pattern is in the numbers. This is not eminent domain applied fairly. This is a company selecting its victims by their poverty."

Martin Hendricks stood in the back row. "She's right. My stakes moved. I saw the original line and I saw the new one. The surveyor came through in June and drove new stakes a hundred yards north of where the March stakes stood. When I asked him about it, he said the March stakes were preliminary. But I watched him pull the old ones out of the ground."

Briggs looked at the room. Three more families spoke. Each described the same pattern: stakes moved, valuations below assessment, no resources to contest.

Ruth asked Briggs to call a vote on whether to petition the county court for a review of the survey and the valuations. Briggs said he would take the motion under advisement. Ruth said she was not asking for advisement, she was asking for a vote, and the township charter required the supervisor to put a motion to a vote when seconded from the floor. Hendricks seconded. Briggs called the vote. It passed, fourteen to three.

Ruth sat down. She had walked into the meeting believing the law worked and the railroad was honest. She had walked into the meeting expecting to be wrong about Abner Logan's claim. She was not wrong. The railroad had chosen its route by the wealth of the landowners, not the efficiency of the line, and the law had not prevented it because the people the law was supposed to protect could not afford to invoke it.

## Di-arc-Hi2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E4 -> E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E5 -> E6, embedded document voice (1610s Japan)**

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The events' chronological order is: E1 (prior belief, spring) -- E2 (belief reinforced, summer) -- E3 (ambiguous signal, early autumn) -- E4 (primary evidence, late autumn) -- E5 (confirming evidence, following week) -- E6 (belief revision, following week). The depicted order opens with the primary evidence, then loops back to the belief's formation before moving forward.

**Scenario.** A senior retainer to a daimyo in the Tokugawa period believes that his lord's new alliance with a neighbouring domain is a defensive pact against a mutual threat. The text of the alliance treaty, intelligence from the retainer's own border agents, and testimony from a captured messenger establish that the alliance is actually an offensive agreement to partition and annex the territory of a third domain -- the retainer's wife's family's domain -- and that the retainer's lord has concealed the true terms because the retainer's family connection would make him oppose the plan.

> A = Ishikawa Gennosuke (senior retainer); B = the alliance with the Mori domain is a defensive pact against the Uesugi threat on the northern border; E = the full text of the alliance treaty specifying offensive annexation of the Takeda domain (the retainer's wife's family), intelligence reports showing troop staging inconsistent with defensive positioning, and a captured messenger's testimony confirming the invasion timeline; B' = the alliance is an offensive agreement to partition and annex the Takeda domain, concealed from the retainer because his marriage tie would make him a source of resistance or warning.

**E4 (primary evidence -- embedded document).** The study of Ishikawa Gennosuke's quarters, a night in late autumn. The retainer sat at his writing desk with a document he should not have possessed. His lord's seal-bearer, a man named Fujita, had brought it an hour earlier -- not on orders, but because Fujita owed Gennosuke a debt of twenty years' standing and because the document's contents, Fujita said, concerned Gennosuke's family directly.

The document was a copy of the alliance treaty between Gennosuke's lord, Matsudaira Nobuyasu, and the neighbouring Mori domain. Gennosuke had seen a version of this treaty in the spring -- a summary prepared by the domain's chief councillor, presented at the senior retainers' assembly, describing a defensive alliance against the Uesugi threat on the northern border. He had approved it. Every senior retainer had approved it. The Uesugi were aggressive, the northern border was exposed, and a mutual-defence pact with the Mori was sound strategy.

The document in front of him was not the summary. It was the full treaty, and it bore both lords' seals. Gennosuke read it by candlelight:

> *Article the Third. The Matsudaira and Mori domains, recognising the strategic necessity of controlling the Takeda corridor between their territories, agree to the following: the Mori domain shall advance from the west along the Kai road; the Matsudaira domain shall advance from the east along the Suwa road; the Takeda domain's territory shall be partitioned along the ridgeline of Mount Yatsu, the western half to the Mori, the eastern half to the Matsudaira. The campaign is to commence before the first snow. Takeda retainers who submit will be absorbed; those who resist will be dispossessed.*

> *Article the Fourth. The existence and terms of Articles Three through Seven are restricted to the signatories and their designated campaign commanders. No senior retainer whose family holds ties to the Takeda domain is to be informed until the campaign is underway. The defensive-alliance summary circulated at retainer assemblies shall remain the operative description for all non-designated personnel.*

Gennosuke read Article the Fourth twice. His wife was born Takeda Sayo, daughter of a Takeda senior retainer. Article Four named him without naming him -- there was only one retainer in the Matsudaira domain whose family held Takeda ties. The alliance he had approved as a defensive pact was an invasion plan targeting his wife's family, and he had been excluded from the true terms because his lord expected him to either resist or warn the Takeda.

He set the document on the desk and did not move for some time.

**E1 (prior belief established -- spring).** Six months earlier, the great hall of Matsudaira castle. The cherry blossoms were late that year, and the assembly had been delayed twice for the lord's illness. When it convened, the chief councillor presented the alliance proposal: a mutual-defence agreement with the Mori domain, aimed at containing the Uesugi threat on the northern frontier. The terms, as summarised, were straightforward -- shared intelligence, coordinated border patrols, mutual obligation to commit forces in the event of a Uesugi attack.

Gennosuke had studied the summary the night before. The logic was sound. The Uesugi had raided three border villages the previous year, and the Matsudaira domain lacked the strength to hold the northern line alone. The Mori were natural partners -- their territory bordered the same frontier, and their lord had his own grievances against the Uesugi.

Gennosuke spoke in favour of the alliance. He noted that the terms were proportionate, that the intelligence-sharing clause had appropriate restrictions, and that the Mori's military reputation made them a credible partner. The assembly voted to approve. The treaty was signed the following month.

Gennosuke wrote to his wife's father in the Takeda domain that spring, mentioning the new alliance in passing. A defensive arrangement, he wrote. Nothing that concerns your house.

**E2 (belief reinforced -- summer).** The northern border, midsummer. Gennosuke had been assigned to inspect the border fortifications -- routine work following the alliance's ratification. He rode the line with a company of twenty men and found the positions strengthened: new watchposts, reinforced palisades, additional stores of arrows and rice. The Mori had matched the investment on their side of the frontier -- Gennosuke could see their new positions from the ridgeline.

He reported to the chief councillor on his return: the border was in good order, the alliance was functioning, and the Uesugi would find the frontier harder to breach than the previous year. The chief councillor thanked him and said the lord was pleased.

Gennosuke noted in his journal that the alliance appeared to be exactly what it claimed to be. The Mori were investing in defence. The border was strengthening. He had no reason to question the arrangement.

**E3 (ambiguous signal -- early autumn).** Gennosuke's quarters, an evening in early autumn. His wife, Sayo, had received a letter from her father in the Takeda domain. She read it aloud to Gennosuke over the evening meal.

Her father wrote that the Takeda border agents had observed unusual activity along the Mori-Takeda boundary -- troop movements, supply wagons, construction of what appeared to be a forward staging camp. The Takeda lord had sent an inquiry to the Mori, asking whether the activity was related to the Uesugi threat. The Mori had replied that it was -- precautionary repositioning in anticipation of an autumn Uesugi campaign.

Sayo's father accepted the explanation but noted that the staging camp's location was peculiar. It was on the Mori's southern border -- the border with the Takeda, not the border with the Uesugi. If the camp was meant for a northern campaign, it was facing the wrong direction.

Gennosuke considered this. He said the Mori might be staging in the south for logistical reasons -- supply lines, terrain, road access. Military positioning did not always point toward the enemy. Sayo's father was a cautious man, and cautious men saw threats where logistics explained the pattern.

He did not raise the matter with the chief councillor. He did not visit the Mori-Takeda border. He set the letter aside and returned to his own duties.

**E5 (confirming evidence -- following week).** The guardhouse at the eastern road gate, one week after Gennosuke read the treaty. He had summoned his two most trusted border agents -- men who reported to him directly, outside the chief councillor's intelligence chain -- and asked them to gather reports on Matsudaira troop movements along the eastern boundary, specifically along the Suwa road.

The agents returned within four days. Their report confirmed what the treaty described: three companies of Matsudaira soldiers had been quietly repositioned from the northern border to staging positions along the Suwa road, the road that led directly into the Takeda corridor. Supply caches had been established at two points along the route. The movements had been conducted at night, under orders sealed by the chief councillor.

The troop positions were not defensive. They were not oriented toward the Uesugi frontier. They were staged for an advance into Takeda territory from the east -- exactly as Article Three of the treaty specified.

Gennosuke dismissed the agents, locked the report in his study, and sat with the treaty and the intelligence side by side. The defensive alliance was an invasion plan. The border fortifications he had inspected in the summer were real -- but they were a screen, a visible display of defensive intent that concealed the true purpose. His lord had shown him the north so he would not look south.

**E6 (belief revision -- consequences).** Gennosuke's study, two days later. He had not slept well. He had spent the intervening time considering his position: a senior retainer of the Matsudaira, married to a Takeda woman, holding intelligence that his lord intended to invade his wife's family's domain. The treaty's Article Four had been written to prevent exactly this moment -- to keep him ignorant until the campaign was underway, when warning would be too late.

He knelt at his desk and wrote two letters. The first was addressed to his lord. He wrote that he had seen the full treaty. He did not name his source. He wrote that he could not participate in a campaign against the Takeda domain, that his family obligation forbade it, and that he was requesting formal release from service. He set the letter aside to be delivered at morning audience.

The second letter was addressed to his wife's father. He sat with the brush above the paper for a long time. To send this letter was an act that his lord would consider a betrayal -- warning the enemy of an invasion. To withhold it was to allow his wife's family to be destroyed without notice.

He wrote the letter. He described the treaty's true terms, the troop positions, and the invasion timeline. He sealed it and gave it to Sayo, who understood without being told what the letter meant for their position in the Matsudaira domain.

Gennosuke did not know whether the warning would arrive in time, or whether it would change anything. He knew that by morning, when his lord received the first letter, he and Sayo would have to leave. He packed his household through the night. They were gone before dawn.

## Di-arc-Hi2-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense (1490s Florence)**

**Scenario.** A Florentine apothecary believes that his guild's monopoly on pharmaceutical compounds is protected by the city's magistrates because the guild's products are superior to those of unlicensed practitioners. A series of patient outcomes, a magistrate's offhand remark, and the guild's own ledgers force him to accept that the monopoly is maintained not by product quality but by a system of payments from the guild to the magistrates, and that the guild's compounds are no more effective than the cheaper alternatives the monopoly suppresses.

> A = Matteo Corvini (apothecary, guild member); B = the guild's pharmaceutical monopoly is maintained because its compounds are demonstrably superior, and the magistrates enforce the monopoly to protect public health; E = identical patient outcomes from guild and non-guild compounds, a magistrate's admission of the payment arrangement, and the guild's own ledgers showing annual disbursements to the magistracy labelled as "regulatory assurance"; B' = the monopoly is maintained by payments to the magistrates, not by product superiority, and the guild's compounds offer no advantage over the unlicensed alternatives they suppress.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The apothecary shop on the Via dei Calzaiuoli, a morning in March. Matteo Corvini stands behind his counter grinding cinchona bark into the compound the guild prescribes for marsh-fever. The shop smells of camphor and dried rosemary. A customer waits at the counter -- a servant from one of the minor houses, sent with a prescription from the household physician.

Matteo fills the prescription, wraps it in waxed cloth, and explains the dosage. The servant asks why the guild's compound costs three times what the herbalist on the Ponte Vecchio charges for the same treatment.

"It is not the same treatment," Matteo says. "The guild's compounds are prepared under the guild's standards -- measured, tested, certified. The herbalist's preparations are unregulated. You do not know what is in them. You do not know the dosage. The magistrates enforce the guild's monopoly because our compounds are reliable and the alternatives are not."

The servant pays and leaves. Matteo returns to his mortar. He has given this answer a hundred times. He believes it the way he believes in the weight of the pestle in his hand -- as a fact so fundamental it does not require examination. The guild exists because its products are superior. The magistrates enforce the monopoly because the public benefits. This is what the guild teaches, what the guild master repeats at every quarterly assembly, and what Matteo has observed for the twelve years he has held his shop.

**E2 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, six weeks later. Matteo has been asked by the guild to consult on a case -- a merchant who contracted marsh-fever on the road from Rome. The hospital's physician has been treating the merchant with the guild's standard cinchona compound for two weeks. The fever has not broken.

Matteo reviews the treatment log and confirms the dosage is correct. He examines the patient and finds nothing unusual -- the fever is persistent but not atypical. He recommends continuing the compound and adjusting the schedule.

As he leaves the ward, the physician walks with him. They pass through a second ward -- a charity ward, where patients without means are treated. The physician mentions, without apparent concern, that three marsh-fever patients in the charity ward recovered in the past week. Matteo asks which compound was used. The physician says the charity ward cannot afford the guild's compound. They use a cinchona preparation made by an unlicensed herbalist who supplies the hospital at a quarter of the guild's price.

Matteo stops walking. "And the outcomes?"

The physician shrugs. "The same, in my experience. The charity patients recover at the same rate as the paying patients. Sometimes faster. I have kept the treatment logs for both wards for three years. There is no difference in recovery time, complication rate, or mortality."

Matteo asks to see the logs. The physician brings them. Matteo sits at a table in the hospital's corridor and reads through three years of marsh-fever treatment records, one ward beside the other. The physician is correct. The outcomes are indistinguishable. The guild's compound, at three times the price, produces the same results as the unlicensed herbalist's preparation.

Matteo copies the relevant figures into his own notebook, thanks the physician, and walks back to his shop. He does not open the shop that afternoon. He sits in the back room with the numbers and tries to account for them. He cannot.

**E3 (secondary confirming evidence).** A banquet at the Palazzo della Signoria, the following week. Matteo attends as a guild representative -- a minor honour, rotated among the guild's senior members. He sits at the lower end of a long table, beside a magistrate named Salviati, who administers the city's trade-licensing court.

The conversation turns to the guild system. Salviati, who has had more wine than the hour warrants, speaks freely. He complains about the burden of enforcement -- the unlicensed herbalists, the complaints from the poorer quarters, the petitions to lower pharmaceutical prices. He says the guild's regulatory fees make the enforcement worthwhile but that the complaints are growing louder.

"Regulatory fees," Matteo says.

"The guild's annual contribution to the magistracy," Salviati says. "For maintaining the licensing regime. Your guild master knows the arrangement. It has been in place since before I took the bench. Six hundred florins a year, paid quarterly, entered in the guild's books as 'regulatory assurance.' Without it, the magistracy would have no budget to enforce the monopoly, and frankly, no particular reason to."

He says this casually, as a statement of administrative fact, and turns to the dish in front of him.

Matteo does not ask a follow-up question. He does not need to. The magistrate has described the monopoly's enforcement mechanism as a payment -- not as a quality standard, not as a public-health measure, but as a financial arrangement. The guild pays the magistrates to enforce the monopoly. The magistrates enforce it because they are paid. The quality of the compound is not part of the equation.

**E4 (belief revision).** The guild hall, three days later. Matteo arrives early for the quarterly assembly. The hall is empty except for the guild clerk, who is preparing the ledgers for the guild master's report. Matteo asks the clerk if he can review the disbursement ledger -- a routine request for a senior guild member auditing the quarterly accounts.

The clerk brings the ledger. Matteo turns to the disbursement section. Near the back, under a heading labelled "Regulatory Assurance," he finds the entries: six hundred florins per year, paid in quarterly instalments of one hundred and fifty, disbursed to the Magistracy of Trade Licensing. The entries extend backward for as far as the ledger covers -- eleven years. Each payment is annotated with the same notation: "For continued maintenance of the guild's exclusive licensing privilege."

Matteo closes the ledger. The monopoly that he has defended to every customer who asked about the herbalist's lower prices is not maintained by the superiority of the guild's products. It is maintained by a payment. The compounds he makes are no more effective than the unlicensed alternatives -- the hospital's treatment logs established that. The magistrates enforce the guild's exclusive licence not because the public benefits but because the guild pays them to. And the price difference -- the three-to-one markup that his customers bear -- funds the payment that keeps the monopoly in place.

He sits in the empty guild hall and considers what he has built his twelve-year practice on. The quality of his work is real -- he grinds his compounds carefully, he measures his dosages, he serves his customers with diligence. But the monopoly that protects his livelihood is not built on that quality. It is built on a ledger entry and a magistrate's cooperation. The guild's teaching -- that the monopoly exists because the guild is better -- is a story the guild tells its members so they will defend a privilege that is purchased, not earned.

The guild master arrives and opens the assembly. Matteo sits through the meeting without speaking. He has not decided what to do with what he now understands. But he knows he cannot give the answer about guild quality to the next servant who asks about the herbalist's prices. That answer, which he has given a hundred times, is no longer available to him.
