## Di-arc-Sf3-01

**Scenario.** The communications officer of a deep-survey relay station has maintained for two years that the anomalous signal pattern being received on the station's low-band array is instrument noise from the array's aging wave-guides. When a visiting sensor technician isolates the signal for maintenance purposes, what remains after the wave-guide noise is filtered out is not silence. The sequence is told in present tense.

> A = Communications Officer Dagny Fell; B = the proposition that the anomalous pattern on the low-band array is instrument noise generated by the aging wave-guides; E = a maintenance isolation of the wave-guide noise component, which removes the expected noise floor but leaves a coherent, repeating pattern in the residual signal; B′ = the proposition that the anomalous pattern is not instrument noise — it is a coherent signal of unknown origin present in the actual incoming data

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The relay station Kessler-7 sits at the edge of the Outer Survey Zone, forty light-minutes from the nearest inhabited platform. Fell runs the comms array alone on the night rotation, which is most rotations. She filed her first anomaly report eighteen months ago: a repeating pattern in the 1.2-gigahertz band, irregular but recurring, which she characterised at the time as wave-guide resonance. The array's wave-guides were four years past their replacement window. Resonance at low frequencies from aging guides was a known failure mode. She had logged it, flagged it for maintenance, and moved on.

The pattern had continued for eighteen months because the maintenance team had been on a backlog and wave-guide replacement was not a critical failure. Fell had continued logging it and continued characterising it the same way in every monthly status report: *LBA low-freq anomaly persists; attributed to wave-guide resonance; maintenance ticket #774 outstanding.* She had looked at the raw signal many times. She had seen what she expected to see.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Six months ago, a new comms engineer posted to the station for a three-month rotation had reviewed Fell's anomaly logs as part of his orientation. He had confirmed her characterisation: wave-guide resonance. He had also confirmed that the maintenance backlog was real and the replacement was overdue, which she already knew. He had added a note to the maintenance ticket escalating the priority level by one tier. He had been rotated out after three months. The wave-guides had not been replaced. The signal had continued.

Fell had found the engineer's confirmation useful at the time. She referenced it when station control queried the anomaly after she filed a routine report. She said a second reviewer had examined the logs and agreed on the characterisation. Station control had closed the query.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** The maintenance team arrived to replace the wave-guides on a Tuesday shift. The sensor technician assigned to the array, a compact woman named Ilsev who had clearly done this before, ran a pre-replacement baseline scan before touching the guides. She said it was her standard procedure — baseline before, baseline after, compare the two. Fell had not been asked about this procedure and found it mildly inconvenient for her shift schedule, since the array would be in maintenance mode for two hours.

Ilsev ran the pre-replacement baseline and then looked at her readout for a while without moving to the next step. She asked Fell whether the anomaly was typically present at the current signal-to-noise level. Fell said yes, it had been persistent for over a year at approximately those parameters. Ilsev said she was going to run the active-isolation mode before she pulled the guides, which would suppress the wave-guide contribution electronically rather than physically, and could Fell observe the output.

Fell said she would observe. She did not think anything significant was about to happen.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Ilsev engages the active isolation and the display changes. The noise floor drops by sixteen decibels as the wave-guide contribution is suppressed. What is left in the low-band display is not silence.

There is a pattern. It is smaller than the total anomaly signal had been, and it is different in character from the noise component — the noise had been wideband and irregular within its recurring period; this is narrowband and structured. Fell has not seen this component before, or rather, she has not seen it as distinct from the surrounding noise. The isolation protocol has separated two things she has been treating as one thing for eighteen months.

Ilsev is looking at her own readout. She says the residual is not consistent with any wave-guide signature she has on record.

Fell asks her to hold the isolation mode and runs the residual signal through the station's pattern-analysis package. The package returns a coherence score of 0.87 on a five-minute window. A coherence score above 0.6 on a five-minute window is the station's threshold for tagging a signal as potentially structured rather than stochastic. Fell has set this threshold herself. The pattern in front of her has cleared it by a substantial margin.

She looks at the display for a long time. The wave-guides are aging and they have been generating noise and the maintenance ticket has been outstanding for eighteen months. All of that is still true. And none of it is what was producing the coherent pattern that is showing on her display right now, because the wave-guide contribution has been subtracted and the pattern is still there.

She says to Ilsev: "Hold the isolation. Don't touch the guides yet."

She pulls up her anomaly logs for the past two years and begins re-examining the raw data with the wave-guide component removed.

**E5 (consequences of revision).** Fell's work console, four hours after the isolation was engaged. She has been running the archived signal data through the same isolation protocol retrospectively, reconstructing what the residual pattern looks like across twenty-two months of logged data. The pattern is consistent. It has not been changing in character over twenty-two months. It has been there the entire time, beneath the wave-guide noise, at a level that was inside the noise floor when she was looking at the composite signal.

She does not know what the signal is. It is periodic at 3.7-hour intervals, structured within each period, and originating from a direction her array cannot triangulate alone. It may be another station's transmission on an unregistered frequency allocation. It may be interference from a distant industrial source. It may be something else. She does not have enough information to determine its nature, only to determine that it is there and that it is coherent.

She files an amended anomaly report: *LBA low-freq anomaly; prior characterisation (wave-guide resonance) is partially incorrect. Wave-guide contribution confirmed and isolated during maintenance baseline. Residual signal remaining after isolation is structured and coherent; does not match wave-guide signature. Recommend array recalibration, full-spectrum directional analysis, and coordination with adjacent survey stations to determine signal source. Maintenance ticket #774 remains valid for wave-guide replacement; isolation of wave-guide noise does not eliminate or explain the residual signal.*

She sends the report to station control and to the regional survey authority. She notes in the transmittal that she has been logging this signal for twenty-two months and is amending her characterisation on the basis of the maintenance baseline data.

Then she asks Ilsev to go ahead with the wave-guide replacement. There is no reason to delay it. The guides need replacing regardless of what the residual signal turns out to be.

## Di-arc-Sf3-02

**Scenario.** The director of a terraforming project on a cold-margin world has operated for six years on the belief that the planet's subsurface ice layer is a stable, uniform reservoir that the project's atmospheric seeding program can draw on indefinitely. A deep-core drilling team on a routine survey returns data that contradicts this model. The sequence is told in free indirect style from the director's perspective. Non-linear.

> A = Director Orin Vassi (Calden Terraforming Project, Mirel-4); B = the proposition that the subsurface ice layer on Mirel-4 is a stable, uniform reservoir adequate to sustain the atmospheric seeding program for the project's full fifty-year timeline; E = deep-core drilling data from the survey at Survey Point 9, which shows that the ice layer in the southern quadrant is a fractured, lens-structured deposit sitting on dry regolith, not a continuous basin — and that the project's water yield estimates have been built on extrapolation from a more favourable northern profile; B′ = the proposition that the subsurface ice layer is not a uniform stable reservoir; the southern quadrant's lens structure will be exhausted within twelve to fifteen years at current draw rates, leaving the project without adequate water yield for the seeding program's second phase

6 beats, non-linear: E4 → E1 → E2 → E3 → E5 → E6

**Note on beat order.** Opens in the present (E4, the arrival of the anomalous data), cuts back to establish prior belief, reinforcement, and partial signal, then returns to the decisive analysis and its aftermath.

**E4 (partial/ambiguous evidence — depicted first).** The drilling operations room, third shift. Vassi is reviewing routine reports when the feed from Survey Point 9 updates. He glances at it, intending to pass over it; the SP-9 drill team has been working the southern quadrant for six weeks on a survey run that he has already decided is confirmatory rather than exploratory.

The stratigraphic profile from the most recent core run is wrong. Not wrong in the way that data is sometimes noisy — wrong in the way that suggests the underlying geology is not what the model predicted. The core shows a layer sequence at 340 metres that does not match the northern-quadrant profile the project's water model has been based on. Where the northern profile shows a continuous ice body with a dense impermeable boundary, the SP-9 core shows a broken sequence: ice lenses interspersed with dry regolith sections, with the total ice content per metre significantly lower than the model's baseline.

Vassi stares at the feed. Then he pulls up the SP-9 team's previous two core runs from earlier in the week. The pattern is consistent across all three. He requests the team's full dataset before they begin the next run.

He does not tell anyone else that evening. He wants to look at the data himself before he says anything.

**E1 (prior belief established).** Six years ago, Vassi's first full season on Mirel-4. The project's water model had been constructed by the planetary survey team before his appointment, using data from the northern quadrant where the initial survey cores had been drilled. The northern cores showed a continuous ice body at 280–450 metres across the surveyed area, with an estimated total volume of 4.3 × 10^11 cubic metres — enough, the model calculated, to sustain the atmospheric seeding program through its fifty-year timeline at the projected draw rates.

Vassi had reviewed the model and the northern core data when he took the position. He had found the estimate conservative rather than optimistic — the northern cores had sampled a relatively small area and the extrapolation to the full basin was a significant projection, but the geological team's basis for the extrapolation was the structural continuity they had observed in the northern samples. Continuous ice bodies tended to be continent-wide on cold-margin worlds with low historical tectonic activity. Mirel-4 had low historical tectonic activity.

He had built his six-year operational plan on the water model. The model was the reason the project was viable.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Year three of the project. The draw wells in the northern quadrant were performing at 92% of the projected yield. The atmospheric seeding program was ahead of schedule on its first-phase carbon loading targets. The project's annual report to the consortium included a section on water security that Vassi had personally drafted, noting that the subsurface reservoir had performed within projected parameters and that the project's water timeline was secure.

The consortium had used that language in its own investor communications. Vassi knew this because a consortium board member had mentioned it approvingly at a briefing. The language had felt, at the time, accurate and appropriate to say.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** Year five, a quarterly review meeting. The project's chief geologist, a careful woman named Adra Sven, had mentioned in passing that the SP-7 core run in the mid-southern sector had shown a slightly lower ice-density reading than the model predicted — within the model's error bounds, but at the lower end. She had noted it as something to monitor.

Vassi had noted it in the meeting minutes and moved to the next item. The reading was within error bounds. One anomalous core sample in a continuous-body model was a statistical inevitability. The model accounted for local variability. Sven had not flagged it as requiring investigation. He had treated it accordingly.

He had not thought about it again until the SP-9 data arrived. He thinks about it now.

**E5 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Adra Sven's office, the morning after Vassi reviewed the SP-9 data. He has brought the core runs with him. He sets them on her desk and tells her what he found.

Sven looks at the data for a long time. She pulls up the SP-7 data from the previous year alongside it. The SP-7 anomaly and the SP-9 sequence are consistent with each other — the SP-7 reading was not a local anomaly. It was the beginning of a pattern that the SP-9 team has now documented more fully.

She pulls the regional geological survey from the pre-project database and starts working through it. After forty minutes she finds what she is looking for: a survey note from the initial mapping team, flagged as low-priority, observing that the southern quadrant showed surface evidence of historic tectonic micro-fracturing absent in the northern sector. The note had recommended southern-quadrant core confirmation before the water model was finalised. The recommendation had not been followed before the model was filed. The water model had been filed on northern data and the extrapolation assumption had been that the southern quadrant shared the northern structure.

It does not share the northern structure. The southern quadrant's ice is fractured into lenses by the old micro-fracture network. The total volume of accessible ice in the southern quadrant is not the projected figure. Sven spends another two hours working through the SP-9 data and the pre-project survey before she gives Vassi a number.

At current draw rates, the southern quadrant's lens deposits will be exhausted in twelve to fifteen years. The project is six years in. The seeding program's second phase, which requires continuous water yield, begins in year nine.

Vassi does not say anything for a while. Then he asks whether there are any assumptions in Sven's analysis that might be optimistic. She says there is one: if the lens structure has a deeper-seated continuous body beneath the fracture zone, the yield could be substantially higher. She says the only way to test that is to drill to a depth they have not yet drilled.

He asks how long that would take. She says six months, minimum.

He asks her to schedule it.

**E6 (consequences of revision).** The director's office, one week after the conversation with Sven. Vassi has drafted a revised water security assessment for the consortium. He has spent the week working through every projection and plan that rested on the original water model: the seeding program's phase-two schedule, the draw-well expansion plan for years eight through twelve, the infrastructure commitments the project has made to the consortium based on the year-nine phase-two start.

The draft assessment is honest in a way that the previous annual report was not — not because the previous report contained false information, but because it had not accounted for the risk that the southern-quadrant data now makes explicit. He writes that the water security assumption underpinning the project's timeline is now uncertain, that the uncertainty arises from a geological characterisation based on northern-quadrant data that does not adequately represent the southern quadrant's structure, and that the project is initiating a deep-confirmation drill program to quantify the risk. He writes that, pending the confirmation results, the phase-two schedule should be treated as subject to revision.

He sends the assessment to the consortium board before the next quarterly briefing. He does not wait for the confirmation results to file it. The board is making decisions based on the prior assessment. They need to know the prior assessment is no longer adequate, even before he knows what the correct assessment is.

The consortium board chair responds within four hours, asking for a call. Vassi takes the call. It is not a comfortable call.

## Di-arc-Sf3-03

**Scenario.** The senior arbiter of a post-scarcity orbital habitat's distribution council has operated for a decade on the belief that the habitat's allocation algorithm produces equitable outcomes because its inputs are objectively measured. A systems analyst completing a routine audit surfaces evidence that the algorithm's measurement layer has a systematic bias that the arbiter's prior audits never checked for. The sequence uses embedded document form.

> A = Senior Arbiter Kaela Drost (Meridian Habitat Distribution Council); B = the proposition that the allocation algorithm produces equitable outcomes because its input measurements are objective; E = an audit finding documenting that the measurement layer's environmental sensor network is denser in high-productivity residential zones than in low-productivity zones, causing the algorithm to systematically underestimate resource consumption per capita in low-productivity areas and over-allocate to high-productivity zones; B′ = the proposition that the algorithm's inputs are not objective — the measurement layer has a systematic density bias, and the allocation outcomes have been inequitable in a patterned, structural way that the arbiter's prior audits never examined

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The Distribution Council chamber, Meridian Habitat, Level 7. The annual equity review. Arbiter Drost has been presenting the same core finding for nine years: the allocation algorithm produces outcomes within the council's equity threshold across all twelve residential zones, as measured by per-capita resource index. The equity threshold was set by the council's founding charter. The algorithm has met it every year.

The founding assumption behind the algorithm was that objective measurement was the basis of fairness — if inputs were accurately measured, the formula would distribute justly. Drost had helped design the measurement specifications when she joined the council. She believed in them. The algorithm had passed every equity review she had conducted. The council had approved the annual report each year without material challenge.

She had not, in nine years, audited the measurement layer itself. The algorithm's outputs were what the equity review examined. The measurement layer was infrastructure. Infrastructure was maintained separately, by the habitat's systems division.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Three years into her tenure, an external review team from the orbital governance authority had conducted a routine compliance assessment. They had reviewed the council's methodology, examined three years of allocation records, and certified the process as compliant with the orbital equity standard. The certification letter had noted, specifically, that the use of objective sensor-based measurement rather than self-reported consumption was a best-practice approach.

Drost had filed the certification letter. She had cited it twice since then when the council's equity methodology was challenged. The external certification had seemed to her like corroboration that the foundation of the system was sound.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** Eighteen months ago, a newly appointed systems analyst named Ferris Nahm had flagged a discrepancy in the sensor maintenance logs during an unrelated review of infrastructure records. The discrepancy was that the replacement cycle for environmental sensors was shorter in the upper-level residential zones than in the lower-level zones, which he attributed to higher use-intensity wearing the sensors faster. He had filed the observation as an infrastructure note, not as a council matter, and had moved on.

The note had been filed in the infrastructure division's records. It had not been forwarded to the council. Drost had not seen it. When Nahm was assigned six months later to conduct the council's annual algorithm audit — a different task, assigned because the regular auditor was on medical leave — he had not initially connected the two things.

He connected them approximately two months into the audit, when he began looking at sensor network density for a different reason. Then he stopped working on the audit and started working on something else entirely for three weeks.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered — embedded document).** Nahm's audit supplementary report, filed as an addendum to the annual audit package, addressed to the Senior Arbiter:

---

*MERIDIAN DISTRIBUTION COUNCIL — ALLOCATION ALGORITHM AUDIT*
*Supplementary Finding: Measurement Layer Sensor Density*
*Prepared by: Ferris Nahm, Systems Analyst (acting auditor)*

*In the course of the standard audit, I examined the environmental sensor network that provides the algorithm's consumption-measurement inputs. I found the following:*

*Sensor density (units per 100 square metres of residential space): Level 9–12 (high-productivity zones): 14.3 sensors per 100 m². Level 1–4 (low-productivity zones): 6.1 sensors per 100 m². Habitat average: 9.8 sensors per 100 m².*

*Lower sensor density produces higher interpolation error in consumption measurements. The algorithm's measurement layer uses spatial interpolation to estimate consumption in unsensored areas. In low-density sensor zones, the interpolated areas are larger and the interpolation error is larger. Because the interpolation algorithm defaults to the zone's prior-period baseline in high-uncertainty areas, and because low-productivity zones have historically shown lower measured consumption (in part, I believe, because of the lower sensor density), the algorithm systematically underestimates resource draw in low-sensor zones.*

*I have modelled the effect across nine years of allocation data using a simulated even-density sensor network. Under even density, the per-capita resource index gap between high-productivity and low-productivity zones increases by 31% relative to the reported figures. Under the simulated even-density scenario, the low-productivity zones have not met the equity threshold in any of the nine years for which I have complete data.*

*The measurement layer density disparity appears to originate in an infrastructure investment differential at the time of the habitat's construction. I have found no evidence that it was an intentional design choice in the allocation methodology; it appears to have been an independent infrastructure decision that was not examined when the measurement specifications were written.*

---

Drost reads the report in the morning before the council convenes. She reads it twice. She reads the modelling section a third time. The equity threshold the council has certified for nine years has been met in the reported figures and not met, under a corrected measurement model, in nine consecutive years. The algorithm is functioning as designed. The measurement layer the algorithm depends on was not designed as part of the algorithm's equity specifications. It was infrastructure that predated her role and that she had not examined.

She sits with the report in front of her. The external certification from the governance authority had reviewed the allocation records. It had not reviewed the sensor network. She had cited that certification as evidence that the measurement foundation was sound. She had been citing a review that had examined the wrong thing.

**E5 (consequences of revision).** The Distribution Council chamber, two days after the report's delivery. Drost has asked all council members to read the supplementary finding before the session. She opens by stating that the council's annual equity review process has been examining the algorithm's outputs without examining the measurement layer that generates the algorithm's inputs, and that the supplementary finding has identified a systematic measurement bias that, if the modelling is correct, means the habitat's actual allocation outcomes have not met the equity threshold for at least nine years.

She states this in those terms, without qualification, before any other business.

She then asks the council to authorise three actions: an independent verification of Nahm's modelling by an external systems team; a survey of the actual sensor network to confirm the density figures; and a temporary suspension of the annual equity certification pending the verification results. She says she is not prepared to certify compliance with the equity threshold while the measurement layer is under examination.

One council member asks whether the arbiter is acting prematurely — Nahm's analysis is a model, not a confirmed finding, and suspending certification may create unnecessary concern before the verification is complete. Drost says the concern is warranted and should be visible rather than suppressed. She says the council has been certifying an outcome without checking whether the measurement foundation was sound. The suspension is not an overcorrection. It is the appropriate response to finding that a key assumption was untested.

The council votes to authorise all three actions. The suspension is logged in the official record.

## Di-arc-Sf3-04

**Scenario.** The chief ecologist of a colony biosphere project believes that the native microbial layer on the target planet is dormant and non-reactive — having observed no response to the colony's initial surface operations over two years. When a graduate researcher runs a set of targeted molecular assays as part of her doctoral work, the results contradict the dormancy assumption in a way that has implications for the entire project. The sequence is dialogue-driven.

> A = Chief Ecologist Dr. Pav Orun (Calder-3 Biosphere Project); B = the proposition that the native microbial layer on Calder-3 is dormant and non-reactive to the colony's surface operations; E = molecular assay results showing that the microbial community in the surface soil layer has undergone measurable gene-expression changes in response to the colony's construction materials and thermal output, consistent with active metabolic adaptation rather than dormancy; B′ = the proposition that the native microbial layer is not dormant — it is metabolically active and has been responding to the colony's presence for at least eighteen months

6 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The project operations room, Calder-3 research base. Dr. Orun is presenting to the colony directorate at the two-year operations review. His ecological impact assessment is the cleanest section of an otherwise complicated report.

"The surface microbial layer has shown no measurable response to our construction footprint," he says. "Biomass concentrations in the affected zones are within natural variation of the pre-arrival baseline. There is no evidence of population-level stress, redistribution, or community restructuring. We characterise the layer as dormant relative to our operations."

The colony director asks whether dormant means inactive or simply slow. Orun says dormant means non-reactive at the detection threshold of the monitoring program. He says if there is activity occurring below that threshold, the instruments the project deployed are not designed to measure it.

The director notes this for the record. Orun moves to the next section of his report.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Six months later, the quarterly monitoring data. The biomass concentrations remain stable. The pH gradient in the construction-adjacent soil remains stable. The metabolic-activity proxy the project uses — carbon dioxide efflux from the surface layer — shows no statistically significant difference between construction-adjacent zones and the control sites twenty kilometres to the north.

Orun writes his quarterly summary: *Native microbial layer status: dormant. No evidence of ecological response to colony presence. Assessment unchanged from Q8.* He has written a version of this sentence for eight consecutive quarters. It has been accurate each time, by the measures the project uses.

He does not find the stability boring. He finds it reassuring. A reactive microbial layer could complicate the biosphere development timeline significantly. Dormancy is the scenario the project needed.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** Graduate researcher Yenne Marsh had been on the base for three months before Orun had a substantive conversation with her about her doctoral work. She was studying xenobiotic stress responses in low-complexity microbial communities — the mechanisms by which dormant microbes activate when environmental conditions change. She had come to Calder-3 specifically because it was a field site where the background ecology was considered stable.

In that conversation, she had mentioned that she was running molecular assays — gene-expression profiling — as part of her baseline characterisation. Orun had said that was interesting and that the results would be a useful complement to the project's biomass and efflux monitoring. He had said the monitoring program to date had not shown any activity signal. Marsh had said she was not surprised to hear that, because gene-expression changes could precede population-level or metabolic changes by months or years.

Orun had noted this as an interesting theoretical point. He had not asked what her initial assay results were showing. He had assumed, without asking, that they were consistent with his monitoring data.

**E4 (partial/ambiguous evidence, secondary).** Marsh had sent Orun a preliminary data package six weeks before the confrontation scene with a brief message: *Some results from the first-round assays that I thought you should know about. The gene-expression profiles in the construction-adjacent samples show some upregulation in stress-response pathways that isn't present in the control samples. Could be a lot of things at this stage — I'm running the confirmatory assays now. Sending this so you have the heads-up.*

Orun had read the message on a busy day, three days before the project's annual inspection, and had filed it under review-pending. He had noted mentally that she had said "could be a lot of things" and had not sent a follow-up. His monitoring data remained unchanged. One researcher's preliminary assay results, explicitly caveated as requiring confirmation, did not seem to him to require immediate attention.

He did not think about it again for six weeks, until Marsh knocked on his office door with the confirmatory results.

**E5 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Marsh's laptop on Orun's desk, the data open to the summary panel.

"You said you had confirmatory results," Orun says.

"Three separate assays, two soil cores per sample zone, five sample zones in the construction footprint and three controls," she says. "The upregulation in the stress-response pathways is confirmed in all five footprint zones and absent in all three controls. It's not noise."

He looks at the data. The gene-expression profiles show measurable changes in a cluster of stress-response genes — he identifies the functional annotations as heat-shock proteins and xenobiotic efflux pumps. Activation of both pathways. The footprint zones show it. The controls do not.

"What's the timeline on the activation?" he asks.

She says the sample depth profile suggests the changes began approximately eighteen months ago, based on the depth at which the altered expression signatures appear. The colony began construction operations twenty-two months ago.

He is quiet for a moment. He says: "My monitoring program uses biomass concentration and CO2 efflux as metabolic proxies."

"Those are population-level and metabolic-rate measures," she says. "Gene expression changes can precede detectable population or metabolic changes. You're measuring the output of the response. I'm measuring the mechanism that's being activated to generate the response."

He asks whether the expression changes indicate a harmful or a benign response to the colony's presence. She says she cannot determine that from the gene-expression data alone; the activated pathways are consistent with adaptation responses, which could be either. She says the efflux pumps suggest the organisms are attempting to process something they are encountering in the construction zones, but what they are processing and whether the processing is succeeding requires more work.

"So the layer is not dormant," Orun says.

"The layer has not been dormant for at least eighteen months," she says. "By my data."

**E6 (consequences of revision — confrontation scene).** The colony directorate meeting, one week later. Orun has asked to revise his ecological impact assessment before it is submitted to the oversight authority. The colony director asks what the revision entails.

Orun presents Marsh's findings. He says the native microbial layer has been showing gene-expression responses to the colony's operational footprint for at least eighteen months, and that his monitoring program, which used biomass and metabolic-rate proxies, was not designed to detect gene-expression-level changes and therefore did not detect the response.

The director asks what Orun is recommending.

"Amendment of the ecological characterisation from dormant to metabolically active and responsive," Orun says. "And an expanded monitoring program that includes molecular assay components. And a review of the biosphere development schedule to assess whether any planned activities have been designed on the assumption of a non-reactive native layer."

The operations manager objects: amending the characterisation will trigger a mandatory review under the planetary protection protocol, which will delay the development schedule by at least six months while the oversight authority assesses the revised risk profile.

"That protocol exists because a mischaracterised native ecology creates risks for the development program," Orun says. "I mischaracterised this one. I did not do it deliberately, but my monitoring design was not adequate to detect the response that was occurring. Filing an amended characterisation is not optional."

The operations manager asks whether Orun is certain the gene-expression data is as significant as Marsh claims. Orun says he has reviewed the methodology and the raw data. He says he is confident in the data and prepared to stand behind it to the oversight authority.

The director calls a vote. The amendment passes. Resolution: Orun prevails; the characterisation is amended and the protocol review proceeds.

He asks Marsh to co-author the amended assessment. She says she will.
