## Di-arc-Fa3-01

**Scenario.** The Master of the Cartographers' Guild has maintained for decades that the Ashen Reach — a stretch of deadland east of the Thornwall — is impassable due to a permanent binding set by the last Mireborn during the Severance War. When a young surveyor returns from an unsanctioned expedition with physical evidence of a passable route, the Master must account for what the evidence actually shows. The sequence is told in dialogue-driven form.

> A = Master Edric Vane (Master of the Cartographers' Guild); B = the proposition that the Ashen Reach is impassable due to a permanent binding from the Severance War; E = a set of survey markers, route sketches, and pressed botanical samples from the Reach's interior, collected by Surveyor Lenn Ossian during a three-week crossing; B′ = the proposition that the binding is no longer active, and that the Ashen Reach is passable — a fact the Guild's standing maps and all Guild-certified route assessments have been incorrectly reporting for at least a generation

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The cartography hall, the examination board for third-rank certification. Lenn Ossian sat on the candidate's stool and Master Vane stood at the board map, pointing with his rod to the grey-shaded expanse east of the Thornwall.

"Name the Reach and give its status," Vane said.

"The Ashen Reach," Lenn said. "Sealed since the Severance War by the Mireborn binding. Impassable by any mortal crossing. Not surveyed. Not surveyable."

"Why not surveyable?"

"Because the binding persists. Any path that enters the Reach closes behind the traveller. No route can be mapped because no route is stable."

Vane set down his rod. "The binding is self-renewing and was last confirmed active by the Third Survey Commission eighty years ago. It is treated as permanent in all current Guild doctrine." He made a note in the margins of Lenn's certification file. "Proceed to the next question."

Lenn passed the examination. The grey-shaded expanse remained grey on the board map behind them.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The hall of records, six months later. Lenn had been assigned to the archive as part of the standard first posting for newly-certified surveyors. She was copying route assessments for the eastern trade corridor when she found the commission report from the Third Survey, the one Vane had cited during the examination. She read it that afternoon instead of finishing the copies.

The report's conclusion confirmed what Vane had said: the binding was active, the paths were unstable, the Reach was impassable. But the methodology section described only a perimeter survey — the commission had not entered the Reach at all. Their evidence for the binding's activity was a set of compass readings taken at the Thornwall boundary, where the instruments had spun, and the testimony of two hedge-mages who said they could feel the binding's resonance from the outside.

Lenn put the report back and returned to her copies. She was a first-posting surveyor. The commission had been authorised by the Guild Council. She finished her shift and said nothing.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** The eastern survey office, eight months after Lenn's posting ended. She had taken a field assignment along the Thornwall's southern leg, mapping boundary markers for a territorial dispute. On the fourth day of the field work, near the base of the wall where the deadland began, her compass did not spin.

She stopped and checked it. She moved twenty paces north, closer to the Reach's edge, and checked it again. Still stable. She took three more readings at intervals along the wall. Stable, stable, stable. The hedge-mage who had written the original perimeter testimony had described compass instability beginning forty yards from the wall's base. Lenn was standing at fifteen yards and her instruments were reading clean.

She noted the compass readings in her field journal, recorded the positions, and continued mapping the boundary markers. She did not file a supplementary report when she returned to the office. She told herself she needed more data before she said anything to anyone.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Master Vane's study, an afternoon in late autumn. Lenn had asked for a private consultation and Vane had granted it, expecting a question about the territorial survey. Instead she set three objects on his desk: her field journal opened to the compass readings, a roll of route sketches, and a cloth bundle of pressed botanical samples.

She told him she had entered the Ashen Reach twenty-two days ago. She had walked it for nineteen days and crossed the full width before turning back. She had recorded a viable route, taken botanical and soil samples, and placed survey markers at one-day intervals along her line. She had used her compass throughout. It had not spun once.

Vane looked at the sketches. He looked at the samples. He knew the plants in the samples — or rather, he knew that they did not grow on either side of the Thornwall. They were interior species, documented in pre-Severance natural histories, described by those records as endemic to the Reach's deep interior. You could not pick them from the boundary.

"You're asking me to tell you these are wrong," he said.

"I'm asking you to tell me what they are," Lenn said.

He could not tell her they were wrong. The binding, if it were still active, did not produce stable compass readings, viable crossing routes, or samples of interior flora that a surveyor could carry out in a satchel and press between sheets of paper. All of those things were in front of him. The binding was not active. It had not been active, he now understood, for some unknowable time — perhaps since before the Third Survey Commission had stood at the boundary with their spinning compasses and called it confirmed.

He sat back in his chair and did not say anything for a long moment. Then he said: "How complete is the route sketch?"

"Enough to draft a first-pass map. I'll need to go back to set the secondary markers."

"Not alone," he said. "And not without a formal commission order." He pulled a fresh sheet of parchment toward him. "I need to write to the Council before any of this is announced. But I need you to understand that if what you've shown me holds up — and I believe it will — then every route assessment the Guild has issued for the eastern approaches for the last eighty years has been wrong. I issued some of them myself."

He did not ask her to leave the samples. That meant something, and she understood what it meant.

**E5 (consequences of revision).** The Guild Council chamber, three weeks later. Vane stood before the seven council members and presented Lenn's evidence: the compass readings, the route sketches, the botanical samples, and a summary of his own review of the Third Survey Commission's methodology. He said the commission's perimeter evidence had been insufficient to confirm the binding's persistence, and that the Guild had been certifying the Reach as impassable on the basis of an eighty-year-old external reading that had not been re-examined.

The council chair asked whether the binding had been broken by a specific act or had simply faded. Vane said he did not know, and that the Guild was not in a position to determine this without consulting the Mireborn scholars at the eastern academies. He said the more immediate question was whether the Guild intended to correct its route assessments and what it intended to say to the merchants and border lords who had been routing around the Reach for two generations.

The chair asked who had made the crossing. Vane named Lenn Ossian. The chair noted that an unsanctioned expedition into a sealed territory was a Guild disciplinary matter. Vane said that was correct, and that he was prepared to bring a formal recommendation to the council on that question as well. His recommendation was that the council treat the expedition as a survey of record, authorise Lenn to lead the formal remapping commission, and note in the record that the original binding-confirmation methodology had been inadequate. He said he was prepared to have his own name attached to that notation, since he had trained candidates on the doctrine for thirty years without examining the evidence it rested on.

The council deliberated for two hours. When they returned, they accepted Vane's recommendation without modification.

## Di-arc-Fa3-02

**Scenario.** The Warden of the eastern grain depots has always believed that the blight striking the outlying farms is caused by a cursed groundwater vein running beneath the eastern fields — a belief shared by the local hedge-priests for three generations. A travelling apothecary who has been treating afflicted farmers asks to examine the depot records, and what she shows the Warden calls the entire theory into question. The sequence is told in free indirect style from the Warden's perspective.

> A = Warden Cassiel Drent; B = the proposition that the eastern farm blight is caused by a cursed groundwater vein running beneath the fields; E = a side-by-side comparison of the apothecary's patient records with the depot's grain-intake logs, showing that afflicted farms are clustered not along the known path of the groundwater vein but at farms that received seed grain from the same specific depot shipment six years prior; B′ = the proposition that the blight's source is a contaminated seed stock, not a groundwater curse, and has been spreading through replanting from a single contaminated lot

4 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** The warden's records were meticulous, and Cassiel was proud of them — three generations of intake logs for the eastern depots, going back to before her father's tenure. The logs noted which farms had submitted to tithe, which had shortfalls, and which had been flagged for blight assessment. The blight assessments, she had always believed, told a clear story: the afflicted farms clustered along the line of the Underfen vein, the subterranean waterway that the hedge-priests had identified in her grandmother's time as cursed by a long-dead water-spirit's dying malediction.

The priests performed a cleansing rite every third autumn. The blight persisted, which the priests attributed to the depth and age of the curse. Cassiel had accepted this because the geographical pattern fit — the afflicted farms did cluster on the Underfen's path, more or less — and because the priests were confident, and because she had no better account to offer.

She had allocated tithe relief funds accordingly, routing more support to farms along the vein, for most of her tenure as Warden. The allocations felt like sound stewardship of a bad situation.

**E2 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** The apothecary, a woman named Maret who had been working the eastern roads for two years, had asked for access to the intake logs on a grey Tuesday afternoon. She said she was tracking the blight's progression and thought the depot records might show something her patient records did not. Cassiel had given her access to the reading room and thought no more about it.

Two days later, Maret knocked on the office door and said she had found something she did not understand and wanted to check against the Warden's knowledge of the depot's distribution practices. Cassiel had expected a question about the Underfen farms. What Maret showed her was a list of farm names annotated against dates of first blight incidence. It did not look, at first glance, like a geographical map. It looked more like a distribution list.

Cassiel had said she did not see what Maret was getting at. Maret had said she was not entirely sure either, and asked whether Cassiel could tell her where the seed grain for the eastern farms had come from, in particular for the distribution six years ago. Cassiel said she would need to check the allocation records and asked Maret to come back the following morning.

That night Cassiel pulled the allocation books for the relevant season. She found the entry before she reached the end of the first page. But she did not yet see what it meant.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Maret spread her comparison on the warden's desk the following morning: on the left, a list of farms with their first recorded blight year, ordered by which farm had gone to grain relief first; on the right, the depot allocation log for the distribution six years prior, showing which farms had received seed grain from Shipment Lot 7 of that season.

The lists were almost identical. Thirty-one farms had gone to grain relief in the six years since that distribution. Twenty-eight of them had received from Lot 7.

Cassiel stared at the lists. She knew the Underfen's course better than she knew her own face, and she looked at the farm names on Maret's list and asked herself whether they ran along the vein. Some did. Enough that the pattern had looked geographical for six years. But three of the worst-afflicted farms sat on the ridge west of the vein, on ground as far from the Underfen as anything in the eastern holdings. She had always noted those three as anomalies — difficult to fit, not quite on the vein's path, possibly contaminated by runoff. She had found reasons to include them in the vein's story.

They were not anomalies. They were part of the actual pattern. They had received from Lot 7.

She asked Maret where Lot 7 had come from. Maret said she did not know, and that was what she needed from the depot records — the provenance log, if there was one, showing which granary or which merchant had supplied the seed stock.

Cassiel went to the back shelf and found the provenance log. The entry for Lot 7 was brief: purchased from the Harken mill, Eastern Corridor, on consignment, because the usual supplier had been unable to fill the order that season. The Harken mill had been cited for milling irregularities three years later and had closed. There was no record of whether the irregularity had been a contamination issue.

The Underfen was a line on a map that had drawn an explanation around it. The seed stock was a single point of origin that six years of replanting had spread across thirty-one farms, along a distribution pattern that happened to overlap with the vein closely enough that nobody had looked past it.

**E4 (consequences of revision).** The warden's hall, the afternoon of the same day. Cassiel had summoned the senior hedge-priest, Father Borvane, and asked him to bring his blight assessment records. She had placed Maret's comparison on the table and had shown it to him without preamble.

Borvane had said the geographical correlation with the vein remained significant. Cassiel had said it remained partial. She had shown him the three ridge farms, far from the vein, that fit the seed-distribution pattern and not the groundwater pattern. She had asked him how the vein's curse reached those farms.

Borvane said curses were not always constrained by direct physical contact. He said spiritual contamination could travel through exchange networks and shared materials. He said replanted grain from a blight-afflicted farm could carry the curse with it.

Cassiel had paused at that. She had then asked him to describe how replanted grain carrying a spiritual contamination would behave, mechanically. Borvane had described, without apparent awareness of what he was doing, something that was functionally indistinguishable from seed-stock contamination spreading through replanting. He had given her the mechanism she needed while arguing that his own explanation was still correct.

She thanked him for his time. She told him she intended to write to the agricultural commission requesting a seed-stock examination of grain from afflicted farms and a provenance audit of the Harken mill's other lots from that season. She said she was not dismissing the possibility of spiritual contamination but she intended to pursue the physical account alongside it, because the physical account fit the distribution pattern without requiring the anomalies that the vein account had always struggled to explain.

Borvane said the commission would take months. The rite was three weeks away.

Cassiel said the rite could proceed, and she would fund it as she always had. But the tithe relief allocations for next season would be based on Maret's distribution list, not the vein map, until she had reason to change that.

## Di-arc-Fa3-03

**Scenario.** The High Arbiter of the Stonecutters' Order believes that a long-running feud between two stone-clan families — the Veldrath and the Murrek — is sustained by a disputed inheritance clause from the founding charter of their shared quarry. After adjudicating fourteen disputes over two decades, she is forced to read the original charter for the first time when a young Murrek journeyman produces a copy he found in a sealed annex. What the charter actually says is not what either family's lawyers have been arguing it says. The sequence is told in present tense.

> A = High Arbiter Sera Ondath; B = the proposition that the Veldrath-Murrek feud is sustained by a genuine ambiguity in the founding charter's inheritance clause that makes both families' claims legally defensible; E = the original founding charter, which contains no ambiguity in the relevant clause — it assigns the quarry's secondary extraction rights unambiguously to the Murrek line, in language that both families' lawyers would have read if they had read the document rather than citing each other's summaries of it; B′ = the proposition that both families have been litigating a dispute whose resolution is already written into the founding document, and that the feud has been sustained not by legal ambiguity but by institutional failure to consult the primary source

6 beats, non-linear: E3 → E1 → E2 → E4 → E5 → E6

**Note on beat order.** The sequence opens in the present (E3, the moment the charter arrives) before cutting back to establish the prior belief and its reinforcement. Chronological order: E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 → E5 → E6.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence — depicted first).** The annex of the Stonecutters' hall smells of lime dust and old leather, and Journeyman Davan Murrek stands in the doorway holding a scroll case that he says he found in a sealed recess behind a collapsed shelf when the hall underwent structural repairs last week. He says the case was sealed with the Order's old wax mark, the one the guild used before the succession of three decades ago. He does not know what is in it. He thought the Arbiter should decide.

Sera takes the case. She does not open it immediately. She asks Davan when the repairs found it and whether he opened the case himself. He says he found it on the fourth day of repairs, he did not open it, and he brought it directly to her. She thanks him and asks him to wait outside.

She sets the case on the reading table and looks at the wax seal. The mark is genuine — she has seen enough old documents to know the old mark from the new. She notes that the case is in the sealed annex rather than the general archive, which means it was deliberately set apart at some point, not simply misfiled. She takes out her knife and breaks the seal.

**E1 (prior belief established).** Eight years into Sera's tenure as High Arbiter, the third time the Veldrath family brought a claim against the Murrek over secondary extraction rights to the eastern seam. The Order's counsel reviewed the founding charter's inheritance clause — or rather, reviewed the summary of the inheritance clause that appeared in every prior adjudication's record, which was itself a summary of the clause as originally argued by the first family advocates two generations ago. The counsel had said, as the counsel before him had said, that the clause was ambiguous on the question of whether "secondary extraction rights" included the eastern seam as a sub-category of surface extraction or as a category of its own.

Sera had adjudicated in favour of a shared-use arrangement, as the two arbiters before her had. She had written her ruling in terms of the unresolved ambiguity. She had believed the ambiguity was real.

She had never read the founding charter itself. The summary had seemed sufficient. It had always seemed sufficient.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The most recent Veldrath claim, two years ago. The Veldrath family's advocate, a sharp woman named Torvesse who had been arguing these cases for fifteen years, had brought a new argument: a secondary document, a letter from one of the charter's original signatories that she said clarified the intent of the clause in the Veldrath family's favour. The Murrek family's advocate had argued that the letter was not authoritative over the charter's text. Sera had ruled that the letter was admissible but not determinative, and had again adjudicated in favour of shared use.

After the ruling, Torvesse had said, privately, that she respected Sera's consistency in treating the ambiguity as real. She had said she expected they would be back in a few years. Sera had said she expected the same. Neither of them had seemed troubled by this. The feud had been sustaining itself on the ambiguity for generations. It was the nature of ambiguous founding documents that they generated perpetual litigation. Sera had accepted this as the shape of the problem.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The founding charter is on parchment, not vellum, and the ink is brown with age but entirely legible. Sera reads it straight through, then reads the inheritance clause a second time.

The clause is four sentences. The first two define the quarry boundaries. The third assigns primary extraction rights jointly to the Veldrath and Murrek families in perpetuity, in equal shares. The fourth sentence — the sentence that has been summarised, argued about, and adjudicated fourteen times in two generations — reads:

*Secondary extraction rights, being defined as all rights to extract stone from any seam more than twelve hand-spans below the primary working face, shall pass in perpetuity to the Murrek family and its lawful heirs, unencumbered, not subject to division or challenge.*

The sentence is not ambiguous. It contains no term that requires interpretation. "Secondary extraction rights" are defined in the same sentence. The eastern seam, which runs eighteen hand-spans below the primary working face, falls within the definition by simple measurement. The Murrek family holds those rights. The charter says so in words that do not invite another reading.

Sera sets the document down. She goes to her records shelf and pulls the adjudication file from the most recent Veldrath claim. She finds the section where the Order's counsel summarised the inheritance clause. The summary reads: *The clause assigns primary rights jointly and makes secondary rights subject to an ambiguity concerning the seam-depth threshold and the question of whether surface and subsurface rights were intended to be severed.* There is no ambiguity in the original document about the seam-depth threshold. The threshold is specified. The counsel had not read the original; he had summarised a summary that had been summarised before him.

She pulls the file from the adjudication before that. The same summary, copied forward. She goes back through seven adjudication files and finds the same summary in all seven. The counsel who first produced it is noted in the earliest record as citing "the charter's inheritance provision." He did not quote it. He characterised it. Every subsequent ruling has rested on that characterisation.

**E5 (belief revision).** Sera sits at the reading table for a long time. The feud between the Veldrath and the Murrek families is not the product of a genuine legal ambiguity. It is the product of fourteen adjudications that resolved an ambiguity no one had verified was actually in the document. Both families have spent generations and considerable resources litigating a question whose answer has been sitting in a sealed annex beneath a collapsed shelf.

The families had advocates who argued confidently. The advocates had relied on prior records. The prior records had relied on a summary. The summary had been wrong. The system had been processing the error faithfully for two generations and calling it adjudication.

She considers what it means that she has ruled seven times herself on a false factual premise. She considers what it means that the Veldrath family has, for two generations, been asserting a claim to rights they were never entitled to under the founding document — not through bad faith, as far as she can tell, but through the same reliance on summarised precedent that the Order itself has been perpetuating.

She makes a note of the conclusions she has reached and their basis. She does not commit them to the record yet. She needs to verify, first, that the document is what it appears to be and not a forgery. She writes a note to the Order's archivist asking for an assessment of the parchment and ink against known documents of the founding period.

**E6 (consequences of revision — confrontation scene).** The arbitration chamber, three weeks later. Both families' advocates are present. Sera has told them only that new evidence bearing on the longstanding dispute has come to light. The archivist has confirmed the charter's authenticity.

Sera places a copy of the inheritance clause — the actual clause, not the summary — on the table between the advocates. She reads it aloud.

Torvesse, the Veldrath advocate, says immediately that the clause must be read in conjunction with the secondary document she introduced two years ago.

"The secondary document cannot supersede the founding charter," Sera says. "State whether your objection is to the authenticity of the charter, the legibility of the clause, or the plain meaning of its terms. Those are the grounds on which a challenge can proceed."

Torvesse is silent for a moment. Then she says her objection is to the legibility — specifically, that the clause's definition of "secondary extraction rights" by seam depth was a provision the founding families subsequently agreed to modify, and that evidence of that modification exists in the family papers.

"Produce the evidence," Sera says. "I will receive it. If it is credible and authenticated, this matter goes to a full evidentiary review before the Order Council. If it is not, I will rule on the basis of the founding charter as written. You have thirty days."

Torvesse says thirty days is not sufficient. Sera says it is the standard period for producing evidence in a contested charter matter and she is not extending it.

The Murrek advocate asks nothing. He waits.

Resolution: deferred to named authority (the Order Council, contingent on evidence being produced within thirty days). If no evidence is produced, Sera will rule. The Veldrath family holds the burden.

## Di-arc-Fa3-04

**Scenario.** The ranking battle-mage of the Stelthain Order believes that a set of ward-failures along the northern border are caused by deliberate interference from a rogue practitioner. When the Order's own ward-keeper examines the failure sites, the evidence points toward something the battle-mage had not considered: the wards were failing because the binding materials used in their construction were counterfeit. The sequence uses embedded document form.

> A = Commander Ysel Harath (ranking battle-mage, Stelthain Order); B = the proposition that the ward-failures along the northern border are caused by deliberate sabotage by a rogue practitioner; E = the ward-keeper's examination report, which documents that the failure pattern is consistent with a material deficiency in the binding compounds used at construction, not with practitioner interference; B′ = the proposition that the ward-failures are caused by counterfeit binding compounds supplied to the Order, not by external sabotage

5 beats, chronological

**E1 (prior belief established).** Commander Harath stood before the Order's tactical council and read from the enumerated ward failures: seven wards compromised along a forty-league stretch in nine months, all at the northern border's weakest exposure points. The pattern was not random. She had mapped the failures on the situation board and the map showed a progression that suggested targeted selection — whoever was attacking the wards knew which nodes to strike.

She named the most likely practitioner. A former Stelthain member, expelled four years prior, who had the knowledge of the Order's ward architecture and the documented capacity for long-range suppression work. She told the council she was requesting authorisation to open a formal investigation and to post additional battle-mages at the remaining border nodes.

The council granted both. Harath left the meeting certain she knew what the problem was. She had not been wrong about the expelled practitioner before.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Three months later, her investigators had placed the expelled practitioner in the northern territories at the time of two of the seven failures. Not at the failure sites, but within a day's travel. That was not proof of direct action, but it was consistent with the theory, and the council agreed it was consistent. Harath tightened the border postings and dispatched a warning to the regional governors.

At the next council briefing she said the investigation was progressing along the expected lines. The council chair asked whether they were close to a formal accusation. Harath said not yet, but the circumstantial picture was building. She felt steady in her assessment. The pattern was holding.

**E3 (partial/ambiguous evidence).** The ward-keeper, a meticulous woman named Torven who had been examining the failure sites under Harath's commission, filed a preliminary report that arrived on Harath's desk on a cold morning six weeks after the investigation had begun. The report was brief and flagged as inconclusive. Torven noted that the failure signatures at two of the sites were unusual — the ward's binding lattice had not been disrupted in the way she would expect from external practitioner interference, where the signature typically showed a clean severance point or a forced-resonance scar. Instead, the lattice had deteriorated gradually along the entire length of the compound binding, which was more consistent with material degradation than with targeted attack.

Torven wrote: *I do not wish to prejudge the investigation's direction. The pattern may yet be consistent with a new suppression technique. I am flagging it for your attention and will complete the remaining site examinations before drawing any conclusions.*

Harath read the report and filed it. She noted in her own record that an advanced practitioner might use a technique designed to mimic material failure and thereby deflect investigation. That was a possibility. It was also, she did not note, a possibility she was proposing because it fit her existing theory.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered — embedded document).** Torven's final examination report, received seven weeks after the preliminary:

---

*WARD-KEEPER'S FIELD EXAMINATION: NORTHERN BORDER WARD-FAILURES*
*Sites examined: all seven. Examiner: Torven Hassk, Senior Ward-Keeper, Stelthain Order.*

*Summary of findings:*

*The failure pattern across all seven sites is consistent with a single cause: the binding compound used in the wards' outer-lattice construction has a crystalline structure incompatible with sustained exposure to northern-facing stone. This incompatibility causes gradual lattice separation over two to three years of exposure. All seven failed wards were installed in the same construction season, using material from the same supply shipment.*

*Comparison with non-failing wards along the same border: seven wards installed in the prior season, using material from a different supply lot, have no detectable lattice degradation.*

*The compound in the failing wards is not consistent with the Stelthain Order's standard binding compound specification. It is a visually similar but chemically distinct material that does not meet the specification's crystalline integrity requirements for cold-stone applications. I have attached a comparative sample analysis conducted by the guild assayer.*

*There is no evidence at any of the seven sites of practitioner interference, forced resonance, or suppression technique. The failure signatures are uniformly consistent with material degradation and inconsistent with external attack.*

*Recommendation: the Order's supply chain for binding compounds should be audited for the construction season in question. The supplier who provided the non-conforming material should be identified. All wards constructed from the same shipment should be assessed for structural integrity regardless of current failure status.*

---

Harath read the report twice. The expelled practitioner's proximity to the border territories did not disappear from her knowledge because of what Torven had written — the proximity was real, the timing was real, the prior record was real — but none of it had been evidence of what she thought it was evidence of. She had been building an account of targeted attack over nine months while the wards were failing for a reason that had nothing to do with the expelled practitioner, and the reason had been present in the materials since the day the wards were installed.

She pulled the construction records for the failing wards and found the supply shipment. She found the vendor. She wrote two messages before she left her study that evening: one to the council, informing them that the investigation's premise required significant revision, and one to the Order's contracts officer requesting the full procurement file for the supply lot in question.

She did not write a third message to the investigators who had been watching the expelled practitioner. She would need to tell them in person.

**E5 (consequences of revision).** The council chamber, the following week. Harath presented Torven's report and the procurement records side by side. She told the council that the ward-failures were caused by substandard binding materials supplied to the Order during the relevant construction season, not by external practitioner sabotage. She said the investigation she had led for nine months had been premised on the wrong cause, and that the evidence she had cited as corroborating the sabotage theory — the expelled practitioner's presence in the region — was circumstantial and had not been probative of anything she had claimed it supported.

The council chair asked whether the expelled practitioner should still be considered a threat. Harath said they remained a threat in principle but had no demonstrated connection to the ward-failures and should no longer be treated as a priority investigation target on the basis of this evidence. She recommended that resources be redirected to the supply-chain audit and to a structural review of all wards from the same construction season.

She also recommended that Torven's field examination methodology be formalised as a standard protocol for all future ward-failure investigations — site examination before causal hypothesis, not after it. The council agreed. The chair noted in the record that the commander's recommendation was accepted and that her candour in revising her own assessment was noted.

Harath found that last phrase easier to receive than she had expected. She had been wrong for nine months about the shape of a problem she was responsible for. Being right about it now felt less like vindication and more like the minimum standard of the job.
