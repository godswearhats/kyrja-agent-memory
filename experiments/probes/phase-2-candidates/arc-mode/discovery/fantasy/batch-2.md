# Discovery arc x Fantasy -- Batch 2 (Tier B)

## Di-arc-Fa2-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** The high archivist of the Conclave of Seers believes that the order's founder, Aethrin the Blind, lost his sight as a child from illness and that his legendary prophetic visions were compensatory gifts of divine origin. A sequence of findings in the founder's sealed correspondence -- a letter describing a deliberate self-blinding ritual, a banned alchemical text annotated in Aethrin's hand, and a confession written to a contemporary -- forces the archivist to accept that Aethrin destroyed his own sight using a proscribed alchemical compound because he believed ordinary vision interfered with the second sight, and that the order's founding mythology is built on a falsehood the founder himself constructed.

> A = Sera Vasith (high archivist of the Conclave of Seers); B = Aethrin lost his sight to childhood illness and his prophetic ability was a divine gift granted in compensation; E = a sealed letter describing self-blinding, an annotated alchemical text on sight-destroying compounds, and a written confession to a contemporary admitting the deliberate act; B' = Aethrin deliberately destroyed his own sight using a banned alchemical compound because he believed it would enhance his second sight, and the founding mythology of the Conclave is a fabrication he authored.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The archive hall was cold, as it always was in the deep chambers beneath the Conclave's tower. Sera Vasith had spent twenty years in this room and she knew its temperatures the way a sailor knew tides -- by season, by hour, by the weight of the air against the stone. She was the high archivist, and the archive was her dominion.

She was preparing the materials for the centennial lecture on Aethrin the Blind, the order's founder. Every hundred years, the high archivist presented the founder's story to the full Conclave -- the story of the boy who lost his sight to the river-fever at age nine and who, in the darkness that followed, found the second sight that would make him the greatest prophet the northern kingdoms had known. It was the Conclave's origin text, the justification for the order's privileges, and the theological basis for the claim that prophetic sight was a compensatory gift from the divine.

Sera believed the story the way she believed in the solidity of the archive's walls. She had read every surviving document in Aethrin's hand. She had catalogued the founding-era manuscripts herself when she was still a junior archivist. The evidence was consistent: Aethrin contracted the river-fever in the year 302, lost his sight by 303, and began prophesying by 305. The Conclave was chartered in 311. The story was not myth to Sera. It was documentary fact, confirmed by primary sources she had handled with her own hands.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Three weeks before the centennial lecture, the sealed annex. The Conclave's master of doctrine had come to Sera with a request: the current High Seer wanted the lecture to address a persistent heresy in the southern chapters, which held that Aethrin's blindness had been self-inflicted. The heresy was old -- it surfaced every few decades, spread by dissident seers who wanted to argue that prophetic ability could be cultivated rather than divinely granted. The High Seer wanted Sera to refute it definitively, using primary sources.

Sera thought the request unnecessary but not unreasonable. She pulled the founding-era medical record -- a healer's log kept by the monastery where the young Aethrin had been treated. The entry for the year 302 described a boy admitted with river-fever, high temperature, pustular rash on the face and hands, and progressive clouding of both eyes over a period of six weeks. The healer's notes were clinical and detailed. The progression was consistent with river-fever's known ophthalmological effects.

Sera copied the relevant passages for the lecture and returned the log to its case. The southern heresy, she thought, was the kind of claim that survived only in the absence of evidence. The evidence was here, in her archive, and it was unambiguous. She felt the particular satisfaction of a scholar holding the document that settles the question.

**E3 (partial evidence encountered).** Two weeks before the lecture, the sealed annex. Sera had been granted access to a chest of Aethrin's private correspondence that had been held in the High Seer's vault since the order's third century. The chest had been sealed for two hundred years. The High Seer had released it for the centennial lecture, expecting it to contain personal letters of historical interest.

Sera opened the chest and began cataloguing. Most of the letters were personal -- correspondence with family, with other prophets, with the monastery that had raised him. She read each one, noted its date and contents, and placed it in the archive's classification system.

Near the bottom of the chest she found a letter in Aethrin's hand, undated, addressed to a name she did not recognise: Olwen. The letter was brief. Most of it was personal -- affectionate, intimate, the kind of writing that suggested a close friendship or more. But one passage stopped her:

*You ask whether I regret it. I do not. The healer's art is imprecise and the compound was stronger than I expected. The pain lasted three days. But the darkness that followed was not empty. It was full. I had been right -- the ordinary sight was a barrier, not a gift. With it gone, the second sight had room.*

Sera read the passage three times. The language was ambiguous. "The compound" could refer to a medicine administered during his illness. "The healer's art" could describe the treatment he received as a child. The passage could be read as a reflection on the pain of his childhood illness and the silver lining of prophetic sight that followed.

But it could also be read another way. Sera noted the letter's existence in her catalogue, placed it in a separate folder, and closed the chest. She did not sleep well that night. She told herself the letter was ambiguous, and ambiguity was not evidence.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** One week before the lecture, the sealed annex. Sera had returned to the chest. She had not been able to leave the letter to Olwen alone. She told herself she was being thorough -- the centennial lecture required a complete catalogue of the newly released correspondence. This was professional diligence, not anxiety.

Beneath the correspondence, wrapped in a separate cloth, she found a small codex. It was not in Aethrin's hand -- it was a printed text, one of the alchemical treatises that had circulated in the northern kingdoms during the third century. She recognised the type: the Conclave had banned the entire genre in its second century because several of the texts described compounds that could damage or destroy eyesight, and the order feared that aspiring seers might use them to induce blindness in pursuit of the second sight.

This particular text was *On the Refinement of Perception*, attributed to an anonymous alchemist. It described, in precise detail, the preparation of a compound made from nightbloom extract and mineral salts that would destroy the optical capacity of the eye without damaging the surrounding tissue. The relevant chapter was marked. The margins contained annotations in a hand Sera recognised immediately: Aethrin's. His notes were not casual. They corrected dosage measurements, noted the duration of the compound's effects, and added a single line at the bottom of the page: *Three days. Painful but complete. The healer's record will serve.*

Sera sat with the codex open on the table in front of her for a long time. *The healer's record will serve.* The medical log she had shown to the master of doctrine three weeks earlier -- the log she had cited as definitive evidence of childhood illness -- was not a record of illness. It was a cover story, either written by a healer who did not know what had really happened or by one who did and was told what to write. Aethrin had used a banned compound to destroy his own sight. He had done it deliberately. And he had ensured that the documentary record told a different story.

**E5 (belief revision -- consequences).** The high archivist's study, two days before the centennial lecture. Sera had laid out the three documents on her worktable: the letter to Olwen, the annotated alchemical codex, and the medical log from the monastery. She had spent three days reading and rereading them. She had checked the handwriting analysis against twelve confirmed samples of Aethrin's writing. The annotations in the codex were his. The letter to Olwen was his. There was no reasonable doubt.

She sat in the chair where she had written every centennial brief for the past two decades and considered what she now held. The Conclave's founding mythology -- that prophetic sight was a divine gift granted in compensation for innocent suffering -- was not merely incomplete. It was a construction, authored by the founder himself, supported by a falsified medical record, and maintained for seven centuries by archivists who had never seen the sealed correspondence.

The question was not whether the documents were genuine. They were. The question was what she would do with a centennial lecture that could no longer say what the High Seer expected it to say. She could not present Aethrin's blindness as a childhood tragedy confirmed by primary sources, because the primary source was compromised. She could not refute the southern heresy, because the southern heresy -- that Aethrin's blindness was self-inflicted -- was correct.

Sera drafted two versions of the lecture that evening. The first presented the documents and their implications. The second omitted them. She read both drafts, burned the second, and sent a message to the High Seer requesting an audience before the lecture. She placed the three documents in a sealed case, locked the case in her personal vault, and sat in the archive hall until the candles burned down, alone with the knowledge that the order's foundation was not what she had spent twenty years believing it to be.

## Di-arc-Fa2-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** The warden of the Thornwall -- a living barrier of enchanted briars that has separated two kingdoms for three centuries -- believes the wall is a neutral defensive structure maintained by the wards his order tends. A dying watchman's testimony, a pattern in the wall's growth, and the content of the original warding charter establish that the Thornwall is not defensive but predatory: it feeds on anyone who enters its boundary, and the order's wards do not maintain it but direct its hunger away from the kingdom that pays the order's tithe.

> A = Warden Edric Hale; B = the Thornwall is a neutral defensive barrier maintained by the order's wards to prevent passage between the two kingdoms; E = a dying watchman's account of the wall consuming a border patrol, growth-pattern records showing the wall expands toward sources of warmth and retreats from warded ground, and the original charter's operative clause directing the order to "feed the wall" not "maintain the wall"; B' = the Thornwall is a predatory organism that consumes living things, and the order's role is not maintenance but management of its appetite -- directing its hunger toward the neighbouring kingdom and away from the one that funds the order.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The watchtower at the wall's eastern gate, a cold morning in late autumn. Warden Edric stood on the parapet with his second, Catrin, looking south at the Thornwall. It stretched from horizon to horizon -- a barrier of black-thorned briars forty feet high and a quarter-mile deep, so dense that no light passed through it. It had stood for three hundred years, maintained by the Order of the Thorn, and it was the reason the two kingdoms had not gone to war in living memory.

"The southern section is thickening again," Catrin said. She pointed to a stretch where the briars had grown visibly denser since their last inspection two weeks earlier.

"It does that before winter," Edric said. "The cold makes the wards contract, and the wall fills the gaps. We'll reinforce the southern ward-stones next week and it'll settle."

Catrin nodded. This was the cycle they had maintained for as long as either of them had served: the wall grew, the wards contained it, the wall settled. The wards were the order's sole purpose -- a network of enchanted stones set into the earth on both sides of the wall, maintained by the order's wardens through seasonal rituals. The wards kept the wall stable, prevented it from growing beyond its boundaries, and ensured that the barrier remained impassable. That was what the order taught its wardens. That was what Edric believed.

"There's something else," Catrin said. "The southern patrol hasn't reported in. They were due at dawn."

"Delayed," Edric said. "The southern stretch is hard walking this time of year."

**E2 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The infirmary at the eastern gate, that evening. A watchman from the southern patrol had been found at the wall's edge, barely alive, dragged to the gatehouse by a passing shepherd. The rest of the patrol -- four men -- had not returned.

Edric sat beside the watchman's cot. The man's legs were wrapped in bandages from ankle to thigh. Beneath the wrappings, the flesh was punctured in dozens of places -- not cut, not torn, but pierced, as if by thorns driven inward with force.

"Tell me what happened," Edric said.

The watchman's voice was thin. "We were walking the southern inspection line. Inside the ward boundary, same as always. Torsten was in front. He stepped off the path -- two steps, maybe three, to check a ward-stone that had shifted."

"And?"

"The wall took him." The watchman swallowed. "The briars moved. Not grew -- moved. They reached out and pulled him in. Fast. He didn't have time to draw his blade. Aldric went in after him and the wall took him too. Brennan and Josser tried to cut them free and the briars wrapped around their arms and dragged them in. I could hear them inside the wall for -- I don't know. A minute. Then I couldn't."

Edric looked at Catrin, who stood by the door. "Walls don't move," he said.

"This one did," the watchman said. "And it wasn't random. It reached for Torsten. It reached for Aldric. When I ran, I tripped and fell against the outer boundary, and the briars near the ward-stones pulled back. They pulled back from me. But five steps away from the ward-stones, they were still reaching."

The watchman closed his eyes. Edric sat with him until the healer asked him to leave. In the corridor, Catrin said: "If the wall pulled back near the ward-stones but reached for him away from them --"

"It means the wards aren't containing the wall," Edric said. "They're repelling it. There's a difference."

He did not finish the thought. But he did not sleep that night, and in the morning he went to the archive.

**E3 (partial evidence -- growth pattern records).** The archive beneath the watchtower, the following morning. Edric pulled the growth-pattern records for the Thornwall -- two centuries of seasonal measurements, kept by every warden who had served before him. The records documented the wall's height, density, and boundary position at each ward-stone, measured quarterly.

He had read these records before. Every warden read them as part of their training. But he had always read them as maintenance logs -- tracking the wall's growth so the wards could be calibrated. He read them differently now.

"Look at this," he said to Catrin, who was helping him lay the records out on the archive table. He pointed to a century-old entry from a warden named Maeris. Maeris had noted that the wall's growth was asymmetric: it grew faster and denser on the northern side -- the side facing the neighbouring kingdom -- than on the southern side, which faced the kingdom that funded the order. Maeris had attributed the asymmetry to soil conditions.

But the pattern held across every decade. The wall grew toward warmth. It grew toward the patrol routes. It grew toward the villages on the northern side of the boundary. And it retreated -- measurably, consistently -- from the ward-stones. Not because the wards contained it. Because the wards repelled it. The wall was not a structure held in shape by the wards. It was something that moved away from the wards and toward anything warm that was not warded.

"It's not a barrier," Edric said. "It's a trap. It faces north because that's where the people are. The wards don't maintain it. The wards tell it where not to go."

Catrin did not answer. She was already pulling the founding charter from the locked case at the back of the archive.

**E4 (belief revision).** The archive, an hour later. The founding charter of the Order of the Thorn lay open between them. Edric had read the charter in his first year as a warden, as every warden did. But he had read it the way the order taught it -- as a maintenance contract, a duty to tend the wards and preserve the wall. He read it again now, with the growth-pattern records spread around him and the watchman's testimony still in his ears.

The charter's operative clause was three lines long. Edric read it aloud:

"The Order of the Thorn is charged by the Crown to feed the wall, to tend the ward-stones that govern its appetite, and to ensure that its hunger is turned northward and held from the Crown's lands and the Crown's people."

He stopped. "Feed the wall," he said. "Not maintain the wall. Feed it."

Catrin pointed to the second clause. "Govern its appetite. Not govern its growth. Appetite."

"And 'turned northward,'" Edric said. "The charter doesn't say 'prevent passage.' It says turn its hunger north. The wall isn't a barrier between two kingdoms. It's a weapon pointed at one of them. And the order's job is to aim it."

He looked at the growth-pattern records, at the asymmetric expansion toward the northern villages, at the ward-stone placement that created a corridor of safety on the southern side and a corridor of danger on the northern side. Three centuries of wardens had read the charter as a maintenance contract because the order's training taught them to read it that way. The language had always said what it said. The order had simply taught its wardens not to read it literally.

Edric closed the charter. He told Catrin to send riders to the four watchmen's families with the news that the patrol was lost. Then he sat down and began writing a report to the High Council of the Order -- not a maintenance report, but a formal demand for an accounting of what the order had known, for how long, and how many people on the northern side of the wall had been consumed by a structure the order called a barrier and the charter called a predator.

## Di-arc-Fa2-3

**6 beats, non-linear: E4 -> E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E5 -> E6, embedded document voice**

**Note on beat order.** This sequence is depicted non-chronologically. The events' chronological order is: E1 (prior belief, years earlier) -- E2 (belief reinforced, recent) -- E3 (ambiguous signal, recent) -- E4 (primary evidence, present) -- E5 (confirming evidence, next day) -- E6 (belief revision, following day). The depicted order opens with the primary evidence, loops back to establish the belief, then moves forward.

**Scenario.** A guild-master of the Lamplighters -- an order that maintains enchanted lanterns illuminating a vast underground city -- believes the lanterns draw their power from a mineral vein beneath the city called the Brightsource, a natural deposit of luminous ore. A failed lantern, a geological survey, and the guild's own founding ledger establish that the Brightsource was exhausted two centuries ago and that the lanterns have since been powered by the life-force of indentured workers sealed in a chamber beneath the guild hall, a practice hidden from all but the guild's inner council.

> A = Guild-master Thessaly Vane; B = the lanterns draw power from the Brightsource, a natural mineral deposit of luminous ore beneath the city; E = a failed lantern exposing a non-mineral energy signature, a geological survey showing the Brightsource vein is exhaustite, and the founding ledger's description of the "renewal chamber" and its human occupants; B' = the Brightsource was exhausted centuries ago and the lanterns are powered by the harvested life-force of indentured workers held in a sealed chamber beneath the guild hall.

**E4 (primary evidence -- present day).** The lantern at the junction of Briar Street and the Fourth Descent had gone dark. This was not, by itself, unusual -- lanterns dimmed, lanterns flickered, lanterns needed their casings cleaned and their ward-filaments replaced. But this lantern had not dimmed or flickered. It had gone dark in a single instant, and when Thessaly Vane knelt beside it and opened the maintenance hatch, the filament inside was cold. Not burned out. Cold. A burned filament left residue -- a faint trace of the Brightsource ore's luminous decay, the same golden dust that coated every lantern casing in the city. This filament was clean. Whatever had been powering it had not been mineral in origin.

Thessaly removed the filament and held it under her inspection glass. She had maintained lanterns for thirty years, first as an apprentice and then as guild-master, and she had never seen a filament without mineral residue. She wrapped it in cloth, sealed it in her belt pouch, and ordered the junction closed to foot traffic until a replacement lantern could be installed. Then she returned to the guild hall and went to the assay room, where the guild's mineralogists tested ore samples from the Brightsource.

She set the cold filament on the assay table and asked the senior mineralogist, Cobb, to identify the energy signature. Cobb ran three tests. The first two measured mineral-decay patterns -- the standard diagnostics for Brightsource ore. Both returned null. The filament carried no mineral signature of any kind. The third test measured ambient vitality -- a diagnostic originally designed to detect contamination from organic material in the mine shafts. The result was not null. The filament carried a strong vitality trace: the residual signature of living energy, metabolic in origin, concentrated and extracted.

Cobb looked at Thessaly. "This filament was never connected to the Brightsource," he said. "Whatever powered it was alive."

Thessaly took the filament and the assay results and locked them in her office. She did not speak to Cobb about it again that day.

**E1 (prior belief established -- years earlier).** Thessaly had been fourteen years old when the old guild-master, Aldren Ferris, had taken her class of apprentices down the Long Stair to see the Brightsource. They had descended for two hours, past the residential levels, past the market warrens, past the water cisterns, until the air turned warm and the walls began to glow.

Aldren had stopped them at a sealed gate and pointed through the grating. Beyond it, the tunnel opened into a vast natural chamber. The walls were veined with luminous ore -- pale gold, faintly pulsing, warm to look at. The Brightsource. The guild's founding texts described it as a gift from the earth to the city: an inexhaustible vein of luminous mineral that the Lamplighters harvested, refined, and channelled through the filaments of every lantern in the underground city. The city existed because the Brightsource existed. The guild existed to tend the connection between the two.

"This is what we serve," Aldren had said to the apprentices. "Not the council, not the merchants, not the district governors. The light. The light comes from here, and we carry it to every street and stairwell and home in this city. When you are Lamplighters, you will maintain that chain. The Brightsource provides. We deliver."

Thessaly had stood at the gate and felt the warmth on her face and believed every word. She had believed it for thirty years since.

**E2 (belief reinforced -- recent).** The guild hall's council chamber, five months before the lantern failure. Thessaly had presented the annual Brightsource report to the city council. The report was a formality -- the same document, with updated figures, that every guild-master had presented for centuries. Ore extraction rates, filament production volumes, lantern maintenance statistics, and the Brightsource reserve estimate.

The reserve estimate was the number Thessaly cared about most. The founding texts described the Brightsource as "inexhaustible," but every guild-master knew that was aspirational language, not geological fact. The guild's mineralogists estimated remaining reserves based on extraction rates and vein density measurements taken at the sealed gate -- the same gate Thessaly had visited as an apprentice. The current estimate was four hundred years of supply at current extraction rates.

The council accepted the report without questions. Thessaly returned to the guild hall satisfied that the city's light was secure for generations. She filed the report in the guild's archive and moved on to the quarter's maintenance schedule.

**E3 (ambiguous signal -- recent).** The guild hall's map room, three months before the lantern failure. Thessaly had commissioned a geological survey of the lower tunnels -- not because she suspected a problem, but because the city council wanted to expand the residential levels and needed to know which tunnel sections were structurally sound. The survey team was independent, hired from the Miners' Compact, and had no connection to the Lamplighters' guild.

The survey team's preliminary report arrived by courier. Thessaly read it at her desk. Most of it was structural data -- load-bearing assessments, water-table depths, ventilation calculations. But one section, near the end, noted an anomaly. The survey team had attempted to map the mineral density of the rock strata below the city's lowest level. Their instruments had detected no luminous ore in the geological column. None. The strata that should have contained the Brightsource vein -- the same strata visible through the sealed gate -- showed mineral profiles consistent with ordinary limestone and basite. No luminous compounds. No extraction-grade ore.

The survey team had flagged this as a calibration error. Their instruments were designed for structural geology, not mineralogy, and they noted that the absence of luminous ore from their readings likely reflected a sensitivity limitation rather than an actual absence.

Thessaly read the note, accepted the calibration explanation, and filed the survey. She did not visit the sealed gate to check. The Brightsource had been there for her entire career. The survey team's instruments were not designed for this purpose. The explanation was reasonable.

**E5 (confirming evidence -- the day after the lantern failure).** The Long Stair, the morning after Cobb's assay. Thessaly descended alone. She had not been below the residential levels in four years -- guild-masters delegated the lower inspections to senior lamplighters. She carried an inspection glass, a mineral probe, and a lantern of her own.

She reached the sealed gate after ninety minutes of descent. She held her lantern up to the grating and looked through.

The chamber beyond was dark. The walls that had glowed pale gold when she was fourteen were black. Not dimmed -- black. She pressed the mineral probe against the rock on her side of the gate. The probe returned the same reading the geological survey had returned three months earlier: limestone and basalt. No luminous ore. No Brightsource.

She tried to open the gate. It was locked with a guild seal she did not recognise -- not the current guild seal, not the previous generation's seal. She had never been through this gate. No guild-master in living memory had been through this gate. The apprentice tour stopped here, at the grating, and the glow visible through it had always been sufficient to confirm what the founding texts described.

But the glow was gone. The ore was gone. And the filament from the Briar Street lantern had never carried a mineral signature. Thessaly stood at the dark gate and understood that whatever had been powering the city's lanterns for the past -- she did not know how long -- it had not been the Brightsource.

She ascended the Long Stair and went to the guild's founding archive.

**E6 (belief revision).** The founding archive, that afternoon. The founding ledger of the Lamplighters' Guild had been kept in a locked case since the guild's first century. Thessaly had read excerpts during her training -- the passages that described the Brightsource, the founding charter, the guild's obligations. She had never read the ledger in full. No guild-master had, as far as she knew. The excerpts were considered sufficient.

She opened the ledger and read from the beginning. The first forty pages matched what she had been taught. The Brightsource, the founding, the extraction process, the filament technology. Then, on the forty-third page, the entries changed. The handwriting changed -- a different guild-master, two centuries after the founding. The entry was dated and formal:

> *The Brightsource vein is exhausted. Final extraction completed on the eleventh day of the Frost Moon. Reserve stocks sufficient for nine months of city illumination at current output. The inner council has resolved that the city will not be told. The renewal chamber beneath the guild hall is to be expanded and its complement increased from forty to sixty. The arrangement with the debtor courts will continue. Indentured workers assigned to the renewal chamber are to be sealed within upon entry. Their life-force, channelled through the guild's filament network, will replace the mineral source. The process is proven -- it has supplemented the Brightsource for the past thirty years as the vein weakened. It will now serve as the sole source. No guild-master below the rank of inner councillor is to be informed. The sealed gate is to remain sealed. The glow visible through the grating will be maintained by a ward-illusion renewed annually.*

Thessaly read the entry three times. She turned the pages. The entries continued for two centuries -- records of the renewal chamber's operation, the number of indentured workers sealed within, the life-force extraction rates, the maintenance of the ward-illusion at the gate. The ledger was meticulous. The inner council had kept perfect records of every person they had sealed in that chamber. The most recent entry was eleven months old.

She closed the ledger. The city's lanterns were burning on stolen life. They had been burning on stolen life for two hundred years. The Brightsource was a memory maintained by an illusion at a gate that no one was permitted to pass. And every guild-master who had presented the annual reserve estimate to the city council -- including Thessaly herself, five months earlier -- had presented a number that measured nothing, drawn from a vein that no longer existed, while beneath the guild hall, people burned.

She locked the ledger in her belt case, left the archive, and walked to the office of the city's chief magistrate. She did not return to the guild hall.

## Di-arc-Fa2-4

**5 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A temple healer in a river-delta kingdom believes that the annual blessing ceremony performed by the high priestess purifies the kingdom's water supply through divine intervention. A drought that disrupts the ceremony's timing, the healer's own clinical observations during the gap, and a conversation with an old irrigation engineer force her to accept that the ceremony's timing coincides with a natural seasonal shift in the river's flow pattern, and that the "purification" is a hydrological event the temple has claimed as divine for generations.

> A = Yara, temple healer; B = the high priestess's annual blessing ceremony purifies the kingdom's water through divine power, and without the ceremony the water would sicken the people; E = clinical observations showing water quality improves on the same schedule during a year the ceremony is delayed, and an irrigation engineer's explanation of the seasonal flow-shift that naturally flushes mineral contamination from the river delta; B' = the water quality improvement is a predictable hydrological event caused by seasonal flow changes, and the temple's ceremony is timed to coincide with a natural process the priesthood has attributed to divine action.

**E1 (prior belief established).** The temple infirmary stands on the eastern bank of the Selu River, where the water runs slow and brown before the blessing. Yara sits at her workbench grinding willow bark and watching the line of patients stretching through the doorway into the courtyard. It is the fifth week before the blessing ceremony -- the sickness season, when the river water carries the grey-mineral taint that cramps the stomach and clouds the eyes.

Every year the same pattern holds. The grey-mineral sickness begins six weeks before the blessing. It worsens through the fifth and fourth weeks. Then the high priestess performs the ceremony on the appointed day, and within three days the water clears, the sickness fades, and the infirmary empties. Yara has served as temple healer for nine years. She has watched this cycle nine times. The blessing works. She treats the sick, and then the goddess, channelled through the high priestess, heals the water.

A young apprentice asks Yara why they bother treating patients when the blessing will cure the water in five weeks anyway. Yara tells her that the blessing heals the source but the body must still recover from the damage already done. "We treat the wound," Yara says. "The goddess prevents the next one."

She believes this. She has built her practice around it.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The temple courtyard, two days after the blessing ceremony. Yara walks the infirmary and counts: four patients remaining. Two days ago there were thirty-one. The water in the testing vessel on her windowsill is clear -- no grey sediment, no mineral smell. She dips a cloth in it, tastes, and confirms what nine years of observation have confirmed before: the water is clean.

The high priestess visits the infirmary that afternoon, as she does every year after the blessing. She asks Yara for the patient count. Yara gives it: thirty-one reduced to four in two days.

"The goddess is generous," the high priestess says.

Yara nods. The timing is exact. The correlation is perfect. She has never had reason to question it, and she does not question it now.

**E3 (partial evidence encountered).** The following year. A drought strikes the river delta in the third month -- the worst in forty years. The river's flow drops to a third of its normal volume. The high priestess announces that the blessing ceremony must be delayed by four weeks: the ritual requires the river to be at a minimum depth, and the drought has dropped it below the threshold. The ceremony will be held when the rains return.

Yara prepares for the worst. If the blessing is delayed by four weeks, the sickness season will extend by four weeks. She requisitions additional willow bark, persuades the temple stores to release extra grain for broth, and converts the outer courtyard into an overflow ward.

The sickness season begins on schedule. The grey-mineral taint appears in the water. Patients arrive. The infirmary fills. Everything proceeds as Yara expects.

Then, in the fifth week -- the week the blessing ceremony would normally occur -- the water begins to clear. No ceremony has been performed. The high priestess is still waiting for the rains. The ritual has not happened. But Yara holds the testing vessel to the light and sees: the grey sediment is settling. The mineral smell is fading. By the sixth week, the water is clean.

The patients begin to recover. Not all at once, and not as fast as in a normal year -- the drought has complicated things. But the trajectory is the same. The infirmary begins to empty on the same schedule it always empties, ceremony or no ceremony.

Yara notes this in her treatment log. She writes: "Water cleared in week five. Ceremony not yet performed. Cause unclear. Possible residual divine protection from last year's blessing." She does not fully trust this explanation, but she does not yet have a better one.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The irrigation canal at the eastern edge of the city, three weeks later. Yara has come to check the water at the canal head -- the point where the Selu's flow enters the city's distribution network. She brings her testing vessel and her treatment log.

An old man sits on the canal wall mending a sluice gate. Yara recognises him: Osen, a retired irrigation engineer who served the kingdom's water authority for decades before the temple absorbed the water authority's functions. He is one of the few people alive who remembers when the water system was managed by engineers rather than priests.

Yara asks him about the drought. Osen is glad to talk. He explains the river's annual cycle in terms Yara has never heard before. The grey-mineral taint, he says, comes from a sediment layer upstream where the river passes through a mineral-rich gorge. During the dry months, the river's flow slows and the mineral concentration builds. Then, in the fifth or sixth week of the wet season, the upstream tributaries swell with snowmelt from the mountains and flush the mineral load downstream and out through the delta's southern channels. The purification is mechanical -- increased volume dilutes and displaces the contaminated water. It happens every year, on roughly the same schedule, regardless of anything that occurs downstream.

Yara asks about the drought year -- the year the ceremony was delayed but the water cleared anyway. Osen nods. He says the snowmelt came on schedule even during the drought, because the mountains received normal precipitation. The delta was dry, but the upstream flush still occurred. The mineral load was still displaced. The water still cleared.

"The ceremony is always performed in the fifth week," Yara says. "The water always clears within days."

"The ceremony is performed in the fifth week because that is when the water clears," Osen says. "Not the other way around. The priests timed the ritual to the flush. My grandmother told me the same thing. She was an engineer too."

Yara sits on the canal wall beside him for a long time. She does not argue. She holds the testing vessel up to the light and watches the clear water move.

**E5 (belief revision -- consequences).** The temple infirmary, that evening. Yara sits at her workbench with her treatment log open. She pages back through nine years of entries. Every year, the same notation: "Water cleared following the blessing ceremony." She reads it nine times. Nine years of attributing a hydrological event to a ritual that happened to coincide with it.

She turns to a fresh page and writes a new entry. She records Osen's explanation of the upstream flush, the snowmelt cycle, and the mechanical process by which the mineral load clears the delta. She records the drought year's evidence: the water cleared without the ceremony, on the same schedule, driven by the same upstream process. She writes: "The ceremony does not purify the water. The water purifies itself. The ceremony marks the timing of a natural event and claims it."

She closes the log. The blessing ceremony is scheduled for the following week -- the rains have returned and the river has risen to the required depth. The high priestess will stand at the river's edge and perform the rite, and the water will be clean, and the people will believe the goddess has answered. Yara will stand in the infirmary and know that the upstream tributaries have done the work that the goddess is credited for.

She considers telling the high priestess what she now understands. She decides against it -- not from cowardice, but from a clinical calculation. The ceremony brings the sick to the temple. The temple is where Yara can treat them. If the people stop believing the ceremony heals the water, they may stop coming to the temple when they are ill. The belief is wrong, but the infrastructure it sustains is real.

Yara closes her treatment log, locks it in her desk, and begins preparing for the ceremony she no longer believes in but intends to attend.
