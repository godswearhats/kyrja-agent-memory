# Discovery arc x Fantasy -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## Di-arc-Fa1-1

**5 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A temple archivist in the Order of the Pale Flame has spent twenty years cataloguing the Order's founding texts and believes the Order was established to protect a sacred fire entrusted to humans by the god Solren. Through a sequence of encounters -- a mistranslated passage, a pre-founding clay tablet, and testimony from a dying elder -- she comes to understand that the Order was founded to contain and suppress the fire, which the original founders considered a curse, not a gift.

> A = Sister Elody (temple archivist); B = "The sacred fire was a gift from Solren, and the Order was founded to protect and tend it"; E = a variant manuscript with a different verb root, a pre-founding clay tablet with containment wards, and the deathbed account of the Elder Lector; B' = "The sacred fire was considered a divine affliction by the founders, and the Order was established to contain it and prevent its spread -- the 'tending' rituals are suppression rites, not devotional ones."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The archive vault beneath the Temple of the Pale Flame, a winter morning. Sister Elody sat at her copying desk with the Order's founding codex open before her -- the Luminara, the text she had spent two decades transcribing, annotating, and teaching to novices. She knew its passages the way a river knows its bed.

The novice beside her was copying the dedication verse, the words every member of the Order spoke at initiation: *Solren gave the fire to the hands of the faithful, and the faithful built the temple to hold what was given.* The novice's hand was unsteady, and Elody corrected her grip on the stylus.

"The fire is the centre of everything we are," Elody said. She said it without emphasis, the way one states that water runs downhill. It was the foundational premise of her life's work. The Luminara said Solren had entrusted the fire to the first priests. The Order existed to tend it. Every ritual, every liturgical calendar, every architectural choice in the temple's design followed from that premise.

She had never questioned it. The text was clear. Twenty years of scholarship had only deepened her certainty.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** The following month, the chapter hall. The Elder Lector -- the Order's senior scholar, ninety years old and failing -- presided over the annual recitation of the founding verses. Elody sat in the front row with her annotated copy of the Luminara.

The Elder Lector read the dedication verse aloud. His voice was thin but his pronunciation was precise. Elody followed along in her text and found no errors. When he reached the passage describing the construction of the first temple -- *they built the hearth deep, and the walls thick, that the fire might endure* -- she nodded. Deep hearths and thick walls. Protection against the elements. A structure built to preserve something precious.

After the recitation, the Elder Lector caught her arm in the corridor. His grip was weak but insistent.

"Elody," he said. "Come to my study before the spring. There is something in the deep archive I should have shown you years ago."

She asked him what it was. He shook his head and released her arm and walked away leaning on his staff. She assumed it was another variant manuscript -- the deep archive held dozens of them, most fragmentary, most repetitive. She made a note to visit him after the frost broke and thought no more of it.

**E3 (partial evidence).** Early spring. Elody was in the archive vault preparing a comparative edition of the Luminara when she found a variant folio she had not previously catalogued. It had been mis-shelved behind a later commentary text. The parchment was older than the standard Luminara by at least a century -- she could tell by the ink composition and the letter forms.

She laid it beside her working copy and began comparing. Most of the text was identical. But in the dedication verse, a single word differed. Where the standard text read *tenere* -- to hold, to tend -- the variant read *teneri* -- to be held, to be restrained. The difference was a single letter, an inflection that changed the verb from active to passive, from care to containment.

*Solren gave the fire to the hands of the faithful, and the faithful built the temple to restrain what was given.*

She stared at the variant. It could be a copyist's error -- a slip of one letter across centuries of transcription. It could be a dialectal variation with no semantic weight. She had seen hundreds of such discrepancies. She catalogued the variant in her notes, recorded the shelf position and the approximate date of the parchment, and set it aside.

But the word stayed with her. *Teneri.* Restrained. She found herself reading the architecture passage differently: *they built the hearth deep, and the walls thick.* Deep hearths and thick walls. Not to preserve the fire against the elements. To prevent the fire from escaping the hearth.

She did not change her position. She noted the ambiguity and continued her work. But the text she had known for twenty years no longer read the same way.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Three weeks later. Elody went to the deep archive -- the locked vault beneath the vault, accessible only to the archivist and the Elder Lector. She had remembered his invitation. She also wanted to look for earlier witnesses to the variant reading.

The deep archive was cold and dry and held materials the Order had collected but never catalogued for general use. Most of it was pre-founding: clay tablets, wax seals, fragments of correspondence between the regional temples that had existed before the Order consolidated them.

She found the Elder Lector's study empty -- he had taken to his bed a week earlier and was not expected to rise again. She searched the shelves herself. On the third shelf, behind a stack of property deeds, she found a clay tablet she had never seen. It was large -- the size of two open hands -- and inscribed in the angular script of the pre-founding temples.

She could read the script. It took her an hour, working with a lamp and a reference sheet, but she could read it. The tablet was a construction order for the first temple's hearth chamber. It specified the depth of the hearth, the thickness of the walls, the composition of the mortar. And it specified a ring of containment wards -- glyphs of binding, suppression, and isolation -- to be inscribed on the interior walls of the chamber, facing inward. Facing the fire.

Wards of protection face outward. They guard against external threats. These wards faced inward. They were built to keep something in.

She set the tablet on the desk and sat in the Elder Lector's chair and read the construction order three more times. The hearth had not been built to nurture the fire. It had been built as a cage.

**E5 (belief revision).** The Elder Lector's bedchamber, the same evening. He was propped against the headboard, his breathing shallow. Elody sat beside the bed with the clay tablet and the variant folio.

She told him what she had found. She told him the variant reading and the containment wards. She asked him whether the fire was a gift or a curse.

The Elder Lector closed his eyes. He was quiet for so long she thought he had fallen asleep. Then he spoke.

He said the founders had not used either word. He said the texts he had read in the deep archive -- texts he had kept from the general catalogue, texts she had not yet found -- described the fire as an affliction. A divine sickness Solren had placed in the earth and the founders had been charged to contain. The rituals the Order performed each day -- the banking of the coals, the sealing of the vents, the ash-gathering at dawn -- were not devotional acts. They were suppression rites. The Order had been tending a prison, not a shrine.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she asked.

"Because you loved the fire," he said. "And I was not certain the founders were right."

She sat with him until he slept. Then she carried the tablet and the folio back to the archive vault and began the work of re-reading everything she had written in twenty years.

## Di-arc-Fa1-2

**4 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A guild master of the Weavers' Compact in a prosperous trade city believes the Compact's monopoly on enchanted cloth is maintained by the superior skill of its artisans. When a disgraced journeyman brings her a rival city's textile sample and a stolen formula, she is forced to accept that the Compact's advantage depends on a rare mineral additive controlled by a single mine -- and that the mine's supply is nearly exhausted.

> A = Guild Master Thera Vane; B = "The Weavers' Compact produces the finest enchanted cloth because our artisans are more skilled than any rival guild's"; E = a rival city's cloth sample that matches Compact quality, and a stolen formula showing the enchantment depends on a mineral additive, not weaving technique; B' = "The Compact's enchanted cloth is not a product of superior skill -- it depends on ashite, a mineral from a single mine, which any guild could use if they had access. The mine's reserves are failing, and when they are gone, the Compact has no advantage."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The Compact's great hall, a feast day. Guild Master Thera Vane stood at the high table and raised a bolt of shimmerweave above her head. The cloth caught the candlelight and bent it, throwing pale blue arcs across the vaulted ceiling.

"Sixty years of mastery," she said to the assembled guild. "Three generations of weavers, each better than the last. No other city's guild can produce this. Not Tannemark. Not Delvain. Not the southern cooperatives. They have tried. They have failed. Because the art is in the hands, not the loom."

The hall cheered. The apprentices in the back row stamped their feet. Thera set the bolt on the table and let the applause run its course. She believed every word. She had trained under the previous guild master for twelve years, and the previous guild master had trained under the one before. The Compact's techniques were proprietary, encoded in apprenticeship, passed hand to hand. Thera had supervised the training of four hundred weavers and watched each one struggle for years before their cloth held an enchantment. Skill was the bottleneck. Skill was the moat.

**E2 (partial evidence).** Five months later, Thera's private workshop behind the guild hall. A journeyman named Cadell stood in front of her desk. He had been expelled from the Compact two years earlier for selling unfinished cloth outside the guild's licencing system. He smelled of road dust and looked like he had not slept.

"I was in Tannemark," he said. He placed a folded cloth on her desk. "Their guild is weaving this."

Thera unfolded it. The cloth was pale grey, finely woven, and when she tilted it toward the lamp, it shimmered. Not the deep blue of the Compact's shimmerweave, but an unmistakable enchantment -- the light clung to the fibres and moved with the fold. She held it to her nose. The scent was the same mineral tang she knew from her own dyeing vats.

"This is good work," she said. She did not say it was as good as the Compact's. It was not, quite. But it was close enough that a buyer in a market stall would not see the difference.

"They have been selling it for four months," Cadell said. "Under their own guild mark. Priced fifteen percent below ours."

Thera set the cloth down. "Tannemark has never produced enchanted cloth. Their weavers are competent, but they have no one with the training."

"I know," Cadell said. "That's what I thought. So I went to their workshop."

She told him to sit down. He sat.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The same room, moments later. Cadell took a folded paper from his coat and laid it on the desk beside the Tannemark cloth.

"I paid a dyer's apprentice in Tannemark for this," he said. "It's the formula for their enchantment bath."

Thera opened the paper. The formula was written in a dyer's shorthand she could read. It listed the standard mordants and fixatives she used herself -- alum, iron water, tannin extract. And at the bottom, in a different hand, a single line: *ashiteite powder, forty grains per vat, dissolved in spirit of wine before the first immersion.*

Thera looked at the line for a long time.

"They're using ashite," she said.

"So are we," Cadell said. "I asked the old dyers before I left the Compact. Every shimmerweave vat has ashite in it. The guild has been buying it from the Greyhollow mine for as long as anyone can remember. But the masters don't teach it as the source of the enchantment. They teach it as a mordant. A fixative. The technique is supposed to be in the weave pattern and the hand tension."

Thera picked up the Tannemark cloth again. She examined the weave. It was a standard tabby -- nothing proprietary, nothing that required years of training. The enchantment was in the fibres, not the structure. Whatever the ashite did to the cloth happened in the vat, not on the loom.

"If anyone can buy ashite and follow this formula," she said slowly, "then the weave technique is not the source of the enchantment."

"No," Cadell said. "It isn't."

**E4 (belief revision).** The following morning, Thera's workshop. She had sent Cadell away the previous evening and spent the night in the workshop running tests. She had woven two identical cloths on the same loom, using the same pattern, the same hand tension, the same thread. She dyed one in the standard enchantment vat. She dyed the other in a vat prepared identically but without the ashite additive.

The cloth with ashite shimmered. The cloth without it was inert. Plain linen. No enchantment. No light. Nothing.

She held the two cloths side by side in the morning light. Twenty-eight years of training. The hand tension she had practised until her fingers cramped. The weave patterns she had memorised in her first year and refined in her twentieth. None of it mattered without forty grains of powite grey powder dissolved in spirit of wine.

She folded both cloths and locked them in her desk. Then she sat down and wrote a letter to the Compact's factor at the Greyhollow mine, asking two questions: how much ashite remained in the mine's reserves, and whether the mine had recently sold to any buyers outside the Compact's contract.

The factor's reply arrived by courier three days later. The mine had sold ashite to four new buyers in the past year, including Tannemark's guild. And the reserves -- the factor's language was careful, but the meaning was not -- were sufficient for approximately six more years of extraction at current rates, after which the known deposits would be exhausted.

Thera read the letter twice. Then she summoned the Compact's council of masters and told them to bring their apprenticeship training manuals. She had changes to make, and not much time to make them.

## Di-arc-Fa1-3

**6 beats, chronological, present tense**

**Scenario.** A young war-captain of the mountain clans believes the lowland kingdom's army is massing on the border to invade the clans' highland territory, as it has done twice in living memory. Through intercepted messages, a parley, and a captured supply manifest, he comes to understand that the army is not preparing for invasion but for evacuation -- the lowland capital is flooding, and the kingdom is seeking high ground for its civilian population.

> A = War-Captain Brennan; B = "The lowland army massing at the Greymarch Pass is an invasion force preparing to seize highland territory"; E = an intercepted courier's message, a parley exchange, and a captured supply manifest; B' = "The lowland army at Greymarch Pass is an evacuation escort -- the River Tain has broken its banks, the lowland capital is flooding, and the kingdom is seeking highland ground for its displaced civilians, not military conquest."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The war-council fire, the Redthorn clan's camp above Greymarch Pass. Brennan stands before the assembled captains with a scout's report in his hand.

"Six thousand infantry," he says. "Four hundred cavalry. Supply wagons in column behind them. They crossed the River Tain at Whiteford Bridge three days ago and are moving north along the valley road. At their current pace, they reach the pass in five days."

The elder captain, Maren, spits into the fire. "Same approach as the last two invasions. Whiteford Bridge, the valley road, Greymarch Pass. They do not vary."

Brennan nods. His father fought in the second highland war. His grandfather died in the first. The lowland kingdom invades through Greymarch because it is the only pass wide enough for supply wagons. The pattern has not changed in fifty years.

"We hold the pass," Brennan says. "I want the forward positions fortified by tomorrow evening. Archers on the ridgeline. Cavalry reserve behind the second defile. We have the ground. They have the numbers. Same as always."

The captains begin assigning units. No one questions the assessment. The lowlands have massed an army and pointed it at the only viable invasion route. The conclusion is self-evident.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Two days later, the forward observation post above the pass. Brennan lies flat on the ridge with a scout beside him, watching the lowland column through the morning haze.

The column is larger than the initial report suggested. The wagons stretch further than Brennan can see. The infantry marches in standard formation -- shield-bearers on the flanks, pikemen in the centre. He counts regimental banners: eight regiments, at least. The cavalry screens the column's flanks in paired patrols.

"Eight regiments," the scout says. "They brought more than last time."

Brennan watches the column for an hour. He notes the spacing, the pace, the order of march. It is a textbook approach -- the same deployment doctrine the lowland kingdom has used for every highland campaign. He returns to camp and orders the final fortifications completed by nightfall.

**E3 (partial evidence).** The following morning. A patrol captures a lowland courier riding ahead of the main column. The courier carries a sealed message pouch. Brennan opens it in his tent.

Most of the messages are administrative: supply inventories, march schedules, unit assignments. One letter, addressed to a lowland colonel, catches his attention. It is from the kingdom's quartermaster general. It reads, in part: *Ensure the civilian column is separated from the military escort by no less than half a mile at all times. The refugees must not be visible from the highland observation posts until the parley is established.*

Brennan reads the sentence again. *Civilian column. Refugees.*

He calls for Maren. Maren reads the letter, frowns, and hands it back.

"A ruse," Maren says. "They used false dispatches in the second war. Planted them on couriers they expected us to capture."

Brennan considers this. Maren's explanation is plausible. The lowland kingdom has used deception before. He files the letter and does not change his dispositions. But he sends a second scout party south along the ridge to look for the civilian column the letter describes.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The next day. The scout party returns at midday. They have not seen a civilian column. But they have seen something else. Behind the last line of wagons in the lowland column, moving slowly on the valley road, are carts Brennan's scouts have never seen in a military supply train. Livestock carts with penned animals. Flat-bed wagons loaded with household goods -- bedframes, grain barrels, stacked crates of crockery. A wagon fitted with canvas shelters in which the scouts counted women, children, and elderly civilians.

Brennan stands at the map table in his tent and listens to the report. Maren is beside him.

"Armies do not bring bedframes," the lead scout says. "Or milking goats."

Brennan asks how many civilian wagons. The scout estimates eighty to a hundred, intermixed with the military supply train rather than separated as the quartermaster's letter had instructed. He says the soldiers on the flanks were not screening for highland attack -- they were herding the civilian wagons back into line when they drifted.

Brennan looks at Maren. Maren does not say "ruse" this time. He says nothing.

**E5 (secondary confirming evidence).** That evening, the pass. Brennan orders a flag of parley raised on the ridgeline. An hour before dusk, a lowland officer rides up the switchback road under a white pennant. Brennan meets him at the first defile with two guards.

The officer is young and exhausted. He introduces himself as a lieutenant of the third regiment. He says he has been sent by his general to request passage through the pass for the civilian population of the lowland city of Tamberlin.

Brennan asks why.

The lieutenant says the River Tain broke its banks eleven days ago. The flood walls at Tamberlin failed on the second night. The lower city is under four feet of water and rising. The granaries are destroyed. The wells are contaminated. The crown has ordered the evacuation of thirty thousand civilians to higher ground, and the highlands north of Greymarch Pass are the nearest elevated terrain with fresh water and defensible shelter.

Brennan asks why the kingdom sent six thousand soldiers if the purpose is evacuation.

The lieutenant says the army is the escort. The roads between Tamberlin and the pass cross open country with no defensible positions. The civilian column is slow, vulnerable, and carrying everything its people own. The army is there to protect the column from bandits and to maintain order during the march. He says the general anticipated that the highland clans would read the approach as military and requests a meeting to negotiate passage terms.

Brennan tells the lieutenant he will send his answer by morning. The lieutenant rides back down the switchback.

**E6 (belief revision).** The war-council fire, late that night. Brennan stands before the captains with the quartermaster's letter, the scout report, and the lieutenant's account.

"It is not an invasion," he says. "The lowland capital is flooding. They are bringing their civilians to our highlands because they have nowhere else to go."

Maren speaks first. "And when the flood recedes? Thirty thousand lowlanders on our land, with six thousand soldiers to keep them there?"

"The lieutenant says the army withdraws once the civilians are settled."

"The lieutenant says what his general told him to say."

Brennan places the captured supply manifest on the ground beside the fire -- the document from the courier's pouch he has been re-reading since the parley. He points to the line items. Grain for four weeks. Medical supplies for a field hospital. Seed stock. Seed stock -- not rations, not siege materials, but barley and oat and turnip seed. An army preparing for conquest carries weapons and food. An army escorting refugees carries seed to plant when they arrive.

"No invasion force in history has packed seed stock," Brennan says. "These people are preparing to survive, not to fight."

The fire crackles. The captains are silent.

"I am not opening the pass," Brennan says. "Not yet. But I am changing our disposition from defence to negotiation. If they are refugees, we set terms -- duration, boundaries, resource sharing, the army's withdrawal schedule. If they violate the terms, we hold the pass and they do not get through. But I will not fortify against an enemy that does not exist and leave thirty thousand people on the valley road with winter coming."

He orders the forward archers to stand down and sends a runner to the lowland general with an offer to meet at the first defile at dawn.

## Di-arc-Fa1-4

**5 beats, non-linear (depicted: E2 -> E3 -> E1 -> E4 -> E5; chronological: E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E4 -> E5), embedded document + free indirect**

**Scenario.** A court physician in a river kingdom believes a plague spreading through the eastern provinces is carried by miasma -- foul air rising from the marshlands -- as the kingdom's medical tradition teaches. Through a pattern in the outbreak data, a failed quarantine, and a canal-worker's testimony, she comes to accept that the plague follows the water supply, not the air, and that the kingdom's canal system is the vector.

> A = Court Physician Anneth; B = "The eastern plague spreads through miasma rising from the marshlands; the disease is airborne and proximity to swamp air is the primary risk"; E = outbreak maps showing cases along canals far from marshland, a failed air-quarantine in a sealed district, and a canal-worker's account of upstream contamination; B' = "The plague spreads through contaminated water, not air -- the canal system distributes the disease from a contaminated upstream source, and the marshland correlation exists because marshland settlements draw canal water, not because they breathe marsh air."

**E2 (partial evidence).** The provincial administrator's office in Delvenne, six weeks into the plague. Anneth has been travelling the eastern provinces for a month, mapping the outbreak at the crown's request. She unrolls her chart on the administrator's desk -- a map of the eastern provinces with each reported case marked in red ink.

The administrator looks at the map. The red marks cluster along the marshland border, as expected. But Anneth has been staring at this map for three weeks, and what troubles her is not the cluster but the outliers. Fourteen cases in Highcross, a hill town thirty miles from the nearest marsh, sitting on dry limestone with no swamp air within a day's ride. Nine cases in Barden, a ridgetop village with constant wind. Six cases in the canal workers' barracks at Thorngate, upstream of the marshes entirely.

She has circled these outliers in blue ink. She draws a line connecting them. The line does not follow the marshland. It follows the Great Eastern Canal.

The administrator asks what the line means. Anneth says she does not know yet. The miasma theory predicts that cases should cluster near stagnant water and low-lying ground where foul air accumulates. The outlier cases are at elevation, in moving air, with no marsh exposure. They should not exist.

She rolls the map and takes it with her.

**E3 (belief reinforced then broken -- embedded document).** Two weeks later. Anneth has ordered an air-quarantine of the canal district in Merrowgate, a town of eight thousand on the canal's southern branch. The quarantine follows standard miasma protocol: seal all windows facing the marshland, burn aromatic herbs in the streets, and station smoke-pots at the district's eastern boundary to purify the incoming air. The district has been sealed for twelve days.

She receives the district physician's report by courier. She reads it in her quarters at the provincial inn:

> *To Court Physician Anneth, with respect -- the quarantine of the canal district is in its twelfth day. The air measures have been followed to the letter. All eastern windows remain sealed. The smoke-pots have burned continuously. No marsh air has entered the district since the quarantine began. Despite this, I must report eleven new cases in the sealed district since the quarantine was imposed, including three in households on the district's western edge, the furthest point from the marshland. The new cases do not correlate with proximity to the sealed boundary. They correlate, as nearly as I can determine, with proximity to the canal-water cisterns from which the district draws its drinking supply. I do not know what to make of this. I await your instruction.*

Anneth reads the report twice. She sets it on the table and looks at her map. The sealed district's air has been clean for twelve days. The disease has continued to spread inside the seal. The miasma protocol has failed. The district physician has noted a correlation with the cisterns. The cisterns draw from the canal.

She marks the eleven new cases on her map. They cluster around the three cisterns that supply the district. The households furthest from the cisterns have no cases. The households closest to them have the most.

**E1 (prior belief established -- retroactive, embedded document).** Anneth's journal, the entry dated six weeks earlier, the night before she departed the capital for the eastern provinces:

> *The crown has ordered me east to map the plague and recommend containment. The reports describe a pattern consistent with marsh miasma: low-lying settlements, stagnant water, humid air, cases concentrated in the evening and night hours when miasma pools in the valleys. The medical faculty's position is clear and I share it -- the disease is airborne, carried in the corrupt exhalations of the marshland, and the appropriate response is air-quarantine, smoke purification, and distance from the source. I expect to confirm this in the field and to recommend a cordon around the marshland border. I do not expect complications.*

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Two days after receiving the district physician's report. Anneth rides to the canal workers' barracks at Thorngate, where six cases were reported among men who work on the canal's upper reaches, far above the marshes. The barracks sit on a hill overlooking the canal junction where the northern and southern branches meet.

She asks to speak with the foreman. He takes her to the canal bank and shows her the junction. The northern branch carries clean mountain water. The southern branch passes through the marshland settlements before reaching the junction. At the junction, the two branches merge. Downstream from the junction, every town and village draws its drinking water from the mixed canal.

The foreman tells her something she has not known. Three months ago, before the plague began, a section of the southern branch collapsed near the village of Ashenmere. The collapse dammed the canal for two weeks. During those two weeks, the stagnant water behind the dam flooded the village's burial ground -- the ground where plague victims from a decade-old outbreak had been interred in shallow graves. When the dam was cleared and the water released, it carried whatever had been in that ground downstream through the southern branch, into the junction, and into every canal-fed cistern in the eastern provinces.

Anneth stands at the canal junction and watches the two branches merge. The clean northern water and the southern water that has passed through a breached burial ground, mixed together, flowing into the drinking supply of every town on her map. She takes out the map and looks at it again. Every red mark sits on a canal-fed settlement. Every blue-circled outlier -- the hill towns, the ridgetop villages, the upstream barracks -- draws water from the canal, not from wells or springs.

The plague does not follow the air. It follows the water.

**E5 (belief revision).** The provincial administrator's office in Delvenne, the following day. Anneth unrolls the map on the same desk where she first showed it six weeks earlier. She has added the cistern data, the canal route, the Ashenmere burial ground, and the junction at Thorngate.

She tells the administrator that the miasma protocol must be abandoned immediately. The sealed windows and smoke-pots are useless -- the disease is not in the air. It is in the canal water. Every settlement drawing from the canal system is at risk, regardless of elevation or proximity to the marshes. The marshland correlation exists because marshland settlements use canal water, not because they breathe marsh air.

She recommends three immediate actions: close all canal-fed cisterns and switch to well water or rain collection; dispatch engineers to the Ashenmere collapse site to determine whether the breach can be sealed; and send riders to every canal-side settlement with instructions to boil all water before drinking.

The administrator asks whether she is certain. She says the sealed district in Merrowgate is the proof. Twelve days of perfect air-quarantine and the disease continued to spread, clustering around the cisterns. She says the canal-worker testimony and the burial-ground breach provide the mechanism. She says the miasma theory cannot explain the hill-town cases, the sealed-district failures, or the cistern correlation. The water theory explains all of them.

The administrator authorises the cistern closures and the riders. Anneth drafts the orders herself, signs them with her court seal, and hands them to the couriers before the hour is out.
