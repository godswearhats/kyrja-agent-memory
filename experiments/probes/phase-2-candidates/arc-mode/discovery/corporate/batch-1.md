# Discovery arc x Corporate -- Batch 1 (Tier A)

## Di-arc-Co1-1

**5 beats, chronological, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A chief revenue officer at a mid-size SaaS company believes the firm's largest enterprise client renewed its three-year contract because of the platform's technical superiority. Over a series of internal meetings and a client site visit, she encounters evidence that the renewal was driven entirely by the client's switching costs and internal procurement inertia -- and that the client's engineering team has already built a parallel integration with a competitor's API.

> A = Mara Voss (chief revenue officer); B = "Zenith Labs renewed because our platform is technically superior to all evaluated alternatives"; E = client-side integration logs, internal Zenith communications, and a direct admission from Zenith's VP of Engineering; B' = "Zenith renewed because migrating away would have required eighteen months of re-engineering they could not justify to their board -- they are already building the competitor integration in parallel and will leave at the next renewal window."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The executive conference room, a Monday morning in March. Mara stood at the head of the table with the Q1 revenue slide behind her. The Zenith Labs renewal -- three years, forty-two million dollars -- was the centrepiece.

"They evaluated four competitors," she said. "Full bake-off. Technical review, security audit, performance benchmarks. They chose us. Not because of price -- Conduit underbid us by eleven percent. Not because of relationship -- half their engineering team turned over since the last contract. They chose us because the platform is better."

The CFO nodded. The CEO wrote something in his notebook. The head of product smiled.

"I want to use this as the case study for the board deck," Mara said. "Proof that technical investment drives retention. This is the story."

Nobody in the room disagreed.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Two weeks later, a quarterly business review with the Zenith account team, the seventh-floor conference room. Mara dialled in from an airport lounge. The account manager walked through the usage metrics: API call volume up fourteen percent quarter over quarter, three new internal teams onboarded at Zenith, support tickets down.

"They're deeper in than they've ever been," the account manager said. "More seats, more integrations, more dependency."

Mara asked about the competitive landscape. The account manager said Zenith's procurement office had gone quiet after the renewal -- no RFIs, no vendor meetings, no signals of dissatisfaction. He said the renewal had settled the question.

"Good," Mara said. "Keep the QBR cadence. I don't want any surprises before the board meeting."

She hung up and opened her laptop to draft the board narrative. The Zenith renewal was the lead paragraph.

**E3 (partial evidence).** Six weeks later, a Wednesday afternoon. Mara's executive assistant forwarded an email that had been sent to the wrong distribution list -- a message from a solutions engineer on the Zenith account to the head of integrations, intended for an internal technical thread. The engineer wrote that Zenith's platform team had asked for documentation on the data export API, specifically the schema for full-history extraction. He noted that the request was unusual: Zenith had never needed bulk export before, and the data formats they specified were not compatible with the company's own analytics tools. They were compatible with Conduit's ingestion pipeline.

Mara read the email twice. She forwarded it to the account manager and asked him to find out what Zenith was building. He replied within the hour: Zenith said it was a routine data-governance exercise. They were centralising their vendor data into a neutral format for compliance purposes.

Mara accepted the explanation. She flagged the email in her files but did not mention it to the CEO.

**E4 (contradicting evidence encountered).** A month later, a client site visit at Zenith's Chicago office. Mara had come to present the annual product roadmap to Zenith's executive team -- a courtesy she extended only to the top five accounts. The meeting went well. Zenith's CTO asked sharp questions about the roadmap and seemed engaged.

Afterward, walking to the lift, Mara passed a glass-walled conference room on the fourth floor. Inside, six engineers sat around a table with two large monitors. On the left monitor she could see a Conduit dashboard -- the competitor's interface, unmistakable, with its green-and-grey palette and the Conduit logo in the upper corner. On the right monitor was a terminal window showing what looked like a data migration script. One of the engineers had a printed spreadsheet open in front of him with two columns: the company's API endpoints on the left, Conduit's on the right, with mapping annotations in red ink.

She stopped. She looked at the room for three seconds. Then she kept walking.

In the lift, she opened her phone and texted the account manager: "Who is Zenith's technical contact at Conduit? Find out today. Do not ask Zenith."

**E5 (belief revision).** The following Monday, Mara's office. The account manager sat across from her with a folder. He had reached a former colleague now working at Conduit's enterprise sales team. The colleague had confirmed, off the record, that Zenith had signed a pilot agreement with Conduit four months ago -- two months before the renewal with Mara's company had closed. The pilot covered the same use cases, the same data sets, the same integration points. Conduit's sales team had been told by Zenith's VP of Engineering that the three-year renewal was a "bridge contract" -- Zenith's procurement team had determined that migrating mid-cycle would cost more in engineering hours than renewing and running both platforms in parallel while the migration completed.

Mara closed the folder. She sat with it for a long time.

"Pull the board deck," she said. "The Zenith narrative comes out. All of it."

"What do we replace it with?" the account manager asked.

"The retention number is real. The reason isn't. We kept them because leaving is expensive, not because we're better. If I put the wrong story in front of the board, we'll invest in the wrong things for three years and lose them anyway." She opened her laptop and deleted the draft paragraph. "Set up a meeting with product. I need to know which accounts are staying for the same reason Zenith is, and which ones are staying because they want to."

## Di-arc-Co1-2

**4 beats, chronological, free indirect style**

**Scenario.** A managing director at a management consultancy believes his firm was selected for a major transformation engagement because of the quality of their proposal and team. A sequence of encounters -- a partner's offhand remark, a procurement document obtained through a colleague -- forces him to accept that the client selected his firm because the client's CEO and the consultancy's senior partner are college friends, and the evaluation process was structured to produce a predetermined outcome.

> A = David Hahn (managing director); B = "The client selected our firm through a competitive evaluation because our proposal was the strongest"; E = a partner's remark about pre-engagement conversations, a procurement scoring document showing retroactively adjusted weights, and the client CEO's own admission; B' = "The selection was arranged between the client's CEO and our senior partner before the RFP was issued; the evaluation was structured to justify a decision already made."

**E1 (prior belief established).** David had led the proposal team for nine weeks, and the nine weeks had nearly killed them. Fourteen-hour days in the war room on the thirty-second floor, the whiteboards covered in process maps and staffing models, the three dry runs before the final presentation. When the client's procurement office called to say they had won, David's first response was to close his office door and sit quietly for five minutes. His second was to send a note to his team: they had earned it.

He believed this completely. The RFP had been rigorous -- a hundred and forty pages of requirements, four competing firms, two rounds of presentations, a technical deep-dive that had lasted six hours. His team had fielded every question. They had proposed a staffing model the client hadn't considered, a phased approach that reduced the first-year cost by twenty percent, and a governance structure that mapped to the client's internal org chart. He had watched the procurement panel's faces during the final presentation and seen them writing.

The senior partner, Richard Colvin, had congratulated him in the hallway the following morning. "Outstanding work," Colvin said. "The proposal made the difference." David thanked him and meant it.

**E2 (partial evidence).** Three weeks later, a Friday evening at the firm's summer reception on the roof terrace. David was standing near the bar when he heard Colvin's voice behind a planter, speaking to another partner.

"-- had dinner with Martin before the RFP went out," Colvin was saying. "He told me what they needed. I told him we could staff it."

The other partner said something David couldn't hear.

"No, the evaluation was real," Colvin said. "Martin ran it properly. But he knew what he wanted before it started."

David set his glass on the railing. He stood there for a moment, then walked to the other side of the terrace. Martin was Martin Geis, the client's CEO. David had met him twice during the engagement -- a polished man with a firm handshake who had deferred to his procurement team on all evaluation questions.

David told himself the conversation could mean many things. Clients often spoke to preferred vendors before issuing an RFP. It was normal. It did not mean the evaluation was compromised. Colvin had said the evaluation was real. David returned to the bar and ordered another drink and did not think about it again that evening.

He thought about it the next morning, and the morning after that.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** Two months into the engagement. David was at the client's office, working from a hot desk on the ninth floor, when a message arrived from a junior consultant on his team. She had been asked by the client's procurement coordinator to help archive the evaluation files. In the process, she had found the scoring matrix -- the document that recorded how each firm had been rated on each criterion.

She sent it to David because one detail confused her. The weighting of the criteria had been changed between the first and second rounds of evaluation. In the first round, technical capability had been weighted at forty percent and team experience at twenty-five percent. In the second round -- after the presentations -- the weights had been reversed: team experience at forty percent, technical capability at twenty-five percent. The adjustment had been made by the procurement director and approved by Martin Geis personally.

David opened the matrix and ran the numbers both ways. Under the original weighting, a competitor -- Harwick & Laine -- had scored highest. Under the revised weighting, David's firm won by six points. The swing came entirely from the team experience category, where Colvin's thirty-year relationship with the client's industry segment had been scored as a differentiator.

David closed the file. He sat at the hot desk and looked at the grey carpet and the fluorescent lights and the procurement coordinator walking past with a box of binders, and he understood that the nine weeks in the war room had not decided anything.

**E4 (belief revision -- consequences).** The following week, David's office, a Tuesday morning. He had requested a meeting with Colvin. He did not bring the scoring matrix. He did not need to.

"Richard," he said. "Was the Geis engagement wired?"

Colvin looked at him for a long moment. He did not deny it. He said that Martin Geis had approached him eight months before the RFP, that they had discussed the engagement scope over two dinners, and that Geis had structured the evaluation to satisfy his board's procurement requirements while ensuring the outcome. He said the proposal still had to be excellent -- Martin would not have been able to justify a weak proposal regardless of the weighting -- and that David's team had delivered exactly what was needed.

"Your work was real," Colvin said. "The decision wasn't."

David nodded. He asked Colvin whether the client's board knew the evaluation had been managed. Colvin said they did not. David asked whether this was how the firm typically won its largest engagements. Colvin said it was not typical, but it was not unusual.

David left the office. He walked to the lift, rode it to the lobby, and stood outside the building for ten minutes in the cold. He was not angry at Colvin. He was recalibrating. The model he had used to understand his own career -- that rigorous proposals won competitive evaluations, that quality was the variable that mattered -- did not describe the system he was operating in. The variable that mattered was the relationship between Colvin and Geis. His proposal had been a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. He had been building the engine while someone else was picking the destination.

He went back upstairs and began drafting a proposal policy memo. If the firm was going to compete on relationships, the proposal teams needed to know it before they spent nine weeks in a war room. He did not send it that day. He sent it the following Monday, addressed to Colvin and the managing partner, with the scoring matrix attached.

## Di-arc-Co1-3

**6 beats, non-linear (depicted: E3 -> E1 -> E2 -> E4 -> E5 -> E6; chronological: E1 -> E2 -> E3 -> E4 -> E5 -> E6), embedded document + present tense**

**Scenario.** A director of quality assurance at a pharmaceutical company believes the firm's new drug passed its Phase III trial on the strength of its clinical efficacy. Through a regulatory audit, she encounters evidence that the trial's statistical significance depended on a mid-trial protocol amendment that changed the primary endpoint -- and that the original endpoint showed no significant benefit over placebo.

> A = Dr. Renata Soares (director of quality assurance); B = "Verantis passed Phase III because the drug is clinically effective against the primary endpoint as designed"; E = the original statistical analysis plan, the mid-trial protocol amendment, and the re-analysis under original parameters; B' = "Verantis passed Phase III because the primary endpoint was changed mid-trial to one the drug could meet; under the original endpoint, the drug showed no statistically significant benefit over placebo."

**E3 (audit scene -- present tense).** Dr. Soares sits in the regulatory affairs conference room with the FDA's pre-approval inspection file open on her laptop. The inspection is routine -- a standard pre-NDA review of the Phase III documentation for Verantis, the company's lead compound. She has been through six of these. She expects this one to go the same way.

The inspector's checklist asks for the original statistical analysis plan, dated before patient enrolment began. Dr. Soares opens the trial master file and locates it: SAP version 1.0, dated fourteen months before the first patient was dosed. She has read the final clinical study report dozens of times, but she has never compared it line by line against the original SAP. She does so now because the inspector's checklist requires it.

The original SAP defines the primary endpoint as the mean change from baseline in the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale at week twelve. The final study report defines the primary endpoint as the proportion of patients achieving a fifty-percent reduction in HDRS score at week eight. They are not the same endpoint. They are not the same timepoint. They are not the same statistical test.

She searches the trial master file for the protocol amendment that authorised the change. She finds it: Amendment 3, dated nine months after enrolment began, approved by the sponsor's medical director and the independent data monitoring committee. The amendment's rationale section states that the original endpoint was "insufficiently sensitive to clinically meaningful treatment effects" and that the revised endpoint "better captures the drug's mechanism of action."

Dr. Soares reads the amendment's rationale three times. Then she opens a blank document and begins writing questions for the clinical team.

**E1 (prior belief established -- embedded document, retrospective).** From Dr. Soares's notebook, the entry dated six months earlier, the morning after the Phase III topline results were announced:

> *Verantis Phase III met primary endpoint. p = 0.003. Clinical team presented at the all-hands this afternoon -- clean data, strong effect size, separation from placebo clear by week six. The drug works. NDA submission timeline moves up to Q3. I asked Dr. Pham whether there were any protocol deviations that would complicate the filing. He said there were none of significance. I have no reason to question this. The trial was well-run.*

She had written this with confidence. She had attended the all-hands presentation, reviewed the topline summary, and seen the survival curves projected on the auditorium screen. The p-value was emphatic. She had filed the note and moved on to the next item on her queue.

**E2 (belief reinforced).** Three months after the topline results, the company's annual R&D day. The auditorium on the fourth floor. The chief medical officer presents the Verantis data to the full research organisation -- two hundred scientists, clinicians, and regulatory staff. He walks through the study design, the patient demographics, the efficacy results, the safety profile.

He does not mention a protocol amendment. The primary endpoint on his slides matches the final study report. The original endpoint does not appear in the presentation. No one in the audience asks about it.

Dr. Soares sits in the third row and takes notes. She writes: *Strong programme. CMO confident in the data. No flags.* She underlines *no flags* and closes her notebook.

**E4 (secondary evidence).** Two days after the audit scene. Dr. Soares has sent her questions to the clinical team. Dr. Pham, the clinical lead, responds by email. He attaches the data monitoring committee's minutes from the meeting where Amendment 3 was approved. The minutes record that the committee reviewed an interim futility analysis showing that the drug was unlikely to reach statistical significance on the original primary endpoint at the planned sample size. The committee recommended either increasing the sample size by forty percent or revising the endpoint. The sponsor chose to revise the endpoint.

Dr. Soares opens the original SAP again. She runs the numbers herself, using the raw data tables from the clinical database. Under the original endpoint -- mean change in HDRS at week twelve -- the treatment effect is 1.4 points, with a p-value of 0.23. Not significant. Not close to significant. Under the revised endpoint -- fifty-percent responder rate at week eight -- the p-value is 0.003.

She prints both analyses and places them side by side on her desk. The drug that passed its trial on the left page fails it on the right.

**E5 (belief revision).** The same office, an hour later. Dr. Soares closes her door. She sits with the two printouts and the protocol amendment and the data monitoring committee minutes and does not move for a long time.

The drug did not pass because it was clinically effective against the endpoint the trial was designed to test. The drug passed because the endpoint was changed, mid-trial, to one the drug could meet. The amendment was procedurally valid -- the DMC approved it, the regulatory filing disclosed it, the FDA would see it in the inspection. Nothing was hidden. But the story she had told herself about why the drug worked -- the story the CMO had told at R&D day, the story the topline announcement had implied -- was not the story the data told. The original hypothesis failed. A new hypothesis was substituted. The new hypothesis succeeded. These are not the same thing.

She opens her laptop and begins drafting a quality finding for the regulatory file. She notes the endpoint change, the futility analysis, the original p-value, and the discrepancy between the CMO's R&D day presentation and the trial documentation. She flags it for discussion with the chief quality officer before the NDA submission.

**E6 (consequences).** The chief quality officer's office, the following morning. Dr. Soares presents the finding. She lays out the timeline: original SAP, futility signal, Amendment 3, revised endpoint, positive result. She shows both analyses.

The CQO reads the printouts. He asks whether the amendment was disclosed in the regulatory filing. Dr. Soares says it was. He asks whether the FDA's inspection team has flagged it. She says they have not yet reached that section of the review but will within the week. He asks what she recommends.

She says the NDA should include a sensitivity analysis showing the drug's performance under the original endpoint, presented alongside the amended results, with a clear narrative explaining the mid-trial change and its rationale. She says the current filing buries the amendment in an appendix and presents only the revised endpoint in the efficacy summary. She says an inspector who reads carefully will find the discrepancy, and a filing that appears to minimise it will raise more questions than a filing that addresses it directly.

The CQO is quiet for a moment. Then he picks up the phone and asks his assistant to schedule a meeting with the CMO and the head of regulatory affairs for that afternoon. He tells Dr. Soares to prepare a briefing document. She returns to her office and begins writing.

## Di-arc-Co1-4

**4 beats, chronological, present tense, dialogue-driven**

**Scenario.** A newly promoted head of data science at a fintech company believes the firm's fraud-detection model is performing well because the accuracy metric reported in the monthly dashboard is 99.2 percent. Through a conversation with an analyst and a direct examination of the model's predictions, she comes to understand that the high accuracy is an artefact of class imbalance -- the model is predicting "not fraud" for nearly every transaction, and the actual fraud-capture rate is below twelve percent.

> A = Priya Chandran (head of data science); B = "Our fraud model is performing well -- 99.2% accuracy means it is catching fraud effectively"; E = an analyst's question about false negatives, a direct audit of the model's predictions on known fraud cases, and the confusion matrix; B' = "The 99.2% accuracy is meaningless -- the model is predicting 'not fraud' almost universally, and it is missing 88% of actual fraudulent transactions. We do not have a working fraud-detection system."

**E1 (prior belief established).** The data science bullpen, a Thursday morning. Priya is two weeks into the role. She stands at the wall-mounted display reviewing the monthly model-performance dashboard with her team.

"Fraud model," she says, tapping the tile. "99.2 percent accuracy. That's stable from last month."

Her lead engineer nods. "It's been above 99 since we deployed in January. Rock solid."

Priya moves to the next tile. She has nine models to review and a leadership sync at eleven. The fraud model is green. She does not open the detail view. She moves on.

**E2 (partial evidence).** Three weeks later, a one-on-one with Jun Reyes, a junior analyst who joined the team before Priya did. Jun's office, a converted phone booth with barely enough room for two chairs.

"I have a question about the fraud model," Jun says. He pulls up a spreadsheet on his laptop. "Operations flagged forty-seven transactions last month as confirmed fraud -- chargebacks, account takeovers, the ones we know for certain were fraudulent after the fact."

Priya nods. "Okay."

"I ran those forty-seven through the model's prediction log. The model flagged five of them as fraud. The other forty-two were predicted as legitimate."

Priya frowns. "That can't be right. The model is at 99.2."

"I know," Jun says. "I checked it three times. The forty-two missed cases are all in the prediction log as 'not fraud.' The model saw them and scored them clean."

Priya asks him to send her the spreadsheet. She tells him not to share it with anyone else until she has reviewed it. She walks back to her desk thinking about what 99.2 percent accuracy means when the model misses forty-two out of forty-seven known fraud cases.

She does not yet have the answer. But the number on the dashboard has stopped meaning what she thought it meant.

**E3 (contradicting evidence encountered).** The following afternoon, Priya's desk. She has pulled the full confusion matrix for the fraud model -- the table she should have opened on her first day and did not.

The numbers are plain. In the previous month, the model evaluated 1.2 million transactions. Of those, 1,194,400 were legitimate and 5,600 were fraudulent. The model predicted "not fraud" for 1,193,100 transactions and "fraud" for 6,900. Of the 6,900 flagged as fraud, 660 were actual fraud. Of the 5,600 actual fraudulent transactions, the model caught 660 and missed 4,940.

The accuracy is 99.2 percent because the model is correct about the 1.19 million legitimate transactions. It is wrong about 88 percent of the fraudulent ones. The number on the dashboard measures the model's ability to identify non-fraud. It does not measure the model's ability to identify fraud.

Priya stares at the matrix. She pulls the model's feature weights. The top-weighted feature is transaction amount, and the threshold is set so high that only transactions above twelve thousand dollars trigger a fraud flag. The median fraudulent transaction in the data is four hundred and twenty dollars. The model is not detecting fraud. It is detecting large transactions.

She prints the confusion matrix and the feature-weight table. She closes the dashboard.

**E4 (belief revision).** Monday morning, the leadership sync. The CTO's office, eight people around the table. Priya is the last item on the agenda.

"I need to report a finding on the fraud model," she says. She places the confusion matrix on the table. "The model's accuracy is 99.2 percent. Its fraud-capture rate is 11.8 percent. It is missing almost nine out of ten fraudulent transactions."

The CTO picks up the printout. "The dashboard says 99.2."

"The dashboard reports accuracy. Accuracy is the wrong metric for a fraud model when 99.5 percent of transactions are legitimate. A model that predicts 'not fraud' for every single transaction -- no logic at all, just a default answer -- would score 99.5 percent accuracy. Our model is performing worse than that default on the dimension that matters."

The room is quiet.

"How long has it been like this?" the CTO asks.

"Since deployment. The metric was never the right one. The model has never had a meaningful fraud-capture rate. We have been reporting a green dashboard for five months while missing almost all actual fraud."

The CTO sets the printout down. "What do you need?"

"Two things. First, the dashboard metric changes today -- precision and recall on the fraud class, not aggregate accuracy. Second, I need approval to pull the model from production and replace it with a rules-based holdover while we rebuild. The current model is not providing fraud protection. It is providing the appearance of fraud protection."

The CTO looks at the CFO. The CFO asks what the exposure is -- how much fraud has passed through in five months. Priya says she will have the number by end of day but estimates it is between eight and fourteen million dollars. The CTO approves both requests. Priya collects the printout and leaves the room.
